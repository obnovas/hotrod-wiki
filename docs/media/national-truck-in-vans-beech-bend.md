# Two Nationals in One Summer: The Vanners, the National Truck-In and Beech Bend (Bowling Green, Kentucky, 1973–2017)

*A magazine's idea, a club's party, a Kentucky park that filled up, and a fight over who owned the word "National"*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium (the early meets are told by a vanner-historian who read the old van magazines and newsletters, by a Bowling Green TV station's look back, by *Hot Rod*'s own 1976 report and by a 2017 feature with the people who were there; they disagree on the first meet's size and on which 1976 meet was "the Nationals") · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> two histories by Greg "Coop" Cooper on the National Truck-in Board's site (c. 1994 and 2021), with vanners' comments; WNKY News 40, Bowling Green (2022), drawing on *Van Happenings*; *Hot Rod* (October 1976); *Autoweek* (2017) · **Last verified:** 2026-11-21 (Round 46) · **Open issues:** ⚠ whose idea the national meet was; ⚠ how many vans came to the first Truck-In; ⚠ which 1976 meet was the fourth National Truck-In, and how big the Colorado one was · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

## A national meet for a work truck

The custom van had a national meet before it had a national organization, and the meet belonged to whichever club would host it. The fight over that idea came to a head at Beech Bend Park in Bowling Green, Kentucky. In 1976 the vanners held two Nationals, and the clubs kept the name.

It started at *Hot Rod*. In 1973 the magazine had run several stories on what Greg Cooper, a longtime vanner who later wrote the hobby's history, called "the New Trend in California at Customizing the lowly delivery van."[^gc94] Editor Terry Cook told *Autoweek* he noticed that an issue sold better when an earlier editor had put a small photo of a custom van on the cover. "A kid didn't have money for a motorhome, but he did ... for a van," Cook said.[^aw] Cooper's history credits the idea of a national van meet to staff writer Steve Green; Cook, in 2017, told it as his own thought.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^gc21][^aw] Cook wrote an editorial asking for a club to host one, and promised the magazine would publicize it.[^gc94][^aw] Nobody is sure where "truck-in" came from. Most say "keep on truckin'," and Cook added that it sounded like a love-in.[^aw]

Rocky Mountain Vans of Denver answered. The first National Truck-In was held at Tiger Run, a resort near Breckenridge, Colorado, in August 1973.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^gc94][^aw] Cooper's history describes "Some games, a show, a band but basically a big open field full of 600 + vans."[^gc94] The second, in 1974, went to Bonner Springs, Kansas, hosted by Vans Am of Kansas City, and drew about twice as many.[^gc94][^aw] That one was sanctioned by a new group, the National Street Van Association, based in Chicago and run by Fred Blumenthal. *Hot Rod* came to treat the NSVA as the voice of the vanners.[^gc94][^gc21]

## More vans than Beech Bend could hold

For 1975 the word came down that the third Nationals would be at Beech Bend Park, hosted by Midwest Vans of Mattoon, Illinois. Two NSVA leaders, Ray Gebhart and Blumenthal, belonged to that club. River City Truckin Co. of Memphis and Ohio Street Vans had also been in the running.[^gc94] It was held in July.[^gc21] The price went to $10 and $15, which Cooper figured was "a 250 percent increase in a little less than 2 years." The NSVA expected 2,500 to 3,000 vans. More than 4,500 came.[^gc94]

The park was not ready for them. The campsites the vanners had been promised were mostly taken by the park's permanent campers, and the dash plaques handed out were cheap plastic. For a lot of people, Cooper wrote, it was "their first bad experience at a National van-in."[^gc94] Bowling Green's WNKY, looking back in 2022, said the crowd was "way more than the facility and city were had prepared for."[^wnky] Joe Madonia was 16 that year, and it was his first Nationals. He came in a caravan of 250 vans, CB radios going.[^aw]

Then the NSVA announced that 1976 would be at Beech Bend again. Rocky Mountain Vans, which had wanted the Nationals back in Colorado, thought the meet should travel to a new host and a new place every year. It did not like what Cooper called "the blatant commercialism" that was coming in. It sent an open letter to the van magazines, the NSVA answered, and the feud went public.[^gc94] Then Rocky Mountain Vans went after the trademark. Because it had hosted the first Nationals, it argued, it had the better claim to the name, and it was awarded the rights to "National Truck-in."[^gc21]

## Two Nationals

So in the summer of 1976 there were two. The NSVA held what *Hot Rod* called its "4th Annual Truck-In" at Beech Bend: more than 6,300 trucks and 32,200 people over four days. A BFGoodrich tractor-trailer parked in the middle of the pits was the center of it, bands played every night, and BFG and *Hot Rod* rolled out their Future Shock van. *Hot Rod* counted vans from all 50 states, more than 1,000 from Canada and a group from the United Kingdom, and called it "the biggest automotive event ever held, anywhere."[^hr76] Bob Carter and his wife came from the Tulsa Truckers in their Hiway Star, a 289 V8 with a four-speed, a homemade shifter, a color TV and a homebuilt water cooler. "The Carters have made all four Nationals," the magazine noted.[^hr76] *Van Happenings*, the NSVA's paper, reported a Van Pull, a 100 Yard Dash, Tug of War, King of the Slide and Skate Board Kahana.[^wnky]

Meanwhile Rocky Mountain Vans held the National Truck-In in Colorado. Cooper counted 6,318 vans in Kentucky and 3,200 in Colorado. *Autoweek* put the Colorado meet in Colorado Springs, with 3,500.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^gc94][^aw] Plenty of vanners did not know there was a feud at all.[^gc94]

The two faced off again in 1977 and 1978. Rocky Mountain Vans and a group called the Two Percenters sued the NSVA over the name. The Two Percenters took theirs from Blumenthal's claim that 98 percent of vanners were not the "wild" bunch Rocky Mountain Vans was supposed to stand for. Most vanners went to both meets anyway.[^aw] Cook remembered the difference between coasts. In California, he said, "the guys were all busy polishing their vans." In the Midwest they were "covering port-a-potties with Christmas tree lights."[^aw] In September 1978 the NSVA quit.[^gc94] Within three years nearly all of the fifteen or so van magazines were gone, and the club newsletter became the way vanners found each other.[^gc94] "I know people who never went to another Truck-In after the NSVA folded," Cooper told *Autoweek*. "They just sold their vans."[^aw]

The name stayed with the clubs. Rocky Mountain Vans ran the Nationals alone for three years, then in 1981 named a board of regional representatives to help pick hosts.[^gc94] The board had nine members, each from a region of the country.[^gc21] In the early 1990s Rocky Mountain Vans handed host selection to the National Truck-in Board, which then incorporated.[^gc21] Cooper's history from about 1994 says the board "is now the owner of the Trademark."[^gc94] Some years nobody bids. In 2013 there were no offers, and the board hosted the meet itself.[^gc21] The 45th National Truck-In, in 2017, was in Mansfield, Ohio, organized by a club called Vans on the Run, with Cooper as its president. Madonia had been to 42 in a row by then, and he owned the Museum of Vanning in Hudson, Florida. "Now it's about family," he said.[^aw]

The crowds at Beech Bend are why the meet never became one organization's show. It still goes to whichever club will put it on.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Whose idea was a national van meet?** Cooper's 2021 history says it "was first proposed by Steve Green, staff writer for Hot Rod Magazine," and that editor Terry Cook then wrote the editorial; WNKY repeats that, citing the board.[^gc21][^wnky] In *Autoweek* (2017) Cook tells it in the first person: "There should be a national van event -- a national truck-in, if you will."[^aw] Both could be true, a writer's suggestion and an editor's column, but neither source mentions the other's man. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **How many vans came to the first Truck-In?** Cooper's history, written around 1994 from old magazines and newsletters, says "600 +," and gives the first two meets as "600 and 1500 vans."[^gc94] *Autoweek* (2017) says "More than 1,000 vans descended on Tiger Run on Aug. 10, 1973," and 1,400 to 1,500 for Bonner Springs.[^aw] A commenter on Cooper's page who went to the second meet remembered "about two thousand vans and pickup trucks."[^gc94] *Hot Rod*'s own reports of 1973 and 1974 would settle the counts. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Which 1976 meet was the fourth National Truck-In, and how big was Colorado's?** *Hot Rod* (October 1976) called Beech Bend "NSVA's 4th Annual Truck-In," and WNKY (2022) counts Bowling Green as the National Truck-In's "years three and four."[^hr76][^wnky] Cooper's 2021 history says Rocky Mountain Vans, once it won the trademark, "would host the Fourth National Truck-in," and his earlier one says "The National truck-in was in Colorado in 76," with 3,200 vans to the NSVA's 6,318.[^gc21][^gc94] *Autoweek* says "In 1976, there were two National Truck-Ins," places the Rocky Mountain Vans meet in Colorado Springs and gives 3,500.[^aw] Cooper names no town. Both sides have a case for the word "Nationals"; the trademark went to Rocky Mountain Vans. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
