# "As If They Were Long-Lost Relatives": The First Hot Rod Power Tour and Goodland, Kansas (1995–1998)

*An editor's idea to drive instead of fly, paper maps on desks, two police cruisers on an off-ramp, Kansas barbecue, a parts store opened at 10 p.m., and a town that stopped work to watch*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** High (for the 1995 and 1998 visits, told in *Hot Rod*'s own tour reports written at the time; the first tour's numbers are Medium, since the magazine's later recollections differ) · **Source types:** *Hot Rod* magazine (March, September and October 1995; October 1996; September 1998), *Hot Rod*'s anniversary pieces (2014, 2019), a participant's 1997 online diary (Selectric Typewriter Museum), a BangShift forum history (2018), the college's own history · **Last verified:** 2026-11-28 (Round 53) · **Open issues:** ⚠ how many came the first time; ◐ why Mingo became Goodland · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "This wasn't the biggest show, but it was by far the friendliest, starting with a welcoming police escort through town."

    — Rob Kinnan, *Hot Rod*, October 1995, looking back at the first Power Tour's stop in Goodland, Kansas.[^hr95oct]

## Drive, don't fly

The Hot Rod Power Tour began as a magazine promotion, and the stop its editors remembered best was a Kansas vo-tech school that fed them and kept its shop open past midnight. In late 1994 *Hot Rod* editor Drew Hardin and his staff, Gray Baskerville, Will Handzel, Rob Kinnan, Cole Quinnell and Jeff Smith, were told to drum up interest in the magazine's new Power Festival series.[^tellem19] Rather than fly to the festival at Norwalk Raceway in Ohio, why not drive there?[^tellem19] "That's when it came to me," Quinnell wrote later: "let's literally drive people to the event." The staff would drive their own cars from Los Angeles and invite readers to join them for the whole drive, at a dinner stop, or just to wave from an overpass.[^cq14] The name put "Power" from the festival together with "Tour" from the Victory Tour road trips in a sister magazine.[^tellem19]

There was no Google Maps. They spread paper maps on their desks and added up the mileage segment by segment.[^cq14] Hardin's editorial in the March 1995 issue put out the call: "The trip should be a blast, and we want a bunch of you to join us." The plan was to overnight in Cedar City, Utah; Glenwood Springs, Colorado; Mingo, Kansas; Kansas City; Springfield, Illinois; the Chicago area; and Detroit, then roll into Norwalk together.[^ed95]

They had no idea whether anyone would come. On May 13, 1995, at the Petersen Automotive Museum, the kickoff drew 200 cars, by *Hot Rod*'s 2019 telling.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>[^tellem19] Quinnell remembered eight readers committed to the whole tour and a couple dozen more cars for the first leg.[^cq14]

## Two cruisers on the off-ramp

The Monday-night stop turned out to be Goodland, not Mingo.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup>[^ed95][^hr95sep] On May 15 the tour ate lunch in Limon, Colorado, and drove into black clouds and radio reports of hail and tornadoes. Then came something "equally scary," the staff wrote: two police cruisers parked on the off-ramp at Goodland. They were there to escort the tour into town. "It seemed as if the whole town came out to greet us." The Northwest Kansas Area Vocational Technical School served Kansas-style barbecue, and then the faculty opened the school's shop. The tour made repairs that ranged from wheel alignment to U-joints. Will Handzel said he had to tighten every nut on his roadster. It had been a 393-mile day.[^hr95sep]

About 50 cars and a couple hundred people were waiting in the school parking lot.[^hr95oct] When the tour was over, Kinnan wrote that all the stops were great, but Goodland was the one that blew them away. The school and the Chamber of Commerce "made all the Tour participants feel as if they were long-lost relatives. They fed us until we couldn't eat anymore." Students and instructors stayed until after midnight lending a hand. Instructor Tom Purvis opened the local parts store at 10 p.m., found U-joints for Jeff Smith's Chevelle driveshaft, and helped put them in. They were there until about midnight, and the cars stayed "under the watchful eyes of students all night."[^hr95oct] The tour went on through Kansas City, Springfield and Merrillville, Indiana, to Detroit, where the cruise night at Continental Lanes drew so many cars that the T-shirts, hats and burgers ran out, and finished at Norwalk after 2,858 miles.[^hr95sep]

## Four years running

Goodland stayed on the route.[^bs18] In 1996 the school's auto shop had *Hot Rod*'s coverage of the year before on display.[^hr96] In 1997 one participant, who kept an online diary of each day, noted that the school had a Cushman Truckster: "So do I, so I took pics." Gray Baskerville photographed his car. The hotel said he had cancelled, but it had one room left, "so we didn't have to camp in the car."[^sel97]

By 1998 more than 250 cars drove the whole distance, about twice the year before, and by Mount Clemens, Michigan, the tour was drawing 4,000 cars and 40,000 people a day.[^hr98] Goodland was still the halfway point. People waved from overpasses on the way in from Denver, and Jeff Koch wrote that the police-escorted trek through town "has become an annual event that everyone stops work to witness." Fifth graders from Central Elementary came out to look at the cars. Edelbrock's Vic Edelbrock Senior Scholarship at the school went to Jason Davis. "For the fourth year in a row," the school opened its garages to anyone who needed work done, until warnings of lightning, tornadoes and golf-ball-size hail cut the evening short and the Goodland crowd, "clearly used to such meteorological maladies," left in a most orderly way. At 5 the next morning the tour lined up in front of the Best Western Buffalo Inn for a 444-mile drive to Kansas City.[^hr98]

The school opened in 1964 as the Northwest Kansas Area Vocational Technical School and is Fort Hays Tech | Northwest today.[^fhnw] It grew from an editors' road trip, and the stop they wrote about first was a school shop in Goodland with the lights on past midnight.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **How many came the first time?** *Hot Rod*'s 2019 history says the kickoff at the Petersen had 200 cars and that seven non-staffers became the "Long Haul Gang" for the whole 2,800 miles.[^tellem19] Cole Quinnell, writing in 2014, remembered eight readers committed for the whole tour and "a couple dozen more cars" for the first leg.[^cq14] The tour's own September 1995 report gives local crowds of 50 to 250 cars at the stops but no kickoff count.[^hr95sep] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Why Goodland instead of Mingo?** The March 1995 plan put the Monday overnight in Mingo, Kansas, and by May the stop was Goodland and its vo-tech school.[^ed95][^hr95sep] Nothing we read says who arranged the change. The *Goodland Star-News* of spring 1995 should know. That the school hosted every year from 1995 through 1998 rests on *Hot Rod*'s 1998 "fourth year in a row" and a forum compiler's route list.[^hr98][^bs18] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
