# Where Lucas Shot It: Petaluma, American Graffiti and the Town's Own Car Show (1946–2023)

*One night in San Rafael, six weeks in Petaluma, and a car show that started at the outlet mall because the police said take baby steps*

## One night in San Rafael

*American Graffiti* is set in George Lucas's Modesto ([Modesto](../places/modesto.md)), but it was shot mostly in Petaluma, a town that already had a drive-in, car clubs and teenagers in hot rods of its own. The movie borrowed its streets for one summer. A generation later, Petaluma's car people turned the movie into their own downtown car show, and the money goes back into the town.

Lucas's hometown had changed too much to play itself, so he chose San Rafael, near his production company.[^ac23] The first night of shooting was Monday, June 26, 1972, on a crowded Fourth Street in San Rafael, with Paul Le Mat as the aging hot rodder John Milner and Mackenzie Phillips as Carol, cruising in a yellow 1932 Ford coupe.[^luc22] Lucas had a budget of about $700,000 and had spent weeks finding dozens of early-'60s cars.[^luc22] After that one night, San Rafael withdrew its permission; the *Argus-Courier* says it declined to shut down streets for any more days. The crew found a replacement in Petaluma, farther north in Sonoma County, where the mayor and city council were happy to cooperate.[^luc22][^ac23]

## Six weeks of nights in Petaluma

They shot mostly at night, often in chilly summer weather, for about six weeks.[^luc22] The Petaluma street scenes are the ones people remember. The police car that loses its rear axle to a cable was filmed in the lot between the McNear and Lan-Mart buildings on Petaluma Boulevard.[^ac08a] The girls threw a water balloon into Milner's car on Western Avenue at Petaluma Boulevard.[^ac08b] The drag race on "Paradise Road" was shot on Frates Road.[^ac23] The freshman hop, where Richard Dreyfuss's Curt spots the girl in the white Thunderbird, was in the Petaluma High School gym parking lot. The clapperboard that launched it, dated 8-2-72, came near the end of filming and is now in the town's museum exhibit.[^pm23] At least one local truck made the film: Jim Bergstrom's pickup.[^ac23]

Phillips was 12 when she was cast. "In the scenes with Paul and I in the '32 deuce, because of the cameras, we were locked in the car for long periods," she told the *Argus-Courier* in 2022. "I was like the mascot on the set."[^ac22] The film came out in August 1973 and became one of the most profitable films in history.[^luc22]

## A town that already had the scene

Petaluma didn't need the movie to have a car scene. At Third and I streets, Quinley's drive-in was built in 1946, with carhops bringing food to the car. Hollie Heglin bought it in 1948 and it became Hollie's.[^sl23] Petaluma's car clubs often met there in the 1950s, as a photo the museum showed in 2023 is captioned.[^ac23] Milt Lund, a Minnesota native who had started there as a dishwasher, took it over and renamed it Lund's.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup> It was "a mandatory socializing spot" for teenagers in hot rods, custom cars and their mothers' sedans, and the unofficial headquarters of the Pacers car club. Lund helped sponsor the Pacers' dragster. He wore a white shirt and white bow tie, and he didn't let the kids camp out: "We didn't allow homesteaders or squatters rights," he told the paper in 2007, with a laugh.[^ac19]

Much of what Petaluma knows of its own teen scene survived by luck. Thousands of *Argus-Courier* negatives from the 1950s and early 1960s were saved from destruction by Ed Mannion, a local historian and *Argus* columnist, and many of the photos were taken by his wife, Chris, a part-time photographer at the paper. Museum collection manager Solange Russek spent about ten years scanning them.[^ac23]

## The police said take baby steps

The movie paid the town back in ways nobody planned. When Petaluma later went to Washington for flood-control money, Mayor David Glass found that as soon as he named the town where *American Graffiti* was filmed, he had officials' attention. "Petaluma got the money it needed," the *Argus-Courier* wrote.[^ac23]

The car show came from the car people. Cruisin' the Boulevard was formed in 2005 by a group of classic-car and *American Graffiti* enthusiasts, with John Furrer as founder.[^ac22] The first Salute to American Graffiti car show was in 2006.[^pm23] The first two shows were held at the Factory Outlet Mall, with only the cruise downtown. "This is what we wanted to do for a long time, but the police department said we should try baby steps," board member Rich Poremba said in 2008, the first year the whole show was downtown. "This year we said, 'How about now?'"[^ac08b] More than 240 cars came, the $50 slots sold out in two days, and the money went to automatic defibrillators for local schools and equipment for the fire department.[^ac08b]

That weekend they re-created the cop-car scene at 4:30 in the morning on May 15, 2008, the hour Lucas had shot it, with the "Jerry's Cherries" car-lot front rebuilt in the same lot and Haskell Wexler, the film's visual consultant, watching. Al Mossi, who owned a local garage, drove the police car. On the first try the cable snapped, recoiled over the brick wall at the back of the lot and smashed into a parked car on the other side. On the second try the axle came off just as the car hit the gutter.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^ac08a]

By 2022 the show was capped at 400 cars, the slots filled in 11 days, and 88 more were on a waiting list. "We've had entries from 18 states and eight different countries," Furrer said.[^ac22] The museum's 2023 exhibit counted more than $200,000 the organization had given back to Petaluma in grants.[^pm23]

Lucas came to Petaluma for one summer because the town said yes. A generation later its own car people turned that yes into a car show on the same streets.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did Lund take over Hollie's?** Harlan Osborne's *Argus-Courier* profile (2007, reprinted 2019) says "In the mid-1950s." The Petaluma History Room's 2023 article says the Lunds took over in 1957, when Hollie's owners went into business with the Quinleys elsewhere, and cites *Argus-Courier* ads from April 1957 and 1958.[^ac19][^sl23] The two also spell the earlier owner "Hollie" and "Holly" Heglin. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Which police car in 2008?** Chris Samson's *Argus-Courier* report calls it a 1961 Ford Galaxie; Meloni Courtway's report the day before calls it "the famous Fairlane 500 police car," rebuilt and taken apart again by Hugh Nelson and his crew at All About Classics of Santa Rosa.[^ac08a][^ac08b] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
