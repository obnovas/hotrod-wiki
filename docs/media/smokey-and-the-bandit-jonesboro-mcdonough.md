# Ghost Cars With No VIN: Smokey and the Bandit in Jonesboro, McDonough and Norcross (Georgia, 1976–2017)

*A Hollywood Trans Am built and broken by Georgia hands, and the towns that still claim it*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium (the filming days come from a fan historian's reconstruction from call sheets, scripts, photographs and interviews; the shop and town stories are firsthand memories told decades later) · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> Jeff Reeves's eyewitness column in the *Henry County Times* (McDonough, 2018); Barry Trivette's 2023 interview at his race shop; the banone.net production pages; the City of Jonesboro's 2017 press release; *Road & Track* (2022) · **Last verified:** 2026-11-23 (Round 48) · **Open issues:** ⚠ when McDonough was filmed; ⚠ how many Trans Ams; ◐ the jump and the record · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "I would love to give you one but they are ghost cars. They do not have a VIN."

    — Hal Needham, as Doug Walker remembered him answering Walker's older brother on McDonough's square, September 1976, told to the banone.net historians.[^bots]

## A week in Clayton County

The best-known Trans Am in the movies was, in everything but its badge, a Georgia car. Its first week of filming, which ran into early September 1976, stayed on Clayton and Henry county roads. A stock-car shop in Norcross readied its stunt cars, and McDonough watched one hit the curb on the town square. Forty years later Jonesboro threw it a party.

Principal photography on *Smokey and the Bandit* began in Clayton County on Monday, August 30, 1976, at Mundy's Mill Road and Highway 54 in Jonesboro. The rest of that week stayed close: Highway 54 between Jonesboro and Fayetteville, a house in Jonesboro for Cledus's home, and on Friday the chase that ends with a patrol car in the pond at Mundy's Mill. On Saturday, September 4, the crew filmed the "Coors warehouse" in downtown Jonesboro by day and McDonough's town square by night.[^bo1] The warehouse was really Arrowhead Alternator, dressed with Coors signs and banners, and the scene stood in for Texarkana.[^boco] The Jonesboro news box out front happened to hold the September 3 paper, with a front-page photo taken two days earlier.[^boco]

The pond car was used again. A May 1977 newspaper interview with Needham told how, when the transportation crew said a tow truck was 45 minutes away, he led his stunt men into the pond and they got the car out so a mechanic could dry its wiring.[^bop]

## A Norcross race shop

The stunt cars came from Baird and Trivette Racing, on Medlock Bridge in Norcross. Barry Trivette, whose father ran it, was 16 or 17. "My Dad's former shop 'Baird and Trivette Racing' was put in charge of prepping and helping with all the stunt work," he said in 2023. The shop was on record with the Georgia Film Commission as a stunt prep shop, and Don Schisler, who had raced NASCAR with his father in the 1960s, steered the movie to them.[^bt23]

The first thing the producers said, Trivette remembered, was "We are gonna wreck a lot of police cars." His father sent them to DeKalb County, which was selling off its old police cars, and those became the movie's state troopers. The shop had a couple of the Trans Ams "before the dealerships had even seen them yet," and was told to keep them covered.[^bt23] The movie cars were 1976 Trans Ams that Pontiac had dressed to look like the coming 1977 Special Edition.[^bopic]

The jump car got the most work. It could not reach speed in the distance allowed with its stock Pontiac engine and automatic, so the shop pulled them and put in the 350 small-block Chevy and transmission from a customer's late model stock car that raced on the asphalt at Dixie. They built a roll cage, mounted the seat on hinges with motorcycle shocks behind it "so it don't break the driver's back," and weighed the car to work out how much ballast to put in the trunk so it would not land nose first. "That was one and done," Trivette said. By his account the car cleared the far ramp, and Needham, who held a jump record himself, had it measured; it was just short.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^bt23]

Trivette went to the finale at the Lakewood Fairgrounds in Atlanta, where his father's customers brought their race cars and filmed racing scenes all day. Nearly all of it was cut; the race cars show only in the background. At the Atlanta premiere someone asked anyone who was in the movie to stand, and all the race car drivers stood up. "So I guess until you see the final cut, you don't know if you are int the movie or not," Trivette said.[^bt23]

## Saturday night on the square

McDonough remembered its night. Jeff Reeves, a lifelong resident, watched from the roof of the McDonough Theatre with family and friends; two grips he had befriended, Buddy Colson and Chris Weldon, told him when the shots would come.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup> He saw the Trans Am come up from Macon Street, jump the wall into the square and hide behind the police shack. When Reynolds got out, he wrote, the girls screamed as if the Beatles had taken the stage at Shea Stadium. A truck parked in town held five new black Trans Ams, backups guarded around the clock by McDonough police.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^hct18]

The banone.net historians, drawing on a resident named Doug Walker, add what went wrong. Stunt driver Alan Gibbs came in too fast, slammed the curb broadside, blew out both passenger-side tires and wrecked the suspension. "Doug said it sounded like a bomb went off." The crew towed it away and pulled another car off the hauler, and that take is the one in the movie. Between shots, Walker's older brother told Needham he would sure like one of those Trans Ams when filming was done, and Needham said they were ghost cars, with no VIN.[^bots]

Reeves's friend Billy Ray Bell, a brick mason, got hired to mind Jerry Reed's basset hound when its keeper went home sick, on the day Reed's truck flattened probably 30 motorcycles outside Grady Lamar's club on Macon Street. Every one, Reeves wrote, was wired down to the ground with railroad spikes; they shot it once, and he was standing right there. Bell sold signed photos of himself with the dog, and then a batch with the dog's paw print as a second autograph, until someone from the production said they could not sell the picture with the dog in it.[^hct18]

## The run home

Needham said in 2007 that the movie did not make enough at Radio City Music Hall "to pay the Rockettes," and then "went right through the roof" once it was sent to the thirteen Southern states. After it came out, Pontiac's Trans Am sales went from 46,701 in 1976 to 68,745 in 1977, 93,341 in 1978 and 117,108 in 1979.[^rt22]

In 2007, for the 30th anniversary, Dave Hall of Restore a Muscle Car and David Hershey started the Bandit Run, and the first one drove from Texarkana to Atlanta.[^br] For the 40th, in June 2017, the City of Jonesboro, which said the run was "largely filmed in and around the city of Jonesboro," hosted the official celebration. Its program had the 11th Bandit Run coming in from Texarkana with more than 250 cars, mostly Trans Ams, meeting the Snowman's Run from Tallahassee in Franklin, Georgia, and rolling into town together. It planned a reenactment of the Coors scene downtown, a $10 street barbecue for the runners, and a question-and-answer session with Reynolds and Susie Ewing, Hot Pants Hilliard in the movie, who the city said had not seen each other since the 1977 premiere.[^jb17] The ghost cars never got VINs, but the Georgia towns and the Georgia shop that built and broke them kept their names on the story.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Was McDonough filmed in August or September 1976?** Reeves remembers "a few days in August of 1976" and "several days that summer."[^hct18] The banone.net production timeline, reconstructed from call sheets and scripts, puts the town-square scene on the night of Saturday, September 4, 1976, the end of the first week of filming, which began August 30.[^bo1][^bots] The call sheets are the stronger record for the date; Reeves's column was written 42 years later. The Grady Lamar's scene is in Reeves's column but not yet on the timeline pages we read. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **How many Trans Ams?** Reeves saw "five brand new 1976 black Pontiac Trans Ams" on a truck in McDonough.[^hct18] Trivette said, "I am not positive. I think the show used 6 cars," including a pristine "beauty car" and a camera car "screwed full of camera mounts."[^bt23] The banone.net pages count the cars one by one (a hero car, an insert car, a stunt car, and car No. 2, out after the curb).[^bots] Reeves calls them 1976 cars; banone.net says the movie cars were 1976 Limited Edition Trans Ams that Pontiac modified to look like the 1977 Special Edition, so both may be right about the year.[^bopic] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Did the jump car nearly beat Needham's record?** Only Trivette's 2023 interview says so, and he said he did not see the jump live; he also could not place where it was filmed.[^bt23] The engine swap and the hinged seat are also his memory alone. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
