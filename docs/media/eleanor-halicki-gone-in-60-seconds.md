# Named, by His Brother's Account, for a Neighbor's Mother: Toby Halicki, Eleanor and the Mustangs That Followed (Dunkirk, N.Y., to Yukon, Okla., 1955–2025)

*A towing family's boy, a yellow fastback his brother says was named for a lady back home, a sequel that killed him, and a quarter century of builders and lawyers arguing over a car*

## A towing family in Dunkirk

The most copied movie Mustang began with a hometown name. By his brother's account, Toby Halicki called the yellow fastback in his 1974 film Eleanor, after the mother of his little brother's friend back in Dunkirk, New York. A quarter century later builders were selling Eleanors, the police had raided one of them, and in 2025 a federal appeals court had to decide whether Eleanor was a character at all.

Halicki's Garage in Dunkirk dates to 1919, when the towing was done with horses. John and Angeline Halicki, who had come from Poland and could not read or write, built it and raised 13 children. For a time the customers wrote their own repairs on the bill, to be figured when the children got home from school. The eight Halicki boys rode along on tow calls from about age 7 and could drive long before it was legal. A Dunkirk High principal, the story goes, once thought his car was driving itself when it was brought back from the garage, because the boy at the wheel was too small to see.[^ape1] Ron Halicki said the family business was the training: clearing a wreck on Route 60 meant telling the police and fire departments what to do, and so did filming one.[^ape1]

In 1955, at 15, Toby left for California with $40 and his older cousin Joe Blazejewicz. Joe could not read, so Toby drove the whole way to Gardena without a license.[^ape2] He pumped gas, opened a body shop while still in high school, and took to Southern California's chopped and channeled street rods.[^lat92] By the *Am-Pol Eagle*'s account he owned six houses and a custom auto and body shop by 21.[^ape2]

## The chase, the crowd and the name

He wrote, produced, directed and starred in *Gone in 60 Seconds* and did his own stunts. Its 40-minute chase destroyed 93 cars, 48 of them police cars.[^lat89] It cost $250,000 to make and grossed $16 million its first year, by the *Am-Pol Eagle*'s count.[^ape2] He filled it with Dunkirk: a wedding at the Kosciuszko Club, Dunkirk Ice Cream, Koch's beer, Point Gratiot, and a 1973 Fredonia State sweatshirt worn by Ron.[^ape2] The car was a yellow fastback Mustang, customized to look like a Mach 1,<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^ca08] and it was named Eleanor for Eleanor Korzeniewski, the mother of Ron's boyhood friend Joe Korzeniewski.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^ape2] In the film every target car has a woman's name for a code, so the thieves can talk about them discreetly.[^ca25]

In October 1974 he brought the film to the Regent Theater on Third Street in Dunkirk for its East Coast premiere and promised to wreck two cars out front. About 2,500 people packed the railroad berm and the street, and the stunt was called off for safety.[^ape2]

## The water tower

In May 1989 he married Denice Shakarian at a house in Dunkirk, with his older brother Rudy, a town justice, officiating.[^ape2] That summer he was filming *Gone in 60 Seconds II* in Tonawanda, just north of Buffalo.[^upi] He had had to buy $8 million in insurance after a local official accused him of lax safety planning, and he told the Associated Press he did fewer of his own stunts now: "I still do some of them, but I don't do any of the hard ones."[^lat89] On Sunday, August 20, an empty water tower rigged to fall came down early, while workers were still cutting its legs with torches. A cable snapped and brought down a telephone pole, and the pole killed him. He was 48.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^lat89][^upi][^ape1] It was 101 days after the wedding.[^ape2]

His warehouse on Vermont Avenue in Gardena sat locked for three years, full of toys, cars and, in one room, Eleanor, "the action car crunched during a hundred stunts." George Barris, a friend, was to help with the auction of the collection planned for July 1992 at the Pasadena Convention Center.[^lat92]

## Eleanors for sale

Denice Halicki ended up with the car and the film rights, showed Eleanor at car shows, and in 1995 optioned a remake to Disney's Hollywood Pictures, keeping the right to merchandise "utilizing the car known as ‘Eleanor’." The 2000 remake's Eleanor was a 1967 Shelby GT-500. Carroll Shelby's trust registered "Eleanor" as a trademark for automobiles, and in 2002 Shelby licensed Unique Motorcars, Inc. to build cars resembling the remake's car. In 2004 Halicki sued over the "replicas of Eleanor."[^ca08]

The replica business had trouble of its own. On November 1 and 2, 2007, Farmers Branch, Texas, police, with state police and transportation officials, searched five Dallas-area sites of Unique Performance, which had been building continuation GT500E "Eleanors" at $100,000 to $200,000, and impounded 61 Mustangs and Camaros with improper or illegible VINs. No one was arrested. The owner, Doug Hasty, said the company had delivered 119 cars since 2003.[^aw07] Before the month was out it had filed for Chapter 7 bankruptcy listing no assets, and Shelby's licensing arm had cut ties.[^aw07b]

In Oklahoma, Jason Engel had grown up in his father's used-car business in Bethany. After the remake came out he spent more than $70,000, and maxed out his credit cards, building his own Eleanor, then sold it at Barrett-Jackson for $141,000 and came home with orders for four more. He and his father started Classic Recreations in Yukon, only blocks from Route 66. In 2011 he met Shelby, and they drew up a deal for licensed Shelby Mustangs within a week. He also took a Mustang to Oklahoma car shows to sit at a state foster-care booth: "If a guy and his wife are devoted enough to build their own custom car, I feel they would be devoted enough to be a great foster family."[^m405]

## A prop, not a character

Halicki and Shelby settled in 2009, then fell out again when Shelby licensed Classic Recreations to build its GT-500CR, and Halicki wrote to owners, auction houses and the shop asserting a copyright. Shelby sued.[^ca25] On May 27, 2025, the Ninth Circuit ruled that across four films and eleven versions, yellow and black, gray and black, and rusty and unpainted, Eleanor had no consistent traits. Halicki's argument, Judge Jeremy Kernodle wrote, "stalls at the starting line: we hold that Eleanor is not a character, much less a copyrightable one." Eleanor, he wrote, "is more akin to a prop than a character."[^ca25] The court found Shelby had not broken the settlement, which barred cars copying Eleanor's distinctive hood and inset lights, and sent the case back for the lower court to issue the declaration Shelby had asked for.[^ca25]

So the courts called it a prop. In Dunkirk, by his brother's account, it was a neighbor's mother's name, given to a car by a towing family's boy who had learned to stage a wreck by clearing them.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **What year was the Mustang?** The Ninth Circuit's 2008 opinion: "a yellow 1971 Fastback Ford Mustang, customized to appear as a Mach 1 Fastback Mustang."[^ca08] The *Am-Pol Eagle*: "a 1973 yellow Ford Mustang stunt car."[^ape2] The 2025 opinion says only "a yellow Fastback Ford Mustang with black stripes."[^ca25] The court's facts came from the 2005 district court record; the weekly's from the family. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **How tall was the tower?** The *Los Angeles Times* (1989) calls it "the 100-foot-tall water tower,"[^lat89] and UPI, from the Tonawanda police, "the empty, 100-foot water tower."[^upi] The Halicki film company's own page says "a 160-foot water tower."[^gi60] The two 1989 reports are used in the story. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Named for Eleanor Korzeniewski?** Only the *Am-Pol Eagle*'s 2009 profile, written with Ron Halicki, gives the name's origin: the stunt car was named for Eleanor Korzeniewski, "the mother of Ron's boyhood friend, Joe Korzeniewski."[^ape2] The courts say only that each target car had "a common, feminine codename."[^ca25] A Dunkirk *Evening Observer* story from 1974 would help. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

*A note on ages:* the *Los Angeles Times* of 1992 gives Halicki's age as 23 in 1974 and 38 at his death, which cannot both fit the 48 its own 1989 report gives; the 1989 figure matches his leaving home at 15 in 1955. The page uses 48.

*A spelling note:* the court opinion spells the Classic Recreations founder "Jason Engel"; *405 Magazine* spells him "Engle." This page follows the court.
