# "Halloween on Wheels": The Street Machine Nationals at Du Quoin (Illinois, 1977–2013)

*A traveling show that wore out its welcomes, a fairground town of 6,000, a Pontiac that took Best Street Machine, and a governor's decision*

## A show nobody wanted twice

The Street Machine Nationals was the big show of the Pro Street years, and it kept wearing out its welcome. Town after town took it and then let it go. A fairground town in southern Illinois kept it for thirteen years, longer than anyone, and then it went too. When it came back, it came back as a family show.

It started small, in Indianapolis in 1977, with some 1,300 cars and 10,000 spectators. After five years in Indianapolis it spent three summers in Springfield, Illinois, and one in East St. Louis in 1985. Some of the moves were for room. Others, *Car Craft*'s own history says, came because towns and police "had grown tired of the show's raunchy reputation as a booze-fueled party-fest." The tenth show was set for Ionia, Michigan, in 1986, until the city backed out at the last minute.[^cc13] The magazine handed the show to Bruce Hubley's Special Events of Indianapolis, and Hubley, *Car Craft* editor Jeff Smith and publisher Harry Hibler went to look at the Du Quoin State Fairgrounds. "It was like going from the worst possible venue (in East St. Louis) to the best possible venue (in Du Quoin)," Smith recalled.[^cc13]

## The weekend of June 1986

Du Quoin was ready for trouble. The *Southern Illinoisan* ran headlines like "Police Preparing For The Rush" and "Former Street Machine Hosts Were Glad To See It Hit The Road."[^hr86] Then the cars came. By the paper's count the show kept the police and the cleanup crews busy with 62,124 participants.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^si86] Cars cruised up and down the streets just north of the fairgrounds, under the watchful eyes of law enforcement personnel.[^si86] Larry Davis filled a reporter's notebook. "We should have come here a long time ago," Smith told the awards crowd. "I come up from Florida every year. I plan my vacation around this," Mike Melby said. Gov. James Thompson, arriving Saturday, said, "My only source of information is Mr. Skilbeck, and Mr. Skilbeck is pleased." Jim Skilbeck was his aide. Michael McBride, 10, of Ava, removed a corn dog from his mouth long enough to brush off a reporter: "I'm busy." Tim Landis, a *Southern Illinoisan* reporter, summed up Saturday night as "Halloween on wheels."[^si86]

The cars were the reason. *Hot Rod*, that November, gave Rick Dobbertin's Pontiac, his "Poncho," Best Street Machine, Best Engine and Best Engineering, and gave Matt Hay Pro Street.[^hr86] The *Southern Illinoisan*'s awards stories sit on the same page as the notebook, and that part of the page has not been read.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup>

After the weekend the paper's headlines turned to "They'll Be Back" and "Worse Didn't Happen."[^hr86]

## Thirteen summers

They did come back. In 1987 a teenager from Manteno, up the interstate, brought a mild Pro Street '66 Chevelle; Troy Trepanier, later of Rad Rides by Troy, came back with it painted pink in 1988. In 1989 more than 100,000 spectators came to a town of about 6,000.[^cc13] Bret Voelkel's air-ride '70 Mustang, shown in the 1990s, was the springboard for his company, Ride Tech.[^cc13] By 1990, *Car Craft*'s history says, many residents were writing to the local paper asking the city to throw the show out, and the show's business was hard to argue with.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^cc13]

In July 1998 Gov. Jim Edgar announced that the Street Machine Nationals would not return to the Du Quoin State Fairgrounds or anywhere else in Illinois. He had met with state Rep. Mike Bost, state Sen. Dave Luechtefeld and fair manager Ron Summers. Almost 600 tickets and arrests had been recorded at that year's show, and Luechtefeld said he had received an unprecedented number of calls about underage and public drinking, nudity and public urination. Bost said the cars were not the problem; the behavior of some of the crowd was. Richard Woodside, a city councilman who had voted to ban the show, said the people of Du Quoin "would tolerate anything within reason, but this was worse than Sodom and Gomorrah."<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^de98] The next day a *Daily Egyptian* columnist mocked the decision, calling the people who came "working class families of this region," and summing up the case against them: "The cars are too loud, the women too loose and the beer too good."[^de98b]

## Back in 2013

The show bounced around, landed in Lima, Ohio, and died there in 2004, with 791 cars and fewer than 15,000 spectators.[^cc13] In June 2013 it returned to Du Quoin for the first time since 1998. *Car Craft*'s people told WSIU it was a more family-oriented show now.[^wsiu][^cc13] The car count was nearly triple what was expected, and there were scarcely any run-ins with the law.[^cc13] Du Quoin had called it Halloween on wheels and sent it away; it let it back in when it came as a family show.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **How big was the 1986 show?** The *Southern Illinoisan* gave "the 62,124 participants."[^si86] *Hot Rod*, that November, said "2500-plus street machiners" came.[^hr86] *Car Craft*'s 2013 history says "3,500 cars and 60,000 onlookers."[^cc13] The paper's figure probably counts everyone through the gate; the magazines count cars. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Who ended the Du Quoin years?** The *Daily Egyptian* (1998): Governor Edgar decided not to renew the contract, after Du Quoin's citizens "had clearly voiced their opposition."[^de98] *Car Craft* (2013): "City leaders and Family Events representatives were unable to come to terms, and the show was not invited back for 1999."[^cc13] The state owns the fairgrounds, so both may be part of the same story; the *Southern Illinoisan* of July 1998 would show it. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **What was it worth?** *Car Craft* gives "the estimated $7 to $15 million economic impact" around 1990.[^cc13] The *Daily Egyptian* in 1998 gives revenue to Southern Illinois "estimated to be between $3 and $5 million," and Woodside said most of it went to Carbondale and Marion while Du Quoin paid for police and cleanup.[^de98] Neither names who made the estimate. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** **What did the awards stories on that page say?** The public OCR of the NewspaperArchive landing page for the *Southern Illinoisan* of June 30, 1986, p. 3, has Larry Davis's notebook and the caption with the 62,124 participants. The awards stories on that same page were not in the portion that could be read. The page returned HTTP 403, and no open copy of the page image turned up. A readable copy could add what the Sunday winners said about their cars, and who else those stories name. *Hot Rod* names Dobbertin's three wins and Hay's Pro Street, and does not fill that gap.[^si86][^hr86] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

*A note on sources:* the notebook quotes and the 62,124 caption are from the public OCR of that landing page, with the scanner's broken apostrophes put back. The awards stories on the page were not in the OCR that could be read, and they are not used here.
