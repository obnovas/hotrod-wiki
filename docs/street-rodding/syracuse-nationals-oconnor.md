# "Why Don't We Put On Our Own Show?": The Syracuse Nationals (Syracuse, N.Y., 2000– )

*A national meet that came once and left, two men from the class of 1962, a monument dealer's crane, and a first weekend with more cars than they had expected*

## The Nationals came once

The Syracuse Nationals grew out of a national meet that came to town once and did not come back. Two local men decided to put on their own, at the same fairgrounds, and the first weekend brought more cars than they had expected.[^pc25][^kp20]

In 1995 the National Street Rod Association held its Nationals at the New York State Fairgrounds, the only time it came to Syracuse, and 10,000 cars came from across the country.[^pc25][^hr19] People liked the grounds, with paved roads, grass, trees and lots of buildings. The NSRA wanted something different and signed a ten-year deal with Louisville, Kentucky.[^pc25] For the next few years, *Hot Rod* wrote, people at car shows kept asking two Syracuse hot rodders whether the Nationals were coming back.[^hr19]

The two were Bob O'Connor of Brewerton and Fred Fibiger. O'Connor had gone to Paul V. Moore High School in Central Square, and Fibiger to North Syracuse.[^pc25] "We both graduated in 1962, and we had a lot of the same friends in common, but we never knew each other," Fibiger said in 2000. They finally met at a car show, over street rods, especially Fords, and started traveling to shows together.[^pc25]

O'Connor had been at it a long time. As an older teenager he and his buddies formed the Road Knights, with a clubhouse in Brewerton. "In those days you'd put glasspack mufflers, fender skirts and spinner hub caps on your car, and call it a hot rod," he said years later. The club put a supercharged Chrysler Hemi in a frame and called it the Purple People Eater. His brother Bill drove, Bob was on the crew, and the family says the car went to Indianapolis and won the national championship for its division in AA Altered.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup>[^kp20] He had also been a typesetter at the *Herald-Journal*, hired before he finished high school, and later bought a monument business. When he needed a crane to lift monuments, he and Bill built one on a truck. Once, setting a mausoleum, he could not get the lifting straps out from under it, so he left and came back 20 minutes later with bags of ice. They set it on the ice, pulled the straps and waited for the ice to melt.[^kp20] In the mid-1990s he built a '32 Ford woodie street rod, with a cabinet maker doing the wood and O'Connor the rest.[^kp20]

## Hot Rod Heaven

"We were talking about it and suddenly we said, 'Why don't we put on our own show?'" O'Connor said. Fibiger said he told him it was a good idea, "hung up the phone, and that's all there was to that."[^pc25] It was not. They made their deal with the fairgrounds, and the Central New York Car Club Association helped put the show on.[^kp20][^hr19] By the eve of the first show, the *Post-Standard*'s car writer, Kenn Peters, wrote that "for the last several months, the two have become inseparable."[^pc25]

The first Syracuse Nationals ran July 28–30, 2000, in the Chevrolet Court area of the fairgrounds, on a scorching weekend.[^pc25][^kp20] Fibiger had pictured 1,500 cars; Peters later recalled that the partners thought 400 to 500 would be a good turnout.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>[^pc25][^kp20] 4,300 cars and trucks came, from as far as California and Texas, from Canada, and one from Australia.[^pc25][^hr19] By midday, Peters wrote, show cars were still streaming through the gates and parking on the midway and the side streets.[^pc25] Newspaper reports put the crowd at 30,000.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>[^pc25] Pop-up car shows sprang up at Carousel Center, Northern Lights Plaza, Sylvan Beach and local ice cream stands, and the chamber of commerce estimated the weekend brought about $4.3 million into Central New York.[^pc25] The partners signed a four-year agreement with the fairgrounds and moved the show up a week so it no longer ran against Oswego Harborfest.[^pc25]

Fibiger stayed two years and sold his share back to O'Connor.[^kp20] Within a few years O'Connor formed the Right Coast Association, the show's parent.[^hr19][^kp20] The first giveaway was a car trailer, then a 2001 Chevy S-10 pickup, and in 2002 a yellow '32 Ford highboy street rod. That car became the show's emblem, and it is the only giveaway with "Syracuse Nationals" pinstriped down both sides, by Bob and his son Rob.[^kp20] Tucci Hot Rods of Marcy, father and son Dave and Dom Tucci, built the ten custom giveaway cars that followed, the last a '32 highboy for the 20th show in 2019.[^kp20]

## The man with the golf cart

O'Connor ran it like a man who still did his own lifting. During show week he could be seen loading his golf cart with ice for vendors and directing traffic in the upper lots.[^kp20] One year the campground power failed, and the truck brought in to replace it sent a surge that fried campers' microwaves and televisions. His son said he went camper to camper asking what was ruined, bought replacements that day and delivered them the next. "I think some campers might have gotten things they didn't deserve," Rob O'Connor said, "but that was my Dad, always doing something for others."[^kp20]

The show kept growing. In 2013, at 69, O'Connor expected nearly 7,800 cars, the paper expected an estimated 80,000 people, and he said the number of cars had grown 15 to 20 percent a year.[^rm13] By its 20th year *Hot Rod* counted more than 8,000 cars from 1985 and earlier and almost 100,000 spectators, with Gene Winfield choosing his own award and pinstripers from around the country painting panels for a charity auction.[^hr19] By then his son Rob was presiding over the show with the Right Coast team and the car club association.[^hr19] By the end of 2019 it had given $1.5 million to charity, and Bob O'Connor died that December.[^kp20]

The 26th show, in July 2026, moved to a Thursday-to-Saturday schedule, added a lowrider hydraulics throwdown on opening night and ended with a burnout afterparty at ESTA's drag strip north of Syracuse.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>[^cny26] Its charities took in a record $107,877, and the 2027 dates were already set.[^sn26] The NSRA came to Syracuse once. Two hot rodders from the class of 1962 gave the town a Nationals of its own, and it stayed.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **How many cars did they expect?** Fibiger, quoted from the *Post-Standard*'s 2000 coverage, said they "envisioned 1,500 cars, maybe."[^pc25] Peters, writing in 2020, said they "thought would be a good turnout of maybe 400 to 500 cars."[^kp20] Croyle's 2025 piece calls 4,300 "nearly triple what was predicted," which follows the 1,500.[^pc25] Both could be true at different moments of planning. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **How big was the first crowd?** Croyle, summarizing the 2000 newspaper reports, gives 30,000 people.[^pc25] O'Connor himself told the paper in 2013 that the first show drew 4,300 cars and 18,000 spectators.[^rm13] The 2000 *Post-Standard* stories themselves would settle which count was the paper's. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **What did the Purple People Eater win?** Only the family's account in Peters's 2020 profile says the Road Knights' car won "the national championship for its division" in AA Altered at Indianapolis; it gives no year, and we have not found the result.[^kp20] An NHRA Nationals results list, or the *Herald-Journal*, would date it. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Cicero or Clay?** *This is CNY* in July 2026 places the show's burnout afterparty at "ESTA Drag Strip in Clay."[^cny26] syracuse.com in 2016 and 2024, CNY Central in 2022 and the strip's own site put ESTA Safety Park in Cicero.[^sy16][^sy24][^cnyc22][^esta] No source we read explains the difference. The same question is open on [ESTA Safety Park](../drag-racing/esta-safety-park-cicero-new-york.md#q-2). <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

A spelling to settle: the 2025 syracuse.com piece spells the co-founder Fibiger (and once Fibinger, in a caption); Peters in 2020 and *Hot Rod* in 2019 spell him Fiebiger.[^pc25][^kp20][^hr19] The story follows the 2025 piece, which quotes the paper's 2000 interview.
