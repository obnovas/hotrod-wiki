# Honest Charley, Hisself: The Chattanooga Speed Shop That Sold by Mail (1948–1990)

*A lunch counter with no room for the register, a trunkful of parts at the stock car races, and a hand-drawn catalog that mountain hot rodders called the bible*

## A register with no room

Before the parts, there was lunch. Charles Edward Card Jr., born in 1905, opened a restaurant on Chestnut Street in downtown Chattanooga in 1940, across from the Read House.[^wiki] It got so busy during the war that the cash register went. Card told *Hot Rod* in 1970 that there was no room for it on the counter, "so I put it outside the door," and asked customers on their way out what they owed. "Customers said, 'My gosh, Charley, you must be honest.' An' that was it. I painted 'Honest' real big on a sign atop the restaurant."[^sema12] The tellings drift: one has him removing the register to make room for one more paying customer, and the revived company's history puts it on the sidewalk under a sign that said "How Honest Are You?"<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^wiki][^mopar]

The "Hisself" came later. People meeting him, Guldahl wrote, kept asking, "You're not Honest Charley, are you? Are you Honest Charley hisself?"[^sema12]

## A trunkful of parts

Card was a car man before he was a parts man. In the 1940s, his biography says, he spent weekends driving to stock car races with his wife, Grace, their two daughters, and a trunkful of racing parts to sell or trade.[^wiki] The *Times Free Press* calls him a leader of the Chattanooga Racing Association, "one of the original sponsors of NASCAR."[^tfp16] The company's own feature tells of a hot rodder who tried to pay for a meal with an aftermarket intake manifold for a flathead Ford; Card took it and sold it at a profit.[^mopar]

In 1948 he opened Honest Charley Speed Shop with his son-in-law, Robert J. Espy, as a partner, in a 260-square-foot storefront at 2204 McCallie Avenue.[^wiki] The trade remembers him for the catalog. The early ones were drawn by hand, by Card and his daughters, with a caricature of "Hisself" for a logo and cartoons in the margins; SEMA calls him "one of the first to publish a parts catalog as far back as 1948."[^wiki][^sema12] The slogans did the selling: "Send money, I'll pay the postage," and "Your money back if it ain't right!"[^mopar] His biography calls the shop the first coast-to-coast mail-order distributor of high-performance parts; the hometown paper's 2016 profile says "the world's first mail-order speed shop."<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^wiki][^tfp16]

## The mountain trade

Chattanooga sat near the Georgia, Alabama and North Carolina lines, and the company's feature says moonshine runners were good customers in the early years: the multiple carburetors, suspension tricks and free-flowing exhaust on the old Fords that hauled whiskey through the mountains came from Honest Charley's.[^mopar] To the northeast, Southwest Virginia's hot rodders had no speed shop of their own. The Blue Ridge Institute says they used junkyard parts or ordered by mail, and that "the regional mail-order 'bible' for the region's car builders was the catalog from Honest Charley's Speed Shop in Chattanooga, Tennessee." Their clubs and their strip at Roanoke are told on the [Roanoke Drag Strip](../drag-racing/roanoke-drag-strip-southwest-virginia.md) page.[^bri1]

The shop grew with them. It moved to a 14,000-square-foot store at 3813 Rossville Boulevard, and in 1964 Card built a 30,000-square-foot distribution warehouse at 108 Honest Street, near the runway of Lovell Field.[^wiki] Retail stores opened in Tennessee, Georgia and Florida, but most of the business still came through the catalog. Racers who won with Honest Charley stickers on their cars collected an "Honest Charley Bonus," and the name rode on Jungle Jim Liberman's funny cars.[^mopar] When Guldahl visited in late 1970, Card had just become the second inductee into SEMA's Hall of Fame and was proud of the IBM 360 that tracked his inventory and orders.[^sema12]

## After Hisself

Card died in 1974 by SEMA's and his biography's count, October 26; in 1975 by the *Times Free Press* and the company's feature.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup> Espy kept the business going until 1990. The fuel crisis, bigger retailers and emissions rules are the reasons given for its end.[^wiki][^sema12][^tfp16][^mopar] In 1998 Corky Coker of Coker Tire bought the name and trademarks and brought it back. "This shop, and this name rings a bell with just about every hot rodder and drag racer from the early days, and I couldn't stand the thoughts of it going away," he told the *Times Free Press*.[^sema12][^tfp16]

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **What happened to the restaurant's cash register?** Card's own words to *Hot Rod* (1970), as SEMA quotes them: no room on the counter, "so I put it outside the door," and customers told him what they owed. Wikipedia: he removed it to make room for one more paying customer and collected at the door. Mopar Connection, written by the revived company's project editor: on busy days customers made their own change, and he put the register "on the sidewalk with a sign that said, 'How Honest Are You?'" The nickname is the same in all three.[^sema12][^wiki][^mopar] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Did Charley Card die in 1974 or 1975?** SEMA (2012) says he "passed away in 1974," and Wikipedia gives Oct. 26, 1974. The *Chattanooga Times Free Press* (2016) and Mopar Connection (2017) both say 1975. A Chattanooga death notice would settle it.[^sema12][^wiki][^tfp16][^mopar] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Was Honest Charley the first coast-to-coast mail-order speed shop?** Wikipedia says the catalogs "established Honest Charley's as the first coast-to-coast mail order distributor," and the *Times Free Press* profile's "claim to fame" line says "the world's first mail-order speed shop"; both read as the brand's own claim, and neither names the record behind it. SEMA says only "one of the first." Bell Auto Parts, which the site calls "an early mail-order speed shop," is told on the [Prewar Speed Shops](prewar-speed-shops.md) page.[^wiki][^tfp16][^sema12] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
