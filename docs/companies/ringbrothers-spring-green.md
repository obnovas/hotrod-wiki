# Still Fixing the Deer Dents: Ringbrothers (Plain and Spring Green, Wisconsin, 1987–2024)

*Two brothers from a family of seven, a basement Camaro, a body shop on Highway 14, and a SEMA best in show that never moved them out of town*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium (the shop's history comes from the brothers themselves, in a 2015 Wisconsin newspaper profile and two later interviews; the dates agree) · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> a Wisconsin newspaper profile by Barry Adams (*The Northwestern*, Oshkosh, 2015); interviews in *Top Gear* (2024) and HotCars (2022) · **Last verified:** 2026-11-21 (Round 46) · **Open issues:** none · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "It's no different than the day we started. We don't want to give up what we do. We don't want to be on the phone. We don't want to be selling. We really don't want to be in front of the cameras. We just want to do the work."

    — Mike Ring, to Barry Adams, 2015.[^nw]

## The youngest of seven

Ringbrothers builds restomods for clients as far away as Russia, and it is still a body shop in a Wisconsin village that will fix the hood you crumpled on a deer.[^nw] Jim and Mike Ring built cars for clients in Russia and on the SEMA floor without leaving town, and they kept buying their parts and doing their favors close to home.

They grew up in Plain, Wisconsin, the youngest of seven children. Their father, Clete, ran a gas station there for 30 years; before the boys were born, he had survived the sinking of the U.S.S. *Gambier Bay* in 1944.[^nw] They were "D" students at River Valley High, except in shop class.[^nw] It was a three-bedroom, one-bathroom house. "I learned how to dance waiting for the bathroom," Jim said.[^tg] His first car was a '55 Chevy, six-cylinder, three on the tree, which he built in his mother's basement. There were no seats yet, so he sat on a five-gallon bucket to pull it into the garage, let the clutch out, fell off the bucket, and drove the car into his mother's water heater. The house had no hot water for about three weeks.[^tg]

Mike went into the Navy, and Jim ran a crane for a while. In 1987, living in Chicago and working for an environmental services company, Jim talked a bank into a $5,000 loan for a 1969 Camaro pace car. "I basically restored that car in my mom and dad's basement and that's kind of when the bug bit me," he said. He made good money on it and decided that was the business.[^nw]

## A body shop on Highway 14

He moved home in 1991 and started a restoration business.[^nw] "We've had the shop here since 1993," Jim said in 2024. "Before that I was in another building down the road, and before that it was my mother's garage."[^tg] In 1994 Mike came home and joined him in the new 5,000-square-foot shop.[^nw] The body shop, Classic Autobody, sits on Highway 14 in Spring Green, seven miles from Plain, and it still does collision work: a door ding, or a hood from "an ill-timed deer on the family minivan."[^nw][^tg] The first request for a full custom build came in 2005, for a 1967 Mustang. They added 3,500 square feet in 2009.[^nw]

In November 2014 a 1966 Chevelle they built for an Ohio man, called *Recoil*, was the General Motors Best in Show at the SEMA Show in Las Vegas, and early in 2015 Jay Leno put the car and the brothers on his YouTube show. "Everybody thinks you've gotta be in L.A. or New York and these major markets where all the suppliers are and you guys are just about in the middle of the country," Leno told them.[^nw] A junked 1972 Winnebago they had turned into a 900-horsepower rolling sports bar, with a fireplace and a glass panel in the floor, went viral. "We've got about $40,000 into it," Jim said. "Kind of stupid, ain't it? But you can't take it with you."[^nw]

By 2015 they had 10 employees in an 8,500-square-foot shop and built about two cars a year, each with $50,000 down and as much as 5,000 hours of labor. Requests came from New Zealand, Australia, the Netherlands and Japan, and one man in South Africa wanted them to work only for him for five years. They said no. Before taking a client they sometimes looked up his property on Google Earth.[^nw]

## Close to home

They kept the business local where they could. In 2015 their engines came from Wegner Motorsports in Markesan, their welding supplies from Miller Electric in Appleton, and their spray booth from Global Finishing Solutions in Osseo. They tested cars on a village street behind the shop with no homes, no businesses and no cross street. They painted crucifixes for a cemetery restoration and the Stations of the Cross at St. Luke's Catholic Church in Plain.[^nw] That summer the Hot Rod Power Tour, starting in Madison, was set to stop at the shop.[^nw]

By 2024 the shop had 24 employees and still did one or two cars a year, each taking a year and a half or two, alongside "a full collision shop which is usually crazy busy" and a line of parts for other people's restomods. A designer, Gary Ragle, was a big part of the process, and parts that did not look right in metal went in the dumpster.[^tg] Jim said the hardest part was finishing: getting a painted car to run and drive right.[^tg] At SEMA in 2022, asked if every car was a client's, they laughed: "We can't afford what we do, we're still saving up for a new lawnmower."[^hc] It was what Mike had said in 2015, back at the body shop with a sander in his hand: no different than the day they started.[^nw]
