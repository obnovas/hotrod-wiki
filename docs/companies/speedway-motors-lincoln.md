# The Empty Box on the Shelf: Speedway Motors and the Speed Shop That Stayed in Lincoln (Nebraska, 1929–2014)

*A teaching degree turned down, a $300 loan, and a pickup truck at the bus station every evening*

## A Lincoln kid and a Maytag go-kart

Speedway Motors grew into one of the largest companies in the performance aftermarket without ever leaving the town where it started, a 400-square-foot store in Lincoln, Nebraska, that only looked fully stocked because of the empty boxes on its shelves.[^rc][^moas][^sema14]

Bill Smith was born in Lincoln in 1929. A neighborhood tinkerer named Milo Caslasky encouraged him. At 12 he built a go-kart out of a Maytag washing machine and baby-buggy wheels, and the police ran him off the street. At 14 he bought a Model T from Caslasky and started fixing and reselling Model Ts for spending money.[^rc] He talked his father into taking him to the midget races at Landis Field. "I remember thinking it was the greatest thing that ever was," he said.[^rc]

The car scene was at Elmer's Conoco station, with "hoods up on their cars," he remembered.[^rc] He once heard a set of Smithy's mufflers, found the nearest set in Denver, and drove there from Lincoln to buy them for his '40 Ford coupe.[^rc] He raced motorcycles on dirt tracks, then roaring roadsters, without telling his mother. He built his first track roadster, number 4x, for the Nebraska Hot Rod Racing Association.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^rc][^moas] After he drove his roadster through a fence in a race at Hastings, he stopped driving and started building cars and hiring the best drivers he could find.[^rc]

## Two of everything

In 1952 he was finishing an industrial education degree at Nebraska Wesleyan University and was offered a teaching job at $2,750 a year. His race car had earned about $4,500 the summer before.[^rc] He turned the job down. With a $300 loan from Joyce, his college sweetheart, he opened Speedway Motors in a 400-square-foot building at 2232 O Street.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^rc][^sema14] To look busier than it was, he put each new part in the window and its empty box on the shelf, "making it look like I had two of them."[^rc]

By 1954 the business had moved a few blocks to 5,000 square feet at 1719 N Street, with room for engine work, swaps and header installations.[^rc] He kept racing as an owner. In 1952, his first year in business, a stock-car driver of his recommended a young motorcycle racer named Lloyd Beckman, and in 1960–61 Beckman drove a Speedway-built '32 Ford modified to 16 straight feature wins at Capitol Beach Speedway in Lincoln.[^moas] Smith also worked with a local fiberglass company on a 'glass '27 Model T roadster body in 1953, and in 1962 Speedway opened its own fiberglass plant, in time for the T-bucket craze.[^rc]

## Joyce's price sheets and the evening bus

Joyce kept the books and dealt with the employees and customers, and the family museum calls her as big a reason for the company's success as Bill.[^moas] He had her type price sheets to take to the races, and by the early '60s they were printing a catalog for mail order.[^rc] Shipping was the hard part in the middle of the country. There was no UPS in Lincoln, and parcel post wasn't dependable, he said, so he used Greyhound and Continental buses, which stopped in every small town and could deliver overnight within 500 miles. "Every evening you'd see me driving to the bus station in my pickup truck, loaded with packages and bus freight bills."[^rc]

Not everything was a parts staple. In the early 1960s he came up with Cool Man Glass Tint, clear lacquer with a tint in an aerosol can, at $5.95. A Chicago salesman, Herb Goldstein, sold it for him at a 15 percent commission, and the fad lasted maybe 18 months. With the profits, he wrote, he bought the race track in Lincoln for $5,000 from his former partners and "eventually lost all my money in the track."[^arg]

## Still in Lincoln

Speedway was at the first Street Rod Nationals in 1970.[^rc] In 1992 Bill and Joyce started the collection that became the Museum of American Speed, and in 2000 the company moved to 42 acres in west Lincoln.[^moas] The speed business was centered in California, and Smith had chances to move, but the museum says his love for Lincoln and the Midwest kept him and the business in his hometown his whole life.[^moas] Joyce died in August 2013. Bill died May 30, 2014, in Lincoln, at 84.[^moas][^hr14]

His four sons took over a company with more than 20,000 parts in its catalogs, and it was still in the same town where he had once set empty boxes on the shelf.[^moas][^rc]
