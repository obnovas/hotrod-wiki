# "I Never Intended to Sell a Quart": Jon Kosmoski and House of Kolor (Minneapolis, 1956–2026)

*A Minnesota painter who made paint for himself, a scarab from King Tut's tomb, and a brand that came out of local car shows*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium (the story is Kosmoski's own, told across many years to magazines, an auction house, a museum and his company's anniversary release; they differ on the early details) · **Source types:** *Custom Classic Trucks* (2009); Mecum Auctions' profile with his own recollections; the Sturgis Motorcycle Museum Hall of Fame; *FenderBender* (2026); the House of Kolor 70th-anniversary release (2026) · **Last verified:** 2026-11-23 (Round 48) · **Open issues:** ⚠ how it started, and whose basement; ⚠ when he began selling paint · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "My inspiration was I wanted the best stuff for me. I never intended to sell a quart of it to anyone."

    — Jon Kosmoski, to Jay Sicht of *FenderBender*, 2026.[^fb26]

## A '40 Chevy in Minneapolis

The brand that calls itself the original custom paint did not start in Southern California. It started in Minneapolis, with a young painter who wanted better paint for his own work and had to make it himself. The painters who saw it at local shows wanted it too, and that is how a basement paint shop became a company.

Kosmoski was born in Minneapolis. His family moved to Puget Sound, where his father worked in the Navy yard, and came back when he was in sixth grade.[^mecum] At 16 he drove a modified 1940 Chevy business coupe, and every time he saw a 1934 Ford, he said, "my heart would skip a beat."[^mecum] In 1956, by *Custom Classic Trucks*' account, he took the '40 Chevy, with a '56 Chevy 265 Powerpack engine he had put in, to "the best shop in town" for paint and was so unimpressed that he decided to become a custom painter and make his own paint.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^cct09] The company's 70th-anniversary release puts it in his words: "What started as a frustration with an uninspired paint job turned into a lifelong mission."[^pr26]

He started small. He painted helmets and, one winter, 600 sets of motorcycle tins in his mother's basement, until she tired of the fumes.[^fb26] Then he rented a stall at a friend's body shop. "My friend Tom had a body shop. He let me use the shop to paint used cars he regularly flipped," he told Mecum. "I made mistakes, but he didn't care. He would always say, 'You're paying for the paint and redoing it.' That's how I learned."[^mecum]

## Best Paint, and a chemist at $50 an hour

He started winning Best Paint at local car shows, and painters came to him wanting his formulas. Around 1962, *FenderBender* wrote, he became an official paint maker.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^fb26] He bought a four-stall shop and called it House of Color; in 1964 he moved across the street and changed the C to a K, for Kosmoski.[^mecum] "I was already making my own paint and clear and learning to grind pigments," he said. "The quantities were adding up, so I hired a guy who had a doctorate in polymer chemistry." The man came once a week for a few hours to answer questions. "I paid him $50 an hour, but it was worth it for the education."[^mecum]

The trouble he was solving was a real one. Nitrocellulose lacquer, the custom painter's paint, was discontinued in the late 1950s, and the acrylic lacquer that replaced it would not take bright pigments well. "What I learned was that nitrocellulose was a natural dispersing aid for pigment, allowing any pigment to be used, whereas the acrylic was difficult for the pigment to incorporate," he explained, in words the Sturgis museum quotes.[^sturgis] He went to organic pigments, which cost more than the inorganic ones the industry used but gave the saturated colors he wanted, and he made an acrylic lacquer that did not crack in a Minnesota winter.[^fb26][^mecum]

In the late 1960s he read that King Tut's tomb held a scarab amulet, and he had an artist, Dave Bell, draw a many-colored scarab for the company's symbol.[^mecum] And instead of keeping his methods secret, *Custom Classic Trucks* wrote, he drove into any town with a good paint store and painters who wanted to learn, set up a clinic in a host's shop, picked up a gun and showed them.[^cct09] By Mecum's count he spent more than four decades on the road, 30 to 40 seminars a year.[^mecum]

## Kandies, urethane and a sale

In the fall of 1982 he released the Kosmic system, candies and acrylic urethane over a base coat of organic pigments.[^mecum] "It took better than 10 years of work," the museum quotes him. He built it for a 15-mil film, more than four times the thickness of a new car's paint, with extra ultraviolet absorber, and said, "It's not uncommon to see a paint job 20 years old with our products."[^sturgis] He told *FenderBender* what urethane changed: before, a painter laid the base one day, let it dry overnight, put the candy on by noon the next day and waited a couple of hours to clear it.[^fb26]

In 1997 he sold House of Kolor to Valspar, also a Minneapolis company, and stayed on as a consultant and the face of the brand.[^cct09] The Sturgis Motorcycle Museum put him in its Hall of Fame in 2003.[^sturgis] Today the brand belongs to Sherwin-Williams, which opened the 70th year in April 2026 by announcing eight anniversary colors.[^pr26] Kosmoski was still talking about it that fall. "You take a raw piece of steel, whether it be motorcycle parts or a car — or whatever it is — and make a thing of beauty out of it. Let me tell you something — that does not get old."[^fb26] He had only wanted the best paint for himself, and the painters at the Minnesota shows saw it and wanted the same thing.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **How did it start, and whose basement?** *Custom Classic Trucks* (2009) says that in 1956 Kosmoski took his '40 Chevy to "the best shop in town," was unimpressed, and started making paint "in his parent's basement," and that neighbors complained about the lacquer smell.[^cct09] The 2026 anniversary release has him calling it "a frustration with an uninspired paint job."[^pr26] *FenderBender* (2026) says he painted helmets and 600 sets of motorcycle tins "in his mother's basement" until she tired of the fumes, and the Sturgis museum also says his mother's basement and has him painting helmets and motorcycles for friends after high school.[^fb26][^sturgis] Mecum dates his start to 1956 "by painting motorcycle tanks."[^mecum] The stories can all be true at once; they are told differently, all by him or from him, decades later. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did he start selling paint?** The company counts from 1956.[^pr26] *FenderBender* says he became an official manufacturer "around 1962" and hired a chemist then.[^fb26] Mecum puts the four-stall House of Color first and the 1964 move and new name after, with the chemist as the paint quantities grew.[^mecum] The Sturgis museum says that "by the mid-1960s" he began making his own line of Kustom Kandy finishes, and "House of Kolor was born."[^sturgis] So 1956 is the start of his painting, and the paint business came in the early to mid-1960s, a year we have not pinned down. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
