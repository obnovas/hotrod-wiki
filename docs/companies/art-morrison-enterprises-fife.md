# "A Better Way to Make a Living": Art Morrison Enterprises (Fife, Wash., 1968 or 1971– )

*A wheelstander crash, a father's carpentry shop, 135 dragsters, and a chassis business that followed its customers from the drag strip to the street*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium (the story is the Morrisons' own, told to a Tacoma business paper in 2001 and to car magazines from 2018 to 2021, and the sale is the buyer's account to his old university; the sources differ on when the business started) · **Source types:** the *Tacoma Daily Index* (2001); Art Morrison to the *Goodguys Gazette*, reprinted by Fuel Curve (2021); Art and Craig Morrison to In The Garage Media (2020) and *Chevy Hardcore* (2018); Matt Jones to Central Washington University's *Crimson & Black* (2023); Performance Racing Industry (2022); the company's own page · **Last verified:** 2026-11-25 (Round 50) · **Open issues:** ⚠ 1968 or 1971; ◐ the Corvette comparison · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "Skill had absolutely nothing to do with it. We had no serious plan. The pro street deal was brought to us by customers because we built drag cars."

    — Art Morrison, *Goodguys Gazette*, reprinted by Fuel Curve, 2021.[^fc21]

## A wheelstander, and a carpentry shop

Art Morrison did not set out to make Pro Street and Pro-Touring frames. He said so himself. A drag racer near Tacoma quit driving, built race cars in his father's old carpentry shop, and let his customers tell him what to build next, from door slammers to Pro Street cars to bolt-on frames for '55 Chevys.[^fc21][^itg20]

He bought his first car at 15, in 1963, an Olds-powered 1930 Model A coupe. The engine was tired, so he and a friend went to the boneyard for a Buick Nailhead and swapped it in that weekend, welding the motor mounts with a Lincoln buzz box.[^itg20] Next came a 1954 Chevrolet with a big-block and a straight axle from a postwar Ford, still street legal, that ran in the mid-12s at 118 mph on weekends.[^itg20][^tdi01] Then the Army drafted him. He served 13 months in Vietnam and was discharged on his 21st birthday.[^itg20]

In 1969 he met Chuck Poole, who ran the Chuck Wagon wheelstanders, and by 1970 he was driving a nitro-burning, big-block VW pickup wheelstander.[^itg20] Then he drove a wheel-standing AMX for a friend, Richard Schroeder. In 1971, Fuel Curve says, it got crossed up near the finish line and tumbled. "The harness was so loose, my whole upper body was hanging out of the window and my helmet was dragging on the ground," he said. "I was knocked out." His wife, Jeanette, saw it. "Jeanette said, 'I'm too young to be a widow,'" he said. "I decided at that moment there had to be a better way to make a living."[^fc21]

He started fixing cars at home: "Anything from an oil change to rebuilding engines and transmissions. Plus, paint jobs."[^fc21] The shop was his father's former carpentry shop, 30 by 60 feet, beside the house.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^itg20] His father was a home builder who cut down his own trees and milled his own framing lumber, *Chevy Hardcore* wrote.[^ch18] By 1972 Art was building door slammers for customers in the modified production classes.[^itg20] The *Tacoma Daily Index* named three of his early cars that held NHRA national records, Tom Turner's, Jim Warter's *Joint Venture* and Mike Ferderer's.[^tdi01] The first things he sold, he said, were engine stands and solvent tanks.[^fc21]

## Dragsters, then the street

The dragsters began with a rear-engine car for Bucky Austin. "Ultimately, I wound up doing 135 dragsters," Morrison said. "Most of them econorails."[^fc21] His son Craig was born in 1975, the year the shop started on rear-engine dragsters, and in 1978 the business moved to an 8,000-square-foot building.[^itg20] By ten, Craig was working around the shop after school.[^itg20] In 1983, Art said, "we just weren't moving forward," so he made a catalog and advertised nationally. "My banker looked at my figures, but he loaned me the money anyway and I've just never had to look back."[^tdi01] That first catalog had door-slammer kits, ladder bars and wheel tubs.[^fc21] In 1989 the shop quit building complete cars and turned to parts.[^ch18]

The street came by way of the drag cars. Customers wanted Pro Street cars, and a shop that built drag cars knew how.[^fc21][^itg20] Going after street rods was harder. "It was around 1990 or '91 that we went to our first Goodguys show and we couldn't pay people to stop and look at us!" Morrison said. "We were just known as drag racing guys." Tooling to bend 2x4 steel rails in the mid-1990s opened that door.[^fc21] In 1990, the *Tacoma Daily Index* noted, he taught himself AutoCAD to test his design ideas.[^tdi01]

Craig joined the business after the University of Washington.[^itg20] "The minute he started," Art said, "he said 'we've got to build a bolt-on frame. No welding.'" Craig wrote a business plan for a Tri-Five Chevy frame.[^fc21] They rolled the first one under Art's own 1955 Chevy and drove it from Tacoma to a competition in Southern California, skid pad, autocross, drag race and braking. In The Garage Media wrote that the '55 ran on virtually the same level as a C5 Corvette.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^itg20] "The first year we sold, like, 20, and then it was 45, then it was 90," Art said in 2021. By then he put the total for the Tri-Five frame alone at around 1,700.[^fc21] The company calls that line, the GT Sport, the frame that "laid the foundation for the burgeoning restomod market."[^ame] Asked whether catching Pro Street in the '80s and '90s and Pro-Touring in the 2000s was skill or luck, he said skill had nothing to do with it. "If you work hard enough at anything, you'll find those are the luckiest people in the world."[^fc21]

In 2005 a professor at Central Washington University got a call from "a businessman in Fife who needed an engineer that was interested in cars." The student he sent was Matt Jones, from Kent, who had raced at Pacific Raceways.[^cwu23] Jones became lead engineer, operations manager and president, working beside Art and the late Craig Morrison, PRI said in 2022.[^pri22] In July 2022 Morrison sold him the controlling interest.[^pri22][^cwu23] Jones said Art could have sold for "twice as much" to a large buyer, but wanted it to stay under local control. "Art couldn't bear the idea of seeing the company he built get torn apart by a big corporation," Jones said.[^cwu23] Art kept an office at the shop.[^pri22] There had been no plan. The customers brought him the work, and the shop that started beside his father's house stayed in Fife.
