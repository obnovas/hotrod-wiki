# Under the Light Pole: Mark Stielow, the Tuckers and the Garage Camaros Behind Pro-Touring (1991–2020)

*A dead '69 Camaro in a hotel parking lot, a race to St. Louis and back by morning, and a word that came over the phone*

## A hotel parking lot in Scottsdale

Pro-Touring, the old muscle car built to corner, stop and cross the country as well as it goes straight, did not start in a speed shop. It started on young GM engineers' own time, in a hotel parking lot, a home garage in Royal Oak and a backyard shop near Detroit. The shops and the parts business came after the cars, and the name came after both.

Mark Stielow grew up around the garage of his father's garbage-hauling business in Kansas City, captained the Formula SAE team at the University of Missouri, and joined GM after he graduated in 1991.[^ch] He had already been through two '69 Camaros. The first he bought out of *Hemmings Motor News* in Van Nuys during a 1988 GM summer internship and had his parents drive home to Kansas City. He crashed it at college, and then crashed another one. That is how he learned the first-generation Camaro's road manners "the hard way," and instead of giving up on the car he wanted to know how to make it handle.[^hem19] At Watkins Glen with a GM racing-support program, he watched part of that year's One Lap of America, a week of road courses, autocrosses and drag strips with highway miles between them, and decided he would run it in a '69 Camaro.[^hem19]

On assignment at GM's Desert Proving Grounds in Mesa, Arizona, he found his car in the classifieds: a '69 that didn't run, with a 427 and a Turbo 400, on reservation land nearby.[^hem19][^hr22] He had it flatbedded to his hotel. "I had it flat-bedded to the Embassy Suites and had them drop it in a spot right under one of the light poles," he told *Hemmings*. He went to work during the day and worked on the car in the parking lot at night, with help from a couple of security guards he had befriended.[^hem19] *Hot Rod* puts the hotel in Scottsdale and says the manager let him work there.[^hr22] The car's steep gears ruled out driving it back to Michigan, so he had it hauled home.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^hem19][^hr22]

## White, straight out of the can

Back in Royal Oak, Stielow and friends pulled the big-block, transmission and rear axle and sold them to pay for the project.[^hem19] The rolling chassis went to Rolla, Missouri, for an eight-point cage by Neil Castle.[^hr22] He meant to paint it red, but his painter friend groaned that it would be his third first-generation Camaro in a row in red. "Then paint it white — bright white, straight out of the can," Stielow told him, and that is how the car got its color.[^hem19]

What went under the white paint was an engineer's work. Stielow pulled GM's factory drawings for the car, built his own taller spindles, moved the upper control arm mounts, and borrowed from the GM parts bins: Corvette ZR1 brakes and a Corvette six-speed. He bought Ronal wheels at cost from the people behind Pontiac's Firehawk program, and gave up on carburetors for an early Edelbrock fuel-injection system because, he said, he understood fuel injection better.[^hem19]

## To St. Louis and back to work

For the 1993 One Lap, Stielow's crew was another young GM engineer, Kyle Tucker, and his high-school friend Tom Baker. A high-pressure fuel line came loose at Michigan International Speedway with Tucker driving, and the fire ended their week.[^hem19][^hr22] Weeks later Stielow was due at *Car Craft*'s Real Street Eliminator in St. Louis. Friends helped repair and repaint the car, and he and Tucker drove it from Detroit to St. Louis, won, and left from the awards straight for home. "We pulled into Detroit around 6 am. I dropped Kyle off, went home, put the Camaro in the garage, took a shower and got dressed, and went to work," Stielow said.[^hem19]

In 1994 John Lingenfelter built him an aluminum small-block, dyno-tested it, and Stielow drove home with it in his truck the same afternoon. The car won the One Lap's Vintage American class that year, sixth overall, and again in its third One Lap with pro driver Stu Hayner, third overall. By then it had a name from its *Hot Rod* feature: Tri-Tip.[^hem19] *Hemmings* says the magazine coverage inspired "legions of classic muscle car owners" to use their cars for more than cruising and straight-line racing.[^hem19]

## The name, by phone

The car came first, and the name came a few years later. Jeff Smith, who had just left *Hot Rod* to edit *Chevy High Performance* in 1998, wrote that he and Stielow talked, on part of a Hot Rod Power Tour around then, about cars like Stielow's, fast and drivable anywhere, and agreed the idea needed a name, the way Pro Street had one ([Pro Street](1980s-pro-street.md)). About a week later Stielow called Smith's office: "Jeff, it's Mark. I have the term – the name." It was "Pro Touring," which Stielow said combined professional car building with European road-touring cars.[^sm20] Smith fought his own management for an April cover on it. That issue mentioned Stielow's Camaro and one being built by Kyle and Stacy Tucker, "another pair of GM engineers and Mark's friends." The first cover "really didn't sell all that well," Smith wrote; the second, about a year later, did better. A rival magazine, *Popular Hot Rodding*, called the same cars "G Machines" for a while.[^sm20]

## Twister and the backyard shop

The Tuckers' car was Kyle's own '69 Camaro, which he had owned since he was 19. Kyle grew up in Missouri racing stock cars and midgets, and a local car owner, Ray Marler, steered him toward engineering.[^pri21][^hol21] The two met as GM engineers in Michigan, and in the late 1990s they built the Camaro over two and a half years, making the parts they couldn't buy. Then they made extras and sold them.[^hol21] The yellow car, Twister, debuted on the Hot Rod Power Tour in 2000.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^hol21] Kyle sold it that year. "We had bills to pay," he said. "It debuted right before SEMA that year, and it was sold before we got home."[^hol21]

The phones started ringing for cars and parts "like that."[^rj18] Kyle took a leave of absence from GM, and the company started in 2000 "to get some cash flow."<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^pri21][^hol21] He has told the next part plainly: "I had a two-car garage at home and later built a slightly larger garage in the backyard and outgrew it." Two employees worked with him in the backyard. "I still wasn't paying myself but was paying my employees."[^pri21] Then came a small middle suite in Brighton, Michigan, leased from Scooter Brothers, whose FAST Fuel Injection had the rest of the building, and he outgrew that in a year.[^pri21] A collector in North Carolina he had built cars for told him Mooresville would suit him. Tucker bought a race shop building there, and "we sold our houses in Michigan, the employees and I," he said, and moved the next year.[^pri21]

Stielow kept building '69 Camaros at night, around his GM job. His eighth, the Mule, ran in 22 how-to installments in *Popular Hot Rodding* and visited GM's design studio while the company was working on its new Camaro. A later one, the Red Devil, took 24 months of his evenings and weekends; if you wanted a copy, he sent you to the Tuckers.[^ch] Detroit Speed won SEMA's first Battle of the Builders in 2014 and won again in 2018, and Holley bought it in 2020.[^pri21] For its twentieth anniversary in 2021, the staff gave Kyle the subframe with serial number 1969, for the Camaro he sold to start the company.[^hol21]

The style has catalogs now, and you can have a whole rolling chassis shipped to your door.[^sm20] It started with a dead Camaro under a hotel light pole and a backyard shop near Detroit.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **What gears did the Arizona Camaro have, and what engine went in?** Stielow told *Hemmings* (2019) the car had 4.56 gears;[^hem19] *Hot Rod* (2022) says 4.11s. The two also differ on the One Lap engine: a 400-cubic-inch small-block with GM "Phase 3" heads (*Hemmings*) or a 406 with "Phase 6" heads (*Hot Rod*).[^hr22] Both are retellings decades later. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did Twister debut?** Holley's 2021 company story says on the Hot Rod Power Tour in 2000, and RacingJunk (2018) says "the summer of 2000," but in the same Holley story Kyle Tucker is quoted saying "It debuted right before SEMA that year," and SEMA's show is held in the fall.[^hol21][^rj18] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **2000 or 2001?** Tucker told *PRI* (2021) the company "was started in 2000," and Holley counted 20 years in 2021; RacingJunk (2018) says he formed Detroit Speed, Inc. "in the beginning of 2001" after Twister's debut.
