# The Chinchillas Next Door: Lancaster Drag-O-Way and Kohr v. Weber (Manor Township, Pa., 1957 or 1958–1960)

*A Saturday-night strip at a country airport, sixteen neighbors, a ranch house full of chinchillas, and a judge who said a silent drag race could not be done*

## Saturday nights at the Garden Spot

Plenty of drag strips were closed by a town council or a lease that ran out. The one at the Garden Spot airport outside Lancaster, Pennsylvania, was closed by a court, and the case turned on a herd of chinchillas. In 1960 the state Supreme Court said a drag race could not be made quiet, so the only cure was to stop it.

Henry S. Weber Jr. owned the land in Manor Township, with an airport and a macadam strip about 3,000 feet long, wide enough for two cars or four motorcycles. Garden Spot Airpark, Inc. ran the races. They went from 6 p.m. to midnight on Saturday nights, and on Fridays when a holiday fell at the end of the week, and now and then until 2 a.m. Sunday.[^kohr] It was known as Lancaster Drag-O-Way. Dick McLaughlin, who founded the Vagabonds Car Club of Lancaster in 1958, remembered it as "newly opened," along Route 462, and said 10,000 hot rodders jammed their way in for the opening.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^ss] Weber, under oath, put the "first season" in the summer of 1957.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^kohr]

There were some two hundred homes within half a mile. Sixteen of the neighbors went to the Court of Common Pleas of Lancaster County for an injunction against Weber and the operator.[^kohr] Two of them were Ralph L. and Elizabeth B. Kohr, whose ranch house held some 1,100 chinchillas, valued at $50,000 to $60,000. The animals, the court said, "became exceedingly nervous in the presence of violent sound and chewed at their fur." Once a chinchilla starts chewing its fur the habit sticks, and it is worthless for breeding or pelts.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^kohr]

## What the neighbors said

The testimony was specific. One neighbor said the noise "is so intense if two people, facing each other, are talking, they just stop talking or you don't hear what the other fellow is saying, you might as well stop." Another said the track lights were bright enough that "I can read a newspaper in any of the bedrooms without any lights there in the summer." With the wind behind it, the noise carried four miles. One plaintiff said the races were run to see not who would win but "who could make the most noise."[^kohr]

Weber's own testimony showed he had heard about the chinchillas early. Sometime in the middle of the first season, he said, Kohr called him over to the ranch, showed him chewed fur, and said it might be the drag race noise. Weber moved the motorcycles away from that end. Kohr called again early one Sunday morning to say someone was racing on the track, and Weber recalled, "I believe one time I sent Bill up to see him, Mr. Holz."[^kohr] Bill Holz was the promoter running the strip.[^dl]

The chancellor granted the injunction and awarded the Kohrs $9,361. He found that Kohr had built his house between the airport and the highway knowing they were there, that the planes and traffic had not bothered the herd, and that "immediately after the races started his chinchillas became highly nervous and began chewing their fur in increasingly larger proportions." A second little track was in the suit too: Paul and Verna Crouse's micro-midget track, about 800 feet from the Kohrs, where the cars were 5½ feet long with 5-horsepower engines and the biggest crowd ever was 210. It had closed in 1958, and a bowling alley stood on the site. The complaint against the Crouses was dismissed.[^kohr]

## A silent race is impossible

Weber and the airpark appealed. They said the area was mostly farmland, that the races ran one night a week for less than half the year, and that they had $118,000 in a lawful business.[^kohr] They asked that the court only order the noise and lights turned down. Justice Michael Musmanno, writing on June 30, 1960, said no: "to think of a silent automobile or motorcycle race is to conjure up what is mechanically impossible. Thus, the only remedy possible under the circumstances was to restrain the drag racing completely." The court affirmed the decree, with the costs on the appellants, and confirmed it on reargument that December. Justice Bell, joined by Justice Eagen, dissented on the damages only: he would not have paid for harm from fright without a physical injury, and he did not think the harm was foreseeable.[^kohr]

The racers did not quit. McLaughlin said Holz went looking for the other airport, in York, and talked to its operator, Oscar Hostetter.[^ss] Bruce Larson, who later won the NHRA Funny Car championship, was one of the racers who followed. "York was my home track after we left Lancaster," he said.[^ss] That strip is told in [Four Wide on the Runway](york-us-30-dragway-thomasville.md). The one at the Garden Spot is remembered for the reason it closed: a herd of chinchillas, and a court that said there was no such thing as a quiet drag race.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did Lancaster Drag-O-Way open?** Weber testified that Kohr first called him "sometime in the middle of the first season, sometime in the summer of '57."[^kohr] McLaughlin says the Vagabonds, founded in 1958, "found a home, for a while, at the newly opened Lancaster Drag-O-Way."[^ss] Both can be loosely true (a strip in its second season is still new), but the court record puts the first season in 1957. The front-matter `years` span is `[1957, 1960]`. That field is `[first, last]`, two integers, and 1957 is the earlier side of the dispute, the earlier year a source gives, so the span covers 1958. It does not decide the question. The title says 1957 or 1958. The 1960 close is the court's decree. The *Lancaster New Era* and *Intelligencer Journal* of 1957 would date the opening. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Did the chinchillas chew their own fur, or fight?** The court, from the trial record, says they "chewed at their fur in some inexplicable attitude of defense."[^kohr] McLaughlin remembered that they "would start fighting each other and rip out their fur."[^ss] The opinion is the record made at the time; McLaughlin's is a memory told some 65 years later. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Ten thousand at the opening?** Only McLaughlin's memory gives the figure: "By McLaughlin's memory, the opening was a wild one, as 10,000 hot rodders jammed their way in from around the region."[^ss] The court gives no crowd counts for the drag strip (it gives 210 as the micro-midget track's biggest crowd). <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

*A spelling note:* the court record spells the promoter "Holz," as does the 2019 history; McLaughlin's quote in *Speed Sport* spells it "Holtz." This page follows the court.
