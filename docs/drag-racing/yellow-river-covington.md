# Yellow River Drag Strip: Ten Seasons on Highway 278 and the Crash of March 2, 1969 (Covington, Ga., 1959–69)

*A funeral home's ambulance chased Garlits for* Life *magazine. Nine years later it came back for the crowd.*

In May 1960 a *Life* photographer named George Silk spent a weekend at a drag strip outside Covington, Ga., filming Don Garlits' "Swamp Rat." The local paper reported that each run "was followed by the Caldwell and Cowan ambulance in pursuit at the request of car driver" Art Malone. A sequence in the magazine showed the funeral home's ambulance, "driven by Sam Cowan and with Johnny Capes, track employee, in the front seat," on the track "chasing the souped-up championship racer."[^cn600616]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>

Nine years later Caldwell and Cowan's ambulances raced to the same strip again, along with Harwell's, Lackey's and Young's.[^cn690306b]

## Racing every Sunday (1959–66)

The *Covington News* announced the opening on July 2, 1959: "Open of the Yellow River Drag Strip is set for Saturday, July 4th, according to an announcement by S. R. Campbell, Jr., manager and supervisor of the local race course." Cars were already entered from Atlanta, Fairburn, Gainesville, Griffin, Athens, Chattanooga and Nashville, and "Some 15 to 20 local cars are also expected."[^cn590702] The same summer the paper carried the legal notice for a company "to be incorporated under the name of 'Yellow River Drag Strip, Inc.'"[^cn590702legal]

Two weeks in, Campbell said attendance at the strip, "located just off the Covington-Atlanta highway," had been very good. Racing was every Sunday at 2 p.m., Friday nights were starting, and "Colored races are held on Saturday afternoons."[^cn590716]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup> An Atlanta club, the "Drifters," had entered 28 cars the Sunday before; its John Blalock, driving Paul McWilliams' Chevrolet-powered '40 Ford coupe, was fastest of the day in C gas. Campbell expected more winners from Covington "in the near future." By August, 115 cars were entered on a Sunday.[^cn590716][^cn590820] A Jackson paper's ad that fall put the strip on "U. S. Rt. 278 4 Miles West" of Covington.[^jpa591105]

Then came Garlits. In May 1960 "One of the largest crowds to ever see a sporting event in the history of Newton County," about 4,000, watched the Tampa racer's exhibition. Malone ran 169.78 miles per hour in 8.72 seconds, and *Life*'s representatives "spent the day at the Yellow River facilities." Shug Campbell, the manager, said the magazine was preparing a feature on all kinds of racing.[^cn600519] The paper reported the spread in the issue of June 13, 1960.[^cn600616] Ten months later the same paper, previewing Garlits at the county's other strip, recalled that "Recently in Life magazine a series of pictures showed the Garlits car making a record run."[^cn610330]

By the mid-1960s the strip was a stop for factory stars. In October 1965 it booked Richard Petty, Don Nicholson and Arnie Beswick for a match race, and that December the paper called Nicholson "king of the Yellow River Drag Strip."[^cn650930][^cn651209] Not every Sunday was a big one. In May 1966 Campbell complained that "baseball and boating has definitely hurt drag racing in this section of Georgia," and even Petty was "not drawing the crowds."[^cn660512] On the big days the crowd spilled onto the road: a letter writer counted "an estimated 300 cars" parked on the shoulder for a March 1966 race and asked why the sheriff had not ticketed them.[^cn660407]

## March 2, 1969

On Sunday, March 2, 1969, Houston Platt's Camaro funny car "roared out of control at 180 m.p.h. and plowed into a crowd of spectators." The *Covington News* counted "11 dead, five in critical conditions, and nearly 50 injured from the crash and flying debris" and called it "the worst disaster in the annals of racing history in the United States."[^cn690306a]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup> Its photographs showed the skid marks "where Houston Platt's car left the asphalt and tore down the chain-link retaining fence," the bank near the finish line where spectators had stood, and "broken posts, beer cans, drink bottles, snack wrappers, shredded pieces of clothing."[^cn690306a]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-6"><a href="#q-6" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup> Jimmy Cheek of Covington was managing the track that afternoon for the owner, "S. R. 'Shug' Campbell."[^cn690306a]

The town's response filled the editorial page. A deputy at the scene radioed the police: "send all available ambulances, rescue units you can contact." Four ambulance services went, troopers kept I-20 and Highway 278 open to the hospital, and off-duty doctors and nurses came in.[^cn690306b] The paper's sports column noted that a Newton County High School athlete, Luke Odum, had been standing "a few feet from the death scene."[^cn690306c] Fifty years later Charlie Sewell, who was five months short of 19 that day, remembered the "Dixie Twister" funny car reaching "around 180 mph when it bolted from the track, sliced a deadly path through spectators, and ended back on the track."[^wcm191023]

The next day the House Motor Vehicles Committee toured the scene and then the other strip in the county, the Atlanta Speed Shop Drag Strip west of Covington. There Arnie Beswick, the funny car driver from Morrison, Ill., told them "the slick track at Yellow River Strip, which was partially sprinkled with sand in spots," had caused the crash, and that spectators had stood within 10 feet of the track "even after being warned to move back." Sanctioned tracks, he said, kept spectators 60 feet back behind metal or concrete rails.[^cn690306a]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-5"><a href="#q-5" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup> The paper added that the strip "has operated for 11 years without serious Injury"[^cn690306a]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup> and, in the sports column, that "There are 15 drag strips in the state that are not sanctioned for safety."[^cn690306c] A UPI story in Griffin put it more bluntly: "There is no Georgia law regulating safety at drag strips."[^gdn690303]

On March 4 the county commissioners suspended "all racing events" in Newton County until the General Assembly acted.[^cn690306a] It did. By July the Department of Public Safety was enforcing a new law requiring every race track operator to carry "at least one-million dollars worth of insurance per accident plus an additional $100,000 per person per accident," effective April 28. Failure to comply was a felony. Of the state's "approximate 55 race tracks, only a small number have been licensed to date."[^cn690710]

The lawsuits followed. A North Carolina widow, Mrs. Edgar Loftis of Marion, sued for $350,000 in federal court, naming the strip's corporation, its owner, an insurance agency, the driver and General Motors.[^cn690327] That June, Platt "told a federal court he is blameless in the mishap."[^on690619]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-7"><a href="#q-7" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup>

The strip did not come back. In 1974, reporting a death at the Speed Shop strip, the *Covington News* recalled the Yellow River crash and added one line about the site: "The Riverside Trailer Park now occupies that area."[^cn740430]

For how other strips answered that spring, see the safety rules a Wisconsin writer laid out after reading about Covington in [The Hospitality Drag Strip](kk-sports-arena-hospitality-bankruptcy.md), and for the sanctioned strip down the road, [From Speed Shop to Strip](atlanta-speed-shop-dragway.md).<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-8"><a href="#q-8" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup>

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** *The July 16, 1959, notice is the only place we have seen Saturday races for Black drivers at Yellow River. We have not found results, ads or a notice of when they ended. The* Atlanta Daily World *is in the same archive and is the next place to look.*[^cn590716] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** *The* Life *spread, the ambulance sequence and the Covington location rest on one paper, the* Covington News *(May 19 and June 16, 1960, and a passing mention on March 30, 1961).[^cn600616][^cn610330] We have not read the magazine itself: Google Books holds the June 13, 1960 issue, but its search page answered with a robot check, which we did not try to get past.* <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** *The death toll grew after the first reports. The* Covington News *of March 6 and the* Griffin Daily News *of March 3 say 11 dead,[^cn690306a][^gdn690303] and Charlie Sewell's 2019 column says "Eleven people died and perhaps 50 more were injured."[^wcm191023] The* Covington News *of March 13 and 27 and July 10, the* Griffin Daily News *of March 29, and the* Covington News *of April 30, 1974 ("killing 12") say 12.[^cn690313][^cn690327][^cn690710][^gdn690329][^cn740430] The injured run from "46" (the* Appleton Post-Crescent*, March 11) to "nearly 50" and "55."[^apc690311] A twelfth death after March 6 would explain the change, but we have not found the notice.* <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** *The* Covington News *of March 6, 1969, says the strip had "operated for 11 years."[^cn690306a] The same paper's July 1959 notices date the opening to July 4, 1959,[^cn590702] which makes the 1969 meet the start of its eleventh season, not the end of its eleventh year. The opening date itself is not in doubt.* <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-5" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** *The slick track and the crowd standing 10 feet away are Beswick's account, given to legislators the day after. We have not found an official finding on the cause, and the defendants' answers to the lawsuit, which might give their own account, are only summarized in the* Omega News*.*[^cn690306a] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-5" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-6" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** *The 1969 papers spell the driver "Houston Platt";[^cn690306a] Charlie Sewell's 2019 column calls him "Huston Platt" and names his car the "Dixie Twister."[^wcm191023] The papers we read do not name the car.* <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-6" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-7" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** *We have not found how the Loftis suit or any other suit ended, or exactly when racing stopped for good at Yellow River. A 1981 legal notice still lists "Yellow River Drag Strip Inc." among Newton County corporations.*[^on690619] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-7" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
