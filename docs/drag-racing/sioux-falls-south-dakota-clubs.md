# Huskies, a Coke Freeze and the Loop: Sioux Falls Hot Rodders and South Dakota's Clubs (1950–71)

*Fifteen clubs, one drive-in, a gas station with no rules, and an Iowa runway for the nearest drag strip*

    — Tom Olsen's Great Plains Street Rodders page on the Barrell drive-in, Sioux Falls.[^gpbar]

Sioux Falls did not lack for clubs. A list the Great Plains Street Rodders compiled of the city's hot rod clubs from 1950 through 1965 names fifteen Sioux Falls clubs and a girls' auxiliary, from the Rod Benders (Harry Torgelson, 1950–54) through the Gear Jammers, Road Hawks, Rebels, Kustom Kars, Pacers, Kingsman, Eliminators, Strokers, Ratios, Dominators, Road Knights and the Ford Racing Team, with two clubs in other towns, the Toppers of Aberdeen and the Howard Turtles, "formed by S.F. Igniters."[^gpclubs] The longest-lived was the Sioux Falls Igniters (Bob Schriever and Zel DePriest, 1954–64).[^gpclubs] They are the club a 1955 report to the nation's city planners described as formed after the police arrested hot rodders racing on the highways, with the Lions Club raising money for a clubhouse, tools and eventually a strip; that story is told in [What the City Planners Wrote (1955)](planners-report-1955.md).

## The loop and the Barrell

From the late 1950s into the '70s the place to be seen was the Barrell, a car-hop drive-in at 31st Street and Minnesota Avenue that "anchored" the south end of the loop. Kids cruised north past the Dairy Queen to downtown, up Phillips Avenue from 14th Street to about 6th, west to Main, south to 14th again, and back down Minnesota to the Barrell, where they parked, ordered from a speaker by the car, and sized up whatever came through. The orders were "Huskies," the Barrell's own tater tots, and a Coke Freeze. Nobody had to phone ahead: "you knew who was going to show up and when."[^gpbar]

Some of the business done there was not on the menu. "More than a few drag races on nearby I-229 were arranged through an encounter at the Barrell," the page says, then adds, "(I've heard that; I'd have no first-hand knowledge of that".[^gpbar]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup> The Barrell closed in the mid-1970s. A Burger King sits on the spot, with photographs of the old drive-in inside, and local club members still stop there on summer evenings.[^gpbar]

## A club with no rules

The Chevrolet Racing Team was hardly a club at all. "There were no meetings, no dues, and no rules," Olsen's page says; it was a bunch of Chevy lovers who worked on their cars, bench-raced and went to the drags together. They met at Park Ridge Texaco, on the southeast corner of 26th Street and Western Avenue, where the owner, Robert Williams, employed one charter member, Daryl VanderPloeg, and Williams's son Randy was the team's youngest member and "lead promoter." Randy went into the Army in 1966 and was killed in Vietnam in 1968.[^gpcrt]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> Some members kept their team jacket and a pair of "starter flags" in the car, and the page allows that an occasional drag race may have been started with them on a remote highway. Steve Selvig towed members to the strip behind his father's 327 Chevy wagon, then popped off the hubcaps and won his own class.[^gpcrt]

## The nearest strip was in Iowa

"For many years, the closest actual drag strip was at Sioux City, IA, on an unused airport runway." Olsen won G/Stock there in his '56 Chevy wagon at the 1965 NHRA Points Meet; Sioux City closed around 1967.[^gpdrag] That year a Marion man filled the gap. Glen Rapp had graduated from Marion High School in 1956, served in the Navy and in 1959 took over his father's Chevrolet dealership; he and his wife bought a Corvette and raced it across the country, drawing detailed maps of each track's layout and copying its operating procedures, then critiquing them on the drive home. In 1965 he decided the area needed a track closer to home; construction began in 1966, and Thunder Valley opened in June 1967.[^sdpb17]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup> It still runs.[^gpdrag]

Olsen's photographs from Thunder Valley fill in the local cast: Earl Britt of Earl's Pizza Palace and his '54 Corvette "Pizza Man," billed as the "Worlds Fastest 6 Cylinder Corvette"; the "Hair Bender," a factory Hemi Plymouth owned by Stewart's School of Hairstyling; Rapp's own '56 Corvette. In the early 1970s a strip at the Sioux Empire Fairgrounds ran for a few years.[^gpdrag]

## Rapid City

At the other end of the state, the Rapid Shifters formed in 1952 and became a charter club of the National Hot Rod Association in 1957; the Starters followed in the spring of 1956. The Lloyd Willey Collection at the state archives holds the two clubs' booklets of member lists, histories and car stories, and the book of a 2010 Rapid Shifters reunion.[^sdarch] A member wrote in 2022 that the club disbanded in 1965 and was reestablished in 2020.[^hambplaq] In 2024 the Rapid Shifters joined the Counts of the Cobblestone to hold the second Vintage Dirt Drags, on a 100-yard dirt course at Bandit Speedway in Box Elder, for cars from 1964 and older, after the Sturgis Dragway that hosted the first one had closed. "There's no keeping track of winners or losers, there's not money to win, it's all about just going out and having fun," said Kellan Buchholz, a Rapid Shifter.[^kota24]
