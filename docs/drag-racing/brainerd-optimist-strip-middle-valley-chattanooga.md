# Just About Thrown Out of Every Meeting: Buddy Houts and the Brainerd Optimist Drag Strip, Chattanooga (1955– )

*A charity project at the end of a farm road, and the newspaperman who said he was just about thrown out of every meeting asking for it*

## The man at the meetings

Chattanooga's Optimist drag strip was not built by a speed shop or a racer. It was built by a service club, as a charity, because a newspaperman kept asking. Buddy Houts was an engineer at WDEF radio and later the *News-Free Press*'s automotive editor. In the 1950s Chattanooga's streets often turned into drag strips and the scenes of fatal crashes, Jolley writes, and Houts, in his 1986 interview, remembered how hard it was to get anyone to act on it. "I was just about thrown out of every meeting," he said of his talks to civic and government groups.[^jol10]

He decided to promote a strip whose proceeds would go to charity, and the Brainerd Optimist Club took it up.[^jol10] Larry Rose, a member of the Road Masters Hot Rod Club that Houts helped found, remembered the start of it in a 2013 interview. In 1955, Rose said, the roads around Chattanooga were ruled by a few people. The fastest cars had been built to haul unlicensed alcohol. The teenagers and young men, some just back from the Korean War, were fascinated by what could be done with an old family sedan. The Road Masters formed that year, he said, with Charlie Epperson as president, David Prater as vice president, Johnny Morris as secretary and Houts among the founders. Dr. William G. Stephenson was the club's adviser. NHRA gave the club its charter on January 26, 1956.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^ash13] Rose also put Honest Charley's Speed Shop, "Chattanooga's 'first speed merchant,'" on McCallie Avenue across from Warner Park "during this time."<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^ash13]

The club's goal from the start, Rose said, was a drag strip in Hamilton County. In early 1956, he said, Dr. Stephenson asked the East Brainerd Optimist Club to finance and build one in Hixson, with the Road Masters to run it.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-5"><a href="#q-5" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^ash13][^jol10] The club also put on the area's first car show that year, at the Warner Park Field House.[^ash13]

## Beasts on Sunday

On May 10, 1957, a *News-Free Press* headline read, "Beasts Will Roar Every Sunday on Drag Strip Built by Brainerd Optimists with Police Okay." The strip was to be 3,160 feet long and 40 feet wide, open to the public, on Crabtree Road just off Hixson Pike in Middle Valley, where, Jolley writes, residents lived on small farms. The Optimists' president, Herbert L. Walker, said, "This strip will provide a safe and supervised place for the fellow who wants to burn up the road." Jolley adds that Houts and the Road Gents Automobile Club, "a group of boys between the ages of 16 and 21," supervised the design and building.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup> The Optimists expected to earn back their $20,000 in about three months and give the rest to charity.[^jol10]

The club members did the finishing by hand. Rose remembered "raking large stones and tree roots into the pit area and digging holes for the fence poles." For timing they had a borrowed police two-cable speed-trap indicator and a human flagman, Johnny Morris.[^ash13] The first races were in daylight. Later some lights went up along one side for about half the length, and Rose remembered night racing as a strobe effect, cars vanishing into the dark and reappearing under each light. Many of the fastest modified cars had no headlights, and past the last light post, he said, "you were on your own in the dark, a pretty scary situation."[^ash13]

The gates opened on Sunday, August 11, 1957. More than 5,000 people paid 90 cents each, and children under 12 got in free. The paper reported no accidents and that each car had been inspected by mechanics. The first class winners included David Prater in a 1933 Ford and Archie Morris in a 1934 Ford coupe.[^jol10] In the early days the overall winner got a $50 war bond, and every other finalist got a trophy.[^bod][^ash13]

Neighbors took a month. By September 11, 1957, a hundred of them had banded together and hired an attorney, Joe Van Derveer, who told the paper, "The speedway has shattered the peace and quietude of the area." They met at the Ed Allison Grocery, Hixson Pike and Thrasher Pike, and complained about traffic, noise and property values. The attorney guessed the loudspeaker could be heard downtown at the Hotel Patten.[^jol10] Jerry Summers wrote in 2021 that Houts had won city and county approval for the track, and that neighbors threatened a suit over their "right to enjoy the peace and quietness of the area."[^sum21]

## Nobody remembered

The club moved its strip, to Scruggs Road in Catoosa County, Ga., near Ringgold. The strip's own page says it moved in 1964, and Summers says the Hixson track was converted that year to Dallas Bay Skypark, a small airport. Jolley says that by 1963 John F. Flewellen had bought the Middle Valley land and built the Skypark.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^bod][^sum21][^jol10] The new strip dropped to an eighth mile in 1976 because of a creek at the end of the track.[^bod] It is one of two surviving strips of the "at least 11" that Summers counted within 100 miles of Chattanooga.[^sum21]

Clayton Adkins, the columnist's uncle, raced a 1956 Chevy at Middle Valley, and told a story from there. He had won 15 races on the strip when a young man from Dunlap showed up in a '55 Chevy with the same engine and beat him by a fender. The next weekend Adkins spent the week on new plugs, points and thinner oil and took the fan off the engine, and he beat the Dunlap man. The man accused him of modifying his engine. "I never saw him again."[^ash13]

In the mid-1980s Houts spoke at an awards banquet for the Brainerd Optimist Club. Afterward, Rose said, he told him he was amazed at "the lack of knowledge of how drag strip racing in Chattanooga came about." He had heard that a policeman started it, that insurance companies did, that other car clubs did. People thought the strip along I-75 was the original.[^ash13] Houts died in 1989. Rose said of him, "He was the best friend I ever had."[^ash14] The strip he got built was a charity project at the end of a farm road. Within thirty years its own beginning had blurred, even for the man who started it.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Which club built it with Houts?** Rose says Houts helped found the Road Masters Hot Rod Club in 1955 and was its president in 1956, and that the Road Masters were to run the strip.[^ash13] Jolley's column says Houts and "the Road Gents Automobile Club, a group of boys between the ages of 16 and 21," supervised the design and construction.[^jol10] They may be one club under two names, or two clubs. Neither source says. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did the Middle Valley strip end?** The strip's own page and Summers say 1964.[^bod][^sum21] Jolley says that by 1963 Flewellen had acquired the land and built the Skypark, which would suggest the racing ended by then.[^jol10] Jolley's column also says the Optimists in 1981 "celebrated the twenty-fifth anniversary of a new drag strip on Scruggs Road." That counts back to 1956, which is neither the 1957 opening nor the 1964 move, and the column does not say what it was counting. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did Honest Charley's open on McCallie Avenue?** Rose says that "also during this time," after the club's January 26, 1956 charter, Honest Charley's "was opened on McCallie Avenue across from Warner Park."[^ash13] The accounts that date the business to 1948 do not name McCallie. Whether he means the same shop or a later one is open. Both sides are on the [Honest Charley page](../companies/honest-charley-speed-shop.md) (q-4).<a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Was the Road Masters Chattanooga's first hot rod club?** Rose's column says the club was "Chattanooga's first hot rod club" and one of the oldest and original NHRA-chartered clubs in the country.[^ash13] Only this one account says so. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-5" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **East Brainerd or Brainerd, and a strip "in Hixson"?** Rose told Ashley that in early 1956 Dr. Stephenson "appealed to the East Brainerd Optimist Club for financing and building a drag strip in Hixson" that the Road Masters would manage. The same column puts the groundbreaking "on property located off Crabtree Road in Middle Valley." Jolley, from the May 10, 1957 *News-Free Press*, says the strip was "Built by Brainerd Optimists" on "Crabtree Road, just off Hixson Pike in Middle Valley," which he places north of Hixson. Both say an Optimist club built it. They do not agree on the club's name, or on calling the site "in Hixson" rather than Middle Valley.[^ash13][^jol10] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-5" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
