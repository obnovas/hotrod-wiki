# Seven Thousand on a Country Rock Road: Cedar Creek and Kentucky's Hometown Drag Strips (1956–72)

*A printer bought a farm south of Louisville, some boys asked to build a drag strip on it, and the county sheriff spent the next few Sundays looking for him*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium (one local-history column built on interviews with the owner and the racers, plus racers' own memories posted to a drag strip memory site; no contemporary newspaper read yet) · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> local history (David Strange's column for *The Courier-Journal*, Aug. 22, 2012, archived with additions by the Bullitt County History Museum); firsthand memories of Kentucky strips posted to Drag Strip List and the H.A.M.B. forum · **Last verified:** 2026-10-30 (Round 27 expansion; quotes checked against the pages) · **Open issues:** opening year, 1955 or 1956 ?; the seven thousand ◐; Kentucky's first strip ◐ · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "Some boys came to me in November of that year, wanting to start up a drag racing strip. I had no idea what drag racing was."

    — C. W. "Wally" Sunderhauf, owner of the farm and the Cedar Creek strip, to David Strange (2012).[^bc12]

C. W. "Wally" Sunderhauf ran a print shop. In August 1955 he bought the old Dewiess Farm on Cedar Creek Road in northern Bullitt County, just south of Louisville, and that November a group of boys came to him wanting to start a drag strip on it. He had no idea what drag racing was. So he went to look: "We went to a few tracks in other cities. Cincinnati. Saint Louis. And that looked to me like a good thing." He made an agreement with them, and the boys "went to work with a fever, leveled the track and graded everything."[^bc12]

## Opening day on a one-lane road

Opening day came in late April.[^bc12]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup> Sunderhauf remembered it as a disaster: "Can you think of what confusion and anything else you can think of trying to get some seven thousand people down a country rock road not wide enough to handle two wide."[^bc12]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup> By the next Monday he had heard from the mayor of Louisville and from the county judge, Buck Dawson, though the judge's brother turned out to be a fan of the track. People assumed Sunderhauf was running the show, the column says, when it was really "being run by just a few young people with gusto wanting to do something new."[^bc12]

Not every neighbor came around. The sheriff, Bill French, called at Sunderhauf's house one night and told him he would have to arrest him if he could find him in Bullitt County. So on race Sundays Sunderhauf spent the morning at his printing plant or a store across the line in Jefferson County, and the sheriff would come up and say, "I see you are not in Bullitt County. See you next week." The two played that scene for several weeks, until things calmed down. Complaints and lawsuits went on for years; the circuit clerk, Nancy Strange, backed the track from the beginning, and so did some of the neighbors. In the early days Joe Keller's father watered the dirt strip to keep the dust down, and Keller raced his first car there.[^bc12]

There was little prize money. Sunderhauf printed flyers listing the last meet's winners and their times, and for most of the racers, the column says, that recognition was as good as anything else.[^bc12]

## Bullitt Dragway (1959–72)

By 1959 the lawsuits, stricter rules such as teardowns, and the cost had worn out some of the original club members, and Wally and his wife Frances decided to run the strip themselves. The county blacktopped the road, barely two cars wide. The strip, now Bullitt Dragway, was paved and got a "Christmas tree" starting system, which paid for itself in a few race days. "There is nothing like selling 1400 hotdogs, 500 BBQ pork sandwiches, and Royal Crown Cola to people all day on race day," Sunderhauf said; the ice alone, a thousand pounds of it, cost more than the soft drink syrup.[^bc12] Jerry Keyes kept Bullitt Dragway results handouts and a page of the rules book; he ran a used, once-wrecked '58 Edsel, bought for $800, at Cedar Creek in the early 1960s and remembered standing on its fading drum brakes, "wondering if it would stop before the end of the track."[^dslky]

To the soldiers at Fort Knox the strip had a third name. In 2010 a former GI who had been at Fort Knox in 1964–65 and raced his street car at Ohio Valley and Lebanon Junction remembered two names as good racers of those years, Buddy Criscoe and Sherrill Huff. A Louisville racer who posts as Kentuckian answered that Lebanon Junction meant Bullitt Dragway, which "was known locally by either name because the dragstrip was in Bullitt County and located on Cedar Creek Road," and filled in the two racers: both ran hard in the modified classes, and Criscoe was the high-performance mechanic at Burns Ford, who later opened his own speed shop, Criscoe's Automotive. The GI replied, "Bullitt County was what we Gi's called it." The old soldier remembered that Criscoe's Mustang ran Chevy power, and guessed that the people at Burns Ford never knew. In the spring of 1965 another strip, Ohio Valley Raceway, opened off the GIs' road, old 31W toward Fort Knox, where the soldier always passed the sign telling him to turn right. Huff later bought it; in 2010 his son Billy, a former IHRA Pro Stock racer, was running it.[^hambky]

Before there were lights, a race ran into the night, and the street cars lined up along the strip with their headlights on while someone taped flashlights to the hoods of the race cars. The column's roll call of cars includes Lyde Wages's "Wompin Wagon" station wagon dragster and "Mighty Mouse," a tiny red Nash Metropolitan fitted out with a big engine; Tony McConnell later found it with a tree grown through it and had to cut the tree down to get the car out.[^bc12] The column does not say who built it. Photo captions the museum added to its copy, provided by Steve Alvey, say his father, Bill Alvey, built the racer from a body he was given, and that it was sold to Ron Thomas about 1965.[^bc12]

In the 1970s new federal workplace safety (OSHA) rules, and insurance that already cost more than $600 a race day and was getting hard to buy at all, caught up with the strip. In 1972 it ran a few more weeks and closed; Sunderhauf also said he was getting too old for it. "I now have the place in Corn," he told Strange. He keeps hay stored on what is left of the strip so airplanes will not crack up on it, because it lies on a due-west line to the Bluelick airport.[^bc12]

## A strip in every corner of the state

Cedar Creek had company. Dave Huber, who grew up near the strip and later drove his own dragster down to race it, believes Beech Bend at Bowling Green was Kentucky's first strip and Cedar Creek perhaps the second.[^bc12]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup> Don Jensen, a soldier stationed at Fort Knox, went to Bowling Green in August 1957 and remembered a quarter mile "on dirt, slight downhill and narrow," about 80 cars, and a '56 Chevy "just bought in So Cal" taking top eliminator at 90 mph; he was wearing a 140 mph club jacket, and nobody there would believe a car could run 140-plus in a quarter mile.[^dslky]

Racers' memories on the same site sketch the rest of the map. At Campbellsville, "the first year, in 1959, the track was clay dirt before it was paved," recalled William McNear, whose father raced there from 1959 until the track closed in 1973. At Thorn Hill in northern Kentucky, where the owner, Ralph Payne, also ran Thorn Hill Lakes, Stan Budke's first memories, from about 1957, are of a starter with a flag, who later used a traffic light and then a clock with a dial and a hand that turned to zero. Owensboro's first strip was an eighth mile, Dave Wandel of Grandview, Indiana, wrote, "as we ran at night and there was little room for shutdown," and "the Ford guys called Chevys Gators around Owensboro." Wandel also ran a '57 Ford in A/Stock on the Sturgis municipal airport in the late 1950s or early 1960s. Mike Phillips, who raced at Owensboro from 1966 to 1970, remembered a Super Stock and FX race that ran Hardinsburg on Friday, Owensboro on Saturday and Bowling Green on Sunday. And Jerry Keyes, then a student at Eastern Kentucky State College, still has the trophy from the day in 1965 when he was the only car in his class at the Richmond Dragway: it reads "Richmond Dragway Winner 1965."[^dslky]

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** **Did Cedar Creek open in 1955 or 1956?** The column dates the track "From 1955 to 1972." Sunderhauf's own account in the same column has him buying the farm in August 1955, the boys coming to him in November "of that year," and opening day "sometime in late April," which would make it April 1956.[^bc12] A contemporary Louisville or Bullitt County paper would settle it. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Were there really seven thousand people on opening day?** The number is Sunderhauf's recollection, more than half a century later.[^bc12] We have not found a crowd count from 1956. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Was Beech Bend Kentucky's first drag strip?** Only Dave Huber's belief, as the column reports it, says so.[^bc12] The earliest firsthand Beech Bend memory we have is from August 1957.[^dslky] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
