# Who Says Hot Rods Are for Kids? Columbus's Trotters, High Winders and the Neighborhood Weeklies (1955–63)

*A lawnmower engine on Linnet Drive, a club garage behind West Broad Street, and a car show that drew 20,000 people to the Fairgrounds*

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "There's too many people frowning on hot rod clubs in general. We're doing more for highway safety than most grown-ups."

    — Dave McLaughlin, 21, president of the West Side High Winders, to the *West Side News and Hilltop Record* (1960).[^hilltop60]

In November 1955 the *West Side News* found a ten-year-old with his own hot rod. Bob Cunningham and his father, Merle, had seen a King Midget at the Ohio State Fair and built a wooden-framed car of their own for a little over $35. "It has a one-half horsepower engine," Bob said. "I think it is the same kind used in gasoline lawnmowers." He drove it in front of the house on Linnet Drive, with a hand throttle and homemade brakes and clutch, and his only problem, the caption said, was whether he needed a license tag.[^wsn55]

## The Trotters' show

The biggest hot rod event in town belonged to the Trotters Hot Rod Club, with its address at 671 N. Fourth Street. In March 1958 its ad in the *West Side News* announced a "Gigantic Auto Show," a "100 Car Show" of rods, antiques, customs, cycles and sports cars, at the Youth Center on the Ohio State Fairgrounds.[^wsn58] By 1961 it was the "Fourth annual Trott-A-Rama," with over 200 show cars "from a 15-state area," sanctioned by the National Hot Rod Association and the Mid-Ohio Timing Association. "Last year over 20,000 people viewed the show," the *Eastern Review* reported.[^er61] The show's publicity worked hard on the name: "The term 'hot rod,' popularly used to denote today's teenager's souped up cars, is erroneous," it explained; a hot rod was any vehicle individually designed and built, and the show cars carried TV sets, running hot and cold water, fur-lined interiors and, in one case, a collapsible middle that "compresses like an accordion" on impact.[^er61]

The 1962 show, the fifth, ran films of Indianapolis, Sebring, Le Mans, Daytona and the NHRA National Drags.[^er62][^ct62] In 1963 John Auble Jr., the Hartley papers' entertainment editor, previewed the sixth under the headline "Who Says 'Hot Rods' Are For Kids?" He bet "a set of spinner hub caps that there are more people who cringe at the words 'hot rod' than not," promised "More than 100 vehicles from a 15-state area,"[^er63a]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> and noted that part of the proceeds would go to Boys' Clubs.[^er63a]

Afterward he wrote that you could not leave without feeling "a bit guilty easing into your auto that still looks like it did when it left the Detroit assembly line." Show chairman Frank Clark, of Marina Drive, "probably still in high school," seemed to him to typify the boys and well-adjusted men who made "hot rodding" "no longer a term to be frowned on." Mike Keefe and Ron Adams of South Everett Street had fitted a late-model Chevrolet with closed-circuit TV and a record player. And the club display trophy went to a joke: the "Barkers" Hot Rod Club of Grove City, a division of Teens Unlimited, took a 1949 Buick, "stuck on" custom items like triple exhaust pipes, splashed paint over the mud and rust, and filled it with junk. "We were formed too late to make a real custom," said Paul Goebbel, one of the members. "So, we hit on this idea instead."[^er63b]

## "Just what goes on in a hot rod club?"

On the West Side, the clubs had to explain themselves. In the summer of 1960 Dave McLaughlin, 21, president of the High Winders, walked into the Hartley newspaper office: "I want you to tell your readers just what goes on in a hot rod club, how it functions and the good that they do." A club, he said, was "a group of fellows that meet together, usually once a week, and share each other's interest and knowledge about cars," and rodding "only where it's safe and authorized." The High Winders had seven members, all from the Hilltop, working in a small garage behind 2488 W. Broad Street. They were chartered with the NHRA, building a channeled 1932 Ford with a dropped rear axle, fixing up a 1934 Ford pickup as a club project, and hoping to get to the National Drag Races in Detroit. The youngest, Bill Hoit, was an 18-year-old senior at West High; the oldest, Hank Teeters, was 26.[^hilltop60]

The suspicion they were answering was real. In April 1959 the *West Side News* ran a plea, "Slow Down The Hot Rod Traffic," after a caller reported West High students racing down Olive Street "at a very high rate of speed and making no stops for cross streets"; police said that with two cruisers on duty the situation should be in hand.[^wsn59] On the East Side, in December 1961, Whitehall's council set fines as high as $50 for those who used the Great Eastern and Town and Country shopping-center lots "as drag strips, hot rod and go kart tracks after business places are closed."[^er61b]

The clubs kept at the safety side of the argument. In July 1962 inspectors from the Crusaders of the Lancaster Hot Rod Club, the Post Office and the Columbus chapter of the American Society of Safety Engineers ran a safety check in front of the Western Electric plant; 50 of the 230 cars inspected were rejected, most for lights.[^er62b]

## Open questions & disputed details

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Did the show's cars come from six states or fifteen?** The 1961 and 1963 stories say a "15-state area"; the club's own ads in 1962 and 1963 say "FROM 6 STATES" and "More than 100 cars from SIX STATES."[^er61][^er62][^er63a] The ads, which the club paid for, may be the safer count. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
