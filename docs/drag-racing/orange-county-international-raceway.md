# The Super Track That Outlasted Its Lease: Orange County International Raceway (Irvine, Calif., 1967–1983)

## "The Super Track"

When Orange County International Raceway opened to the public on August 5, 1967, it billed itself as "the Super Track." [Tom McEwen](../people/tom-mcewen.md), in the Guedel & Holland/National Automotive Specialties dragster, set the first track record that day, a 7.02, reached the final and red-lighted to Bobby Tapia in Larry Stellings' car. (John Guedel later set the track speed record, 223.88 mph, at a Division 7 points meet that year.) Future *National Dragster* editor Bill Holland, who fielded that Top Fueler with Guedel, remembered usherettes, rollers in the pits so cars could warm up, scoreboards and reaction timers that were new to the sport, and a tower "an impressive sight to behold." The rumor, he said, was that the builders had taken cues from Disneyland.[^nhra0831]

The track grew out of a partnership brokered by NHRA vice president Bernie Partridge. Larry Vaughn and Bill White had grown up on the 80,000-acre Irvine Ranch; Mike Jones, a former designer and draftsman then working as a racing-engine designer, came with financial backer Mike McKenna, a newspaper publisher and auto dealer. Jones had first tried to put drag racing at Anaheim's new Angels ballpark; that did not work. The Irvine Company had already turned down bids from Carroll Shelby and Jim Hall, and one from White alone. White (president), Vaughn (secretary/treasurer) and Jones (vice president and general manager) promised not just a quarter-mile and pits but, Burgess wrote, a facility "of unprecedented style and grace." They brought in five Orange County investors, including Chevrolet dealer Carroll Cone, investment banker David Brant, amusement-park owner Raymond Martin, and Irvine Company stockholders Mrs. Myford Irvine and Mrs. David Brant. The project was first budgeted at $80,000. The company granted a 55-year lease in east Irvine, "in the middle of a giant Y formed where the great Golden State and San Diego freeways meet."[^nhra0831]

The Orange County Planning Commission approved the plan on November 9, 1966; a joint press conference with NHRA and the Irvine Company followed the next day. Ground was broken on February 15, 1967, grading began March 13, and the first racing surface was poured on Monday, July 10. The total cost, Burgess wrote, was nearly $750,000. The track was 75 feet wide and 4,200 feet long, with a 300-foot sand trap beyond that, capped by water-filled plastic barriers. Jones hired Marines from the El Toro air base who knew crash and fire rescue. A distinctive 40-foot, three-story tower held offices, a timing and announcing deck and a top-floor suite. In the opening-season program Jones wrote, "If there were one basic premise on which Orange County International Raceway was founded, it is that, if the sport of auto racing is to enjoy continued growth and success, facilities must be provided that are commensurate with the professional status of the men and cars competing there."[^nhra0831]

## Funny Cars, taxes and a lame-duck lease

Early competition came from Lions, "just less than a half hour up the 405 Freeway in Wilmington," and from Irwindale. Jones leaned on Funny Cars: he developed the Manufacturers Meet and the idea of lining the floppers along the track before eliminations as a photograph. After Lions closed at the end of 1972, the gate improved, "but the track still struggled." In the first two and a half years, Burgess wrote, property taxes climbed by 400 percent, insurance premiums rose 15 percent in 1968 and 21 percent in 1969, and trash-collection fees doubled.[^nhra0831]

The track opened under NHRA sanction. After Jones left in July 1973 it signed a five-year pact with AHRA. AHRA president Jim Tice brought in C.J. "Pappy" Hart, the Lions and Santa Ana mainstay, as general manager for a short stay that included the track's first national event and clashes that led to Hart's resignation. Tice walked away during the 1974 season. Millionaire businessman, Pro Stock driver and Funny Car owner Larry Huff took over on June 1, 1974, still under AHRA, and dropped the track in March 1975. The long-term lease that could have run through 2022 returned to the Irvine Company. Vaughn, last of the original four founders still in, struck a five-year deal to keep leasing the land. Bill Doner and Steve Evans of International Raceway Parks brought the track back to NHRA; Burgess writes that drunkenness and rowdy crowds followed, spurred by rock concerts and an annual Fox Hunt that let women in bathing-suit attire in free. In the most notorious incident, a 24-year-old man died after being hit in the head with a beer bottle. Longtime starter Larry Sutton remembered "always way too many people and not enough security" and "legs all the way down the racetrack with people sitting on the guardrail."[^nhra0831]

By 1980 Doner wanted out. Partridge again stepped in, this time matching him with former Funny Car racer Charlie Allen, whose All-American Boy cars had run at OCIR from opening day and who had been trying to build a track in Fontana. "I wanted to return the track to more of a family atmosphere," Allen told Burgess. "We spent $300,000 to $400,000 on a quasifacelift." The next three seasons, Burgess wrote, were some of the track's best, with eight to ten major events a year, including the Manufacturers Meet, 64 Funny Cars and the Summer Showdown. Allen knew he had inherited a lame duck, "set to be roasted at the end of the 1982 season."[^nhra0831]

In early 1982 he started negotiating for extra time. He learned that the Irvine Company president answered every letter, so he got television, radio and the *Orange County Register* to tell fans to write. "At one point, he was getting 300 to 500 letters a day." The president called him in and asked, "Can you stop this thing?" Allen did, "and made them the good guys for letting us stay another year." Announcer Mike McClelland dated the end from early 1983, when the Alton Parkway bridge over Interstate 5 started rising out of the strawberry fields across the freeway. "That's when I knew it was over."[^nhra0831]

## The Last Drag Race

The final program was advertised for October 29, 1983, a Saturday. Racing ran well into October 30. "Racers praying not to get eliminated to earn another last shot at the fabled quarter-mile," Burgess wrote, "and fans hanging on into the wee hours of the morning like jilted lovers." More than 20 nitro Funny Cars showed up for an eight-car field; more than 300 bracket cars entered E.T. racing. Gary Beck in Top Fuel and Kenny Bernstein in Funny Car were the last professional winners. Beck's Top Fuel final was a solo, 5.59 at 245.23 mph, at 1:25 a.m., after Doug Kerhulas could not repair his car. Twenty minutes later Bernstein beat John Force, 6.13 to 6.22, after both lost traction. [Don Prudhomme](../people/don-prudhomme.md) had made the Funny Car field in the bump spot, a 6.11, and lost in round one to Billy Meyer's 5.89, low elapsed time of the meet for the floppers. Shirley Muldowney, who had won the World Finals at the same track two weeks earlier, lost to Beck, 6.06 to 5.73. McEwen did not make the last Funny Car show.[^nhra0829]

Unlike Lions' farewell, where fans ran wild after the Top Fuel final, E.T. racing continued for more than an hour after the professional finals. The last pair, McClelland remembered, went down the track sometime around 3 a.m. Allen told Burgess the anticlimax was the plan: he had heard rumors that people would tear the place apart for souvenirs, as they had at Lions, "so our plan was to outlast them." By the time it finished, "the 200 people who were still there were too tired to do anything. The stands were virtually empty." For the first time in more than 30 years, Burgess wrote, Southern California had no full-time dragstrip in operation.[^nhra0829]

Allen had already begun construction on [Firebird International Raceway](firebird-international-raceway-chandler.md) outside Phoenix in 1983. In return for not slowing the Irvine Company's development, he was allowed to take anything he could salvage. He looked into moving the tower and did not. "That success made me realize that I wanted to keep doing this, which is why I built Firebird Int'l Raceway." The tower stood more than a year before it came down. Someone put a plywood sign on the doors: "Yes, we are closed. It's all over."[^nhra0831]
