# "Our Name Is Mud": Washington's Suburbs Argue Over Drag Strips (1953–63)

*Teenagers asked for a place to race, an editorial called it foolishness, a father of three boys wrote back, and the racing went out to Manassas and Aquasco anyway*

## The hearing (1953)

On an October night in 1953 more than 75 Prince Georges County hot rodders met county officials to ask for a drag strip, "a stretch of roadway where they can race safely." Their argument was simple: young people were going to race regardless, so give them a place to do it without endangering anyone else. Their spokesman was Lorick Fox, a 29-year-old sixth-grade teacher at West Lanham Hills. "Our name is mud, right to begin with," he said. "You say 'hot rod,' and, b-r-other, you've had it."[^es531017]

The police chief, F. Allen Richards, was there, along with State Senator John Raymond Fletcher and an assistant state's attorney, Grover Small. Small was the only official who would commit himself: he thought a private racing strip "was a great idea." Two sites were suggested, both of them airstrips, one at Beltsville and the other at Greenbelt.[^es531017]

Within weeks the clubs and the officials had a formal body, the Prince Georges Automotive Safety Association, with Senator Fletcher as chairman and a county policeman, Pfc. Emmett F. G. Gray, as vice chairman. Two delegates came from each of five hot rod clubs. It wanted "a flat, clear area where youngsters could compete with their cars," planned to teach auto mechanics, and was the only way into the county police department's new traffic safety school. Among its backers was the mayor of Hyattsville, Jesse Baggett.[^es531207]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup> One of its clubs, Gents Roadster, Inc., had already run a "road-eo" in the Northwestern High School parking lot, testing drivers on parking in narrow spaces and keeping their wheels on painted lines.[^es531123]

## Across the river: Old Dominion

The strip came first in Virginia. In May 1954 Al Gore, director of Old Dominion Speedway at Manassas, announced he would "introduce hot rod competition Sunday at 1 p.m. on his new half-mile drag strip,"[^es540507] and by summer the [D.C. Dragons](dc-dragons-jo-ann-johnson.md) were running meets there. In March 1956 Gore said the strip would be lengthened to a mile, which "will allow for a quarter mile dragging rather than the present one-fifth mile."[^es560322]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> Old Dominion's strip stayed busy: a motorcycle scramble ran on it in December 1957;[^es571201] in May 1959 Joe Tucci of Quantico brought a new Chrysler-powered dragster to its "5th-mile drag strip" to take on the Alexandria Accelerators' Ford-powered record holder;[^es590513] a new eighth-mile strip opened in June 1960 alongside the old fifth-mile one, and that September the strip got new asphalt.[^es600605][^es600918] In 1963 the "fifth-of-a-mile straightaway" ran only stock and sportsman classes, as listed by the National Hot Rod Association.[^es630301]

## The argument (1956)

When Montgomery County hot rodders asked for a strip beside a proposed county airport in 1956, one resident called it "the most ridiculous action of all," and pictured "hundreds of happy hot rodders speeding joyously on a strip paid for by the taxpayers of Montgomery County."[^es560704] (The Dragons' president answered him; see [the Dragons](dc-dragons-jo-ann-johnson.md).)

That September the *Evening Star* weighed in. The International Association of Chiefs of Police had just resolved that drag racing, "even when officially supervised," might encourage speeding on the highways, and the paper agreed that such contests were "no substitute for the teaching of safe-driving practices." Its last line was blunt: "Why any city or county would subsidize such foolishness is hard to understand."[^es560917]

Five days later the letters page answered. Arthur E. Morrissette, the father of three teenage boys, listed what boys were condemned for, from hanging on the corner to smoking, and concluded: "Boys are condemned if they do anything, by the same people who did the same things or worse when they were teenagers... Let the boys try their skills where they can hurt only themselves or their own property." A second letter came from Tony Brisbin, past president of the Kam Krackers Hot Rod Club of San Antonio, Texas, who pointed out that California, "by far the leader of the drag program," had "almost as many 'strips' as the rest of the country put together."[^es560922] (San Antonio's own strips are in [San Antonio's Double Eagle](san-antonio-double-eagle-alamo-dragway.md).)

## Knights with business cards (1957)

The clubs kept working on their image. In February 1957 the *Star*'s Fifi Gorska profiled the Knights of Maryland, 21 members in black jackets with red and yellow shields who carried tools and gas cans to rescue stranded motorists. Afterward they handed over a card: "You have been assisted by a member of the Knights of Prince Georges County, Md." Unusually among the dozen or so clubs in the county, the Knights had girl members; their secretary was 16-year-old Jane Brady. A member who got any ticket, even a parking ticket, and did not report it was suspended. "One of the big aims of the Knights is to get a drag strip in Prince Georges County."[^es570217]

## Aquasco (1957–63)

Prince Georges never built one, but southern Maryland did. In May 1957 Aquasco Speedway, "38 miles from the District line" by way of Branch Avenue and the Brandywine turnoff, staged quarter-mile drags under the Automobile Timing Association of America; that November it opened the strip to inexperienced drivers with no entry fee.[^es570529][^es571117] By 1959 the Southern Maryland Timing Association ran the season,[^es590301] and in March 1960 the Sunday teen page showed "some 200 cars" entering the opening-day races "while 2,500 persons, mostly teen-agers, watch." The same page named the clubs: the Strokers of Maryland in red jackets, the Sleepers of Silver Spring in blue, the Dusters of Burtonsville, the Headers of Oxon Hill, the Gear Grinders of Hillside and the Prince Georges Kustoms.[^es600306] [Malcolm Durham](../people/malcolm-durham.md), newly arrived in Washington from North Carolina, ran his '56 Chevy at Aquasco with success before his match-race years.

In April 1963 Aquasco added 1,500 feet, widened the strip to 60 feet and paved the return road; the strip now measured 4,500 feet of "high-traction asphalt."[^es630425] Two weeks later Art Arfons of Akron brought his jet-powered "Green Monster" and ran the quarter mile in 8.4 seconds at 198 mph before a crowd of about 2,500. He said he had topped 200 on an untimed run.[^es630506]

## No (1963)

That same spring the county said no to a strip of its own. In March more than 50 residents of Cheltenham and Brandywine opposed Thomas J. La Roque's plan for a drag strip on 30 acres about half a mile south of Route 301, near the Boys Village; a state police sergeant, R. E. Stallings, testified that drag racing would encourage young drivers to break traffic laws, and the county commissioners voted unanimously to deny it.[^es630319] La Roque refiled for a $250,000 raceway and grandstand. After a four-hour hearing in May, Commissioner Jesse S. Baggett told him, "What you're doing is bringing in another typical nuisance that the people in that area won't derive any benefit from," and the board said no again.[^es630522] A week later Judge William B. Bowie refused to order a permit, agreeing with the county attorney, Robert B. Mathias, that "the county's right to issue a permit implies the right to deny it."[^es630529]

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **How long was Old Dominion's first drag strip?** Gore's 1954 announcement calls it a "new half-mile drag strip," and the *Star*'s July 1954 picture story says the Dragons raced to the quarter-mile mark on a half-mile strip.[^es540507][^dragons] But in 1956 Gore spoke of "the present one-fifth mile" of dragging, and the strip was still called a fifth of a mile in 1959 and 1963.[^es560322][^es590513][^es630301] The half mile may be the whole strip and the fifth the timed distance, or the layout may have changed; the 1956 plan for a quarter mile does not seem to have been carried out by 1963. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Was the Jesse Baggett who backed the 1953 safety association the same man who called a drag strip a "typical nuisance" in 1963?** The 1953 report lists "Mayor Jesse Baggett of Hyattsville" among the association's supporters;[^es531207] the 1963 report names "Commissioner Jesse S. Baggett."[^es630522] The name and county match, but we have not confirmed they are one person. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
