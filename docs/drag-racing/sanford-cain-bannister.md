# Barricades at the End of the Runway: Fred Cain, Fran Bannister and the Hurri-Cain (Wilmington, Mass., 1948–72)

*Sunday mornings on a borrowed runway, a tripod on a coupe's roof, and a dealership's Hemi*

    "Fran will always be the number one hot rod guy in New England."

    — Fred D. Cain, as reported by Larz F. Neilson, *Town Crier* (Wilmington, Mass.), Aug. 20, 2008.[^tc080820]

The Beverly Airport gave the hot rodders one runway, on Sunday mornings, from 6:00 to 8:30. That is how the Wilmington *Town Crier* told Fred D. Cain's story in 2008: he and a few friends raced a 1939 Ford coupe there in the late 1940s until "some motorcyclists decided to use another runway," and the permission was rescinded.[^tc080820]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup> His father, Fred F. Cain, was a partner in the Shawsheen Pines Airport off Hopkins Street, where they could use a hangar, but that runway "wasn't paved and it was too short for racing."[^tc080820]

So they went to Maine.

## Sanford (late 1940s–1960s)

Cain and his neighbor on Clark Street, Fran Bannister, asked the Sanford Airport for permission to hold one race: Bannister in his '32 roadster against Cain in the '39 coupe. They got one runway, "but had to erect barricades at the end to warn pilots not to land there." Bannister won. "The following Sunday, 30 racers showed up, and the drag races ran at Sanford for over 15 years." In time they raced on the airports at Orange, Mass., and Laconia, N.H., too.[^tc080820]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup> A 2007 feature in the same paper adds that the deal was struck with the airport and the New England Timing Association, and that the elder Cain used to fly from the Tew Mac Airport in Tewksbury to Sanford to watch his son race.[^tc070718]

Bannister was an Army veteran who had been a motor-pool mechanic.[^tc080820] The Audrain Automobile Museum, which has shown the restored roadster, describes its 1939 Ford truck grille and hand-made aluminum hood, says *Hot Rod* featured the car in March 1950, and says Fran and his brother Ralph built several notable cars together.[^audrain] According to the hot rod wiki *Kustomrama*, which cites a later book, the brothers built this one at the Boston trucking company where both worked as mechanics and finished it in 1949.[^kr]

## Behind the motel at Bonneville (1950)

Bannister kept Ford flathead blocks in his basement and set aside one with a particularly thick casting. He put it in Cain's coupe for a break-in, and Cain drove it to Wolfeboro, N.H., under orders not to go over 50. On the way home, at the top of a long hill, Cain floored it and "buried the speedometer at 120."[^tc080820]

In August 1950 they drove to the Bonneville Salt Flats, 2,500 miles each way with no interstates. Bannister drove the roadster with his wife Penny and their two-year-old son Paul and a stock flathead under the hood. Cain followed in the coupe with Bannister's racing engine in the back seat and "a tripod for changing engines" on the roof. At the salt, the *Town Crier* says, people were unimpressed by a nice roadster with a stock engine. "Then Bannister and Cain went out behind the motel, set up the tripod and changed engines." Bannister was clocked at 127.11 miles an hour.[^tc080820]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup> The Audrain Automobile Museum, which has shown the restored roadster, gives the engine as a 258-cubic-inch 1942 Mercury with Weiand heads and three Strombergs, and the speed as 127 mph, "officiated, and documented by the Southern California Timing Association."[^audrain] *Kustomrama*, citing *Hot Rod* of October 1950, says Bannister was the first to arrive on the salt and among the last to leave.[^kr]

## The Hurri-Cain (1964–72)

Fred F. Cain founded a Chrysler-Plymouth dealership in Wilmington and was elected a state representative in 1964. When Chrysler brought out the 426 Hemi that year, the dealership ordered a stripped-down 1964 Plymouth Belvedere with one, a Super Stock that Fred D. often drove, first at Sanford and then at New England Dragway in Epping, N.H. One Sunday in 1964 his brother Bob towed the car to Connecticut Dragway, the driver went missing at qualifying time, and Bob made the run himself and beat the regular driver's time. Harriet Cain and Fred F. Cain thought up the name Hurri-Cain.[^tc070718]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup> Dick Perreault came to work at Cain's in 1966 and became Bob's partner on the car.[^tc070718]

By September 1969 the dealership was advertising "FRED F. CAIN'S 725 H.-P. 'HURRI-CAIN'," a 1969 Barracuda funny car on injected gas with a 426 Hemi, best elapsed time 8.85 seconds at 152 miles an hour. Driver: Robert Cain. Mechanic: Dick Perreault. The ad claimed low E.T. and top speed at the AHRA Spring Nationals at Bristol, Tenn., strip records "at 10 of the 15 strips where the car has been run," races "from Canada to Miami, and as far west as Gary, Ind.," and a circuit-meet win the weekend before at Atco, N.J.[^tc690904]

In June 1972 Bobby Cain brought the car to the Swain School in Wilmington. On May 21, the *Town Crier* reported, he had won the World Cup Meet "in Sinair, Canada," at 214 miles an hour and 6.89 seconds.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-5"><a href="#q-5" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup> He and his mechanic, spelled "Dick Perrault" in that story, showed the car to every class in turn. "They even started the car, and drove it around a little, for the youngsters."[^tc720601]

Fran Bannister worked for Fred Cain for many years and retired to Georgia. In 1990, the 2008 feature says, he went back to the salt at 70 and ran 112.199.[^tc080820]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-6"><a href="#q-6" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup> Fred Cain went into the Tyrods New England Racers and Hot Rodders Hall of Fame in July 2008, a year after Bob Cain went into New England Dragway's. The feature that reported it did not end on the strip. Fred's high-speed antics, it said, were "not limited to dry land," and he had many stories to tell, "including racing his boat at 175 m.p.h."[^tc080820]

For a runway fight that went the other way, see [Tear Gas on the Runways (Pennsauken, N.J.)](pennsauken-central-airport-1958.md).<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-7"><a href="#q-7" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup>

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **A '39 or a '40 Ford?** The 2008 feature says Fred Cain raced a 1939 Ford coupe at Beverly, and its photo caption shows "Fred Cain's '39 Ford coupe."[^tc080820] The 2007 feature says the story began with "a '40 Ford coupe with a flathead V8" that Fred Cain and Fran Bannister ran at the Beverly Airport in 1948.[^tc070718] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **One race, then 15 years?** The one-race permission, the barricades, Bannister's win, the 30 racers the next Sunday and the "over 15 years" all come from Fred Cain's account in the 2008 feature.[^tc080820] The 2007 feature confirms the Sanford runway and the barriers but gives no dates.[^tc070718] We have not found a 1940s or 1950s Maine paper on the Sanford drags. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **A 40-year record?** The *Town Crier* says the 127.11 set "a record for Ford flathead engines that lasted for 40 years," and that a Peabody man tried three times to break it.[^tc080820] The museum page and *Kustomrama* give 127 mph but do not call it a record, and the museum lists the engine as a 1942 Mercury.[^audrain][^kr] We have not seen the 1950 SCTA results. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** **Which car was the first Hurri-Cain?** The 2007 feature's text says the name "was first used on the 1965 car," while its photo caption calls the 1964 Belvedere "the very first 'Hurri-Cain'."[^tc070718] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-5" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** **Where is "Sinair"?** The page image prints "Sinair, Canada" and the date as May 21st. We have not identified the strip or found the meet's results. Sanair, at Saint-Pie, Que., is a plausible reading, but nothing we have read confirms it.[^tc720601] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-5" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-6" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **112 or 128?** The 2008 *Town Crier* says that in 1990, at age 70, Bannister reprised the run at 112.199.[^tc080820] The Audrain museum says that "nearly 40-years later" he drove his friend Jack Hartney's roadster at Bonneville and turned 128 mph, one mile an hour faster than in 1950.[^audrain] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-6" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
