# A Watermelon Farmer's Dragster: Melvin Heath, the Fairgrounds Nationals and Oklahoma's Hometown Strips (1954–1966)

*A national champion from a peanut-and-watermelon farm, an engine from a barn in Marlow, and the Nationals on a strip the Jaycees built*

!!! quote "In their own words"

    — connerj, on Melvin Heath's dragster, in a H.A.M.B. thread of 1957 Oklahoma City photos.[^hambokc]

Rush Springs farmer Melvin Heath won the 1956 Nationals with a homebuilt dragster and became NHRA's second national champion. A family post says George Woolever built the engine in a barn in Marlow. The next two Nationals ran on a drag strip the Jaycees built at the Oklahoma City fairgrounds. Around that, Oklahoma raced an airstrip near Jet, a dusty road at Ardmore, and a Sunday runway.

## The holeshot farmer from Rush Springs

Melvin Heath farmed his whole life on the family place at Rush Springs, in Grady County, raising "everything from peanuts, alfalfa, watermelons, and wheat to cattle." He had an animal science degree from Oklahoma State and, his family says, sat for more than 50 years on the Grady County Soil Conservation Board. He also "had an ingenious mind and a unique ability to take ideas and make them into practical machinery to use on his farm."[^heath]

Those skills went into a dragster. It first appeared in 1954 at the first Drag Safari meet in Caddo Mills, Texas, where Heath won Open Gas and was runner-up in Top Eliminator. Then Heath and his wife, Betty, "spent the next three months improving their race car," a 331-cubic-inch Chrysler Hemi with six Stromberg carburetors on nitro. After a disappointing 1955 Nationals at Great Bend, it all came together in 1956 at the Nationals in Kansas City (see [Kansas City Timing Association](kansas-city-timing-association.md)). Heath, "a known holeshot artist," beat Don Little wire to wire with a 10.49 and became NHRA's second national champion.[^heath] The car is in the Wally Parks NHRA Motorsports Museum in Pomona. Heath died on Feb. 17, 2025, at 94, and his services were in Rush Springs.[^heath]

NHRA calls it a "homebuilt dragster."[^heath] Woolever's great-nephew passes on a family story that puts more hands on it. The forum member connerj wrote that "my great uncle george woolever built the engine in it in his barn in marlow, ok," and that Woolever raced a 1941 Lincoln Continental of his own.[^hambokc]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup> Only that post says who built the engine. A Tulsa racer who had lined up against that Lincoln answered that Woolever "had leave down pat way before anybody else," and that losing to him was "when i learned the the differnce between driver reaction time and car reaction time."[^hambokc]

## The Nationals at the fairgrounds, 1957–58

Oklahoma City's new state fairgrounds, on the old Sandlot ballpark land west of May Avenue, held its first fair on Sept. 25, 1954. Built with it were grandstands, an oval track and, just south, "a drag strip which hosted the National Championship Drag Races in 1957-1958."[^fatokie] *Hot Rod* gave the credit to the town's young businessmen. The strip, 3,800 feet long, "was built by the Jaycees and is operated as their major project of the year," with the junior chamber of commerce and the Okla City Timing Association as hosts. It sat "only two miles from the center of Oklahoma City."[^hr58]

The cover of the 1957 program dates the Third Annual NHRA National Championship Drags to Aug. 30 through Sept. 2, 1957.[^ahrf] NHRA's history praises the "ample paved pit areas, separate spectator and participant parking, a cafeteria, and more," and counts 450 entrants despite that year's new fuel ban. *Hot Rod* counted 465 cars and some 40,000 people over four days.[^firstnat][^hr58]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> The two counts do not agree. Buddy Sampson won in the Money Oldsmobile Special with a 10.42. In 1958 entries were capped at 500 and filled a month early. Ted Cyr won Top Eliminator at 10.04 in his spare car, a blue Chrysler dragster with "For Sale" lettered on its nose in shoe polish.[^firstnat]

The people in the stands remembered other things. "Was pretty much like heaven to a 17 year old kid," one forum member wrote of 1957. Another recalled that the Texas fuel racer Bobby Langley won an award for the best-appearing car and crew, in uniforms his wife, Ruth Ann, had made.[^hambokc] (Langley also raced at [Green Valley Raceway](green-valley-raceway-smithfield.md) and [Houston's Freeway Dragstrip](houston-freeway-dragstrip.md).) Bill Honea, then 23, got the job of driving Paul Grimmett's 1939 Simca with a 1951 Cadillac engine in 1958. "when everyone else tried it and couldn't make it go, I finally got up enough nerve to try it." He got it to 120 mph and 11.20 in A/C.[^dslok] And one member's 2016 question is still on the thread. Their father, Bill Mangum, raced a '34 Ford at Oklahoma City in 1957 and won trophies, but "my family has no pictures left." The only reply so far is another member's offer to ask his own dad.[^hambokc]

Some came a very long way. In late August 1958 Bruce Olson flat-towed his Hemi-powered 1932 Ford coupe from Beach, North Dakota, to the fourth Nationals, and ran 13.40 at 107 mph in B Altered Coupe.[^olsonok] From Walker Air Force Base in Roswell, New Mexico, the president of the base's Duster's Hot Rod Club, Larry Sanchez, raced "at all the local strips and Oklahoma City Nationals," a club officer remembered, without saying which year.[^dslnmok] (Their home scenes: [North Dakota](north-dakota-minot-borrowed-strips.md) and [New Mexico](new-mexico-timing-association-albuquerque.md).)

## Airstrips, pastures and a wet-down starting line

Most Oklahoma racing happened far from the Nationals. Up in Enid, a member posted a club's clippings in 2017: the Playboys Rod and Custom Club, with the message "if you think hot rodding in the fifties was limited to the west coast check it out." Its 1957 photos came from Kagelmann, "a WWII era air strip by the Great Salt Plains near Jet OK." The poster, G V Gordon, grew up a mile south of Vance Air Force Base and rode his bike to the drags there, where he saw Eddie Hill's twin-engine Pontiac dragster run.[^hambenid]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup> The clippings give no race dates. Enid used-car dealer Jim Lewis, his obituary says, spent his weekends "drag racing with friends just outside the Enid city limits" in '55–'57 Chevys and early '60s Corvettes and won dozens of trophies. In the 1960s and 1970s he was "one of very few dealers in Oklahoma that sold high performance muscle and collector cars."[^lewis]

To the south, Ardmore's first strip ran on Lake Murray Drive, where the city's airport is now. Monroe Cameron's first memory of it is 1957. "only the starting line was paved," and his best friend's dad "sprayed water on the strip to keep the dust down." When the strip later moved to Springer, where Cameron first saw racing in 1963, its owner was Art Shaw, who had an automotive shop on Broadway.[^dslok]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup> On when it opened, there is one recollection, from 1957. In the early 1960s Rob Ritter raced on the runway at Clinton-Sherman Air Force Base at Burns Flat, "a Sunday event, 1/4 mile with plenty of shut down room," which he thinks was just called the Burns Flat Drags. In Tulsa, Duane Walker ran a Corvair Monza at Tulsa North Airport, where there was no light system, "just a person with a flag."[^dslok] A pit pass and a *Duncan Banner* advertisement place a race at Duncan on Sunday, Nov. 6, 1960.[^dslok]

By August 1966, when Willow Run opened on the east side of Oklahoma City while still under construction, the touring stars were coming to Oklahoma. Doug Waggoner remembers Eddie Hill, Mike Sorokin with the Surfers, Bobby Langley and Gene Snow on opening day.[^dslok] For the teenagers in Tulsa, though, the street was still where it started. See [The Restless Ribbon](../places/brookside-restless-ribbon-tulsa.md).

A watermelon farmer from Rush Springs became NHRA's second national champion, and the engine, on one family post, came out of a barn in Marlow. The next two Nationals ran on the strip the Jaycees built. The rest of the racing was an airstrip, a dusty road, and a Sunday runway.
