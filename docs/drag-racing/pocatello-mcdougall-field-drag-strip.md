# The Ace's Runway: McDougall Field and the Pocatello Drag Strip (Idaho, 1928–1990s)

*An airport named for a hometown ace, a runway the interstate cut in half, and a timing-tower clerk who said she would take a racer's keys*

From 1928, the field Pocatello named for a hometown ace was the airport, and then the drag strip. The interstate cut the runway in half. A clerk in the timing tower told a racer she would take his keys. The sources still do not agree when it closed: 1973, about 1993, or 1999.

## An airport for an ace

In 1928 Pocatello bought a piece of land near what is now the Simplot plant, already in use as an informal landing field, and county tractors leveled the sagebrush that spring. The field wasn't finished, or dedicated, when Harry Owens McDougall came home that November for an Armistice Day air show. He was 32, a local boy who lived in Boise by then, Idaho's only flying ace of the Great War, with the Distinguished Flying Cross and the Croix de Guerre. During an air race two miles west of town his plane went into a tailspin and nose-dived into the ground, killing him and his 20-year-old sister-in-law, Elda Rice. The town renamed its airport for him. Local historian Justin Smith dates the official dedication of McDougall Field to Jan. 1, 1929. A commemorative postmark on the abandoned-airfields site reads Nov. 11, 1929.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>[^isj20][^af]

The airport grew through the 1930s and the war: a paved east-west runway by 1941, three paved runways at its peak, a terminal with a restaurant downstairs and the control tower upstairs. Then the Army's much bigger Pocatello air base nearby went quiet after the war, and on Feb. 20, 1949, it was conveyed to the city to replace McDougall. McDougall Field disappeared from the aeronautical charts that year.[^isj20][^af]

## The runway that stayed

The interstate came through next. By 1969 Interstate 86 ran right through the middle of the old airfield, and the north ends of two runways had gone back to farming. The western half of the east-west runway was the only part left, and the 1971 USGS topographic map labeled it "Drag Strip."[^af] "McDougall's east-west runway remained and was converted into a dragstrip that operated for many years just south of the freeway," Smith wrote in the *Idaho State Journal*. The old tower building lived on for a while as the Pilot House restaurant.[^isj20]

The Firebird Raceway nomination to the National Register, written with the New family's records, says that by 1960 Idaho's racers had two places to go, Gowen Field Road in Boise and the Pocatello Drag Strip, and that from 1954 to 1973 racers on the eastern side of the state "would race along the municipal airway strip in Pocatello." Races ran there now and then "until the strip needed too much repair." Drag racing historian Bret Kepner remembered it as Intermountain Dragway: it "hosted several large events in its life span," but by the end it had an "extremely rough deteriorating surface."[^nrhp][^af]

## The Safari comes to town

The big events started early. In 1954 the NHRA announced a Safety Safari stop at Pocatello, and Boise's [Bootleggers](firebird-raceway-idaho-bootleggers.md) drove across the state for it. Bill New missed out: a couple of days before the trip a garbage truck hauled his new old-stock hood off to the dump, and Bud Coons wouldn't pass his deuce at tech without one. The meet made *Hot Rod* magazine. Roger Worley of Boise's Gear Jammers ran the W.A.S. roadster with Amyx and Stevens at "the original NHRA Safety Safari meets in Pocatello (circa 1954 and 1955)," and the nomination calls the 1954 and 1955 meets Idaho's first major drag races at a site set up for the quarter mile.[^fb20][^worley][^nrhp] Both the nomination and the family history put those meets on "an abandoned air base." McDougall had closed in 1949. The Army base was conveyed to the city on Feb. 20, 1949, to replace it, and Smith and Pearson both describe that field as the city airport before those meets, so an abandoned McDougall is the guess, not a sourced fact.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup>[^isj20][^af]

## The girl in the tower

By 1967 the strip had a timing tower and Kathy Steinmetz, then in her early twenties, was working in it. Al Steinmetz had "the fastest car out there," she said, but kept missing third gear, so she told him that if he missed another one she would take his keys. "He did and brought me the keys and we won over thirty trophies that year and then we got married." She did most of her winning in F/Stock in Al's '67 Olds 442, won D/Stock at the 1976 Winternationals, and over a career from 1967 to 1995 set five NHRA national records. "Race tracks can be romantic places," she said.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup>[^wdr] She told it again, in her own words, on *Drag Strip List*: she was working in the tower for Jim Sterling when she met Al, and after he handed over his keys, "we won over thirty trophies that year and then we got married. So race tracks can be romantic places."[^dslid]

## Lights, sand and grass in the joints

The racers' own memories fill in the years the official histories skip. Mel Bashore was 16 in the summer of 1962, when his family was living in Pocatello and he heard about an NHRA regional race. With the family's one car taken, he rode his bike out to the strip "west of the city at the old airport," past a lot of empty space and a few industries, and sat on small bleachers. Steve Thomas, who had special-ordered one of the first 1967 Camaros through Caldwell's Burns Chevrolet, "won stock eliminator at Pocatello Dragway in 1967."[^dslid]

Kathy Steinmetz's photographs, captioned on the same page, date the changes. The first Christmas tree went in at Pocatello Dragway in 1966, with the timing crew working off the back of a flat-bed truck: "We had to count the times the clock went around to see how fast they went." In 1970 bleachers came over to Intermountain Raceway from the old Bannock County Fairgrounds. Jim Hennessey bought the drag strip in 1973 and installed lights for night racing in 1976, and on May 12, 1974, funny cars came to town: Ed "The Ace" McCulloch's "Revellution" Dodge Dart against Twig Zeigler's "Pizza Haven" Plymouth, in a match race at Intermountain Raceway.[^dslid]

The surface told its age. Vincent Hinman warned of "the low spot in the right hand lane that filled up with fine sand from the Simplot tailing pile across the street," where he saw the Wild Turkey, a '40, catch air under both slicks. Dave Lindorff came in about 1977 with his brother's Vega, got bad directions from the locals, and found grass growing in every expansion joint of the old concrete runway: "a patchwork of concrete and grass for the actual drag strip."[^dslid]

## Lease's end

How it ended depends on whom you ask.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> The nomination says that in 1973 the city "declined to renew the lease for the strip due to other environmental concerns," and Hemmings' 2019 story repeats that Pocatello "operated until 1973 based on a lease from that city." Robert Pearson, who grew up in Pocatello and walked the old field in 2002, remembered racing there much later: "The airport was used as a drag strip for several years but about 10 years ago that operation was shut down when FMC (the big factory just to the south) bought the property." Kepner agreed that the strip was purchased and was so worn by then that "it wouldn't have been around much longer, anyway."[^nrhp][^hemm19][^af] Kathy Steinmetz, who raced there from 1967, sold her car and became the announcer for Ed Jones in 1995, put the end later still: she announced "until the track closed in 1999." Her captions also have Hennessey buying the strip, not losing it, in 1973.[^dslid]

The nomination says the owner of Sage Raceway, the eighth-mile strip in Idaho Falls, "grew up racing on the track in Pocatello, and after realizing it had closed decided to open his own dragstrip." Satellite pictures still show the hangar foundations and slightly more than half of the old runway south of I-86, west of U.S. 30.[^nrhp][^af][^isj20]

McDougall came home before the field was finished, and the town named the airport for him. The runway became the strip, the interstate cut it, and a racer brought his keys to the clerk in the tower. The sources still do not agree when it closed.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Did the Pocatello strip close in 1973, about 1993, or 1999?** The Firebird nomination (2018) and Hemmings (2019), which follows it, say the city declined to renew the lease in 1973. Robert Pearson, writing in 2003, said the operation "was shut down" about ten years earlier, when FMC bought the property, and Bret Kepner also ties the end to the purchase. The two may be talking about different strips, or racing may have resumed on the runway under another operator ("Intermountain Dragway") after 1973. Kathy Steinmetz, who announced there from 1995, says she did so "until the track closed in 1999," and her photograph captions have Jim Hennessey buying the strip in 1973 and adding lights in 1976, which fits a change of hands that year better than a closing. Pocatello city lease records and the *Idaho State Journal* would settle it.[^nrhp][^hemm19][^af][^dslid] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Which "abandoned air base" hosted the 1954 and 1955 Safety Safari meets?** The nomination says "an abandoned air base west of Pocatello" and the Firebird family history says "the abandoned air base." McDougall Field had closed in 1949. No source states 1951. Smith says the Army base "was conveyed to the city of Pocatello to replace McDougall Field" on Feb. 20, 1949, and that "within two years the Pocatello Municipal Airport began operation" there; Pearson says Hayfield "shut down in 1952 when the Pocatello Airbase became the city airport." By either account the Army field was the city airport before the Safari, so an abandoned McDougall is our best guess, not a sourced fact. Freeman's McDougall Field entry places that Army airfield "to the west"; Smith places the base "two miles to the east" of McDougall. *Hot Rod*'s 1954 coverage should name the site.[^nrhp][^fb20][^isj20][^af] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Did Kathy Steinmetz meet Al Steinmetz in the Pocatello timing tower in 1967?** She tells the tower story herself on *Drag Strip List* (working for Jim Sterling, the keys, thirty trophies, the wedding) but gives no year; the 1967 date and the records come from one compiled biography site. An NHRA records list or an Idaho newspaper wedding notice would confirm them.[^wdr][^dslid] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When was McDougall Field dedicated?** Justin Smith: "On Jan. 1, 1929, McDougall Field was officially dedicated and open for business." The abandoned-airfields site shows a postmark that "commemorated the 11/11/29 Dedication of McDougall Field," on the first anniversary of McDougall's death. There may have been an opening and a later ceremony.[^isj20][^af] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
