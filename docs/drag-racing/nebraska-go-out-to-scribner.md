# "Go Out to Scribner": Nebraska Hot Rodders and the Long Wait for a Sanctioned Strip (Lincoln, Neb., 1951–59)

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> daily newspapers (*The Lincoln Star* and the *Lincoln Evening Journal*, Lincoln, Neb.): news briefs, a sports-page feature, safety council and club notices and display ads, 1951–73, read as scanned page OCR, with the 1956 and 1958 stories checked against the page images; a magazine company history and an NHRA column for background · **Last verified:** 2026-10-22 (Round 22-West) · **Open issues:** whether the 1956 "hot rod parts dealer" Bill Smith is Speedway Motors' founder ◐; whether a strip at Arrow Airport ever opened ? · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "Tell them not to drive junkers and if they want to drag race go out to Scribner and be careful of kids."

    — An Omaha teenager, after a street chase that killed a girl in front of her home, *The Lincoln Star*, Nov. 10, 1954.[^ls541110]

In the early 1950s a Nebraska teenager who wanted to find out how fast his car was had three choices. He could go to a fairgrounds oval, drive to an old air base at Scribner, or try it on O Street. The Lincoln papers recorded all three. They also recorded the long effort by the city's clubs to give him a fourth: a sanctioned strip near home.

## Hot rods on the fairgrounds oval (1951)

In the Lincoln papers of 1951, "hot rod racing" meant racing on a circle track. That July, at the Adams County fairgrounds in Hastings, Bob Rager of Lincoln won his fourth straight trophy dash on the "hot rod race card" before a crowd of 2,500.[^lej510705] In August a Hot Rod Racing association filed incorporation papers with the secretary of state. Six Nebraskans and a Kansan signed them, with M. C. Kent of Hastings as resident agent. Its stated purpose was "the encouragement and promotion of the racing of automobiles specifically designed for that purpose on established race tracks in the state of Nebraska."[^lej510818] Nothing in it mentions straight-line racing.

## "A term I learned from my son" (1954)

Drag racing arrived as a new word. In April 1954 Gov. Robert Crosby suggested that more Nebraska towns set aside streets or roads for "drag races—a term I learned from my son."[^ls540426] A month later a display ad in the *Star* announced "DRAG RACES Monday, May 31 SCRIBNER AIR-BASE."[^ls540529] That summer the NHRA's traveling Drag Safari, led by former Pomona police sergeant Bud Coons, put Scribner, Neb., on its ten-stop 1954 tour.[^nhra2015]

In November an Omaha street chase ended with a car striking and killing Barbara Jean Rukas in front of her home. One of the two teenage drivers told reporters he had been racing "with the kid," and he gave the advice quoted above. The paper added one line of its own: "Organized drag races are held at Scribner, Neb."[^ls541110]

## Talk at City Hall, and a race out O Street (1956)

In April 1956 Lincoln Police Capt. Orrin Graves called a meeting at City Hall to discuss how drag racing could be taken off the city streets. The speaker was Jack Clark of Omaha, an NHRA representative: "A lot of people who say hot rodding on a drag strip is dangerous and foolish are just ignorant." Clark described what a strip needed: a quarter-mile of straight hard-surfaced road, preferably 60 feet wide, with timing traps 132 feet apart. Under the clubs' bylaws, he said, members with moving violations on city streets were suspended from the strip. Bruce Seamark, who owned a hot rod, said he had counted about 50 drag races in a two-hour watch at 16th and O. Bill Smith, a "local hot rod parts dealer," said "there has been a lot of constant talk about need for a drag strip."[^ls560420]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>

That fall there was racing, though not yet on a sanctioned strip. The Nemaha Valley Timing Association announced regional drag championships for Sunday, Oct. 7, on a strip "four miles south of Verdon," near Falls City.[^ls561004] Two weeks later an ad in the *Star* read: "DRAG RACES 1 PM Sun., Oct. 21 18 Miles West on O Street … Sponsored by: ECCENTRICS OF LINCOLN." At the bottom it said: "Ad donated by SPEEDWAY MOTORS 1719 N."[^ls561020] Speedway Motors had been founded in Lincoln in 1952 by "Speedy" Bill Smith and moved in 1954 to 1719 N Street, the address in the ad.[^hrspeedway]

## Seventy-five dragsters at the Muny Airport (1958)

By October 1958 the races had a regular home of sorts, at Lincoln's Municipal Airport. The *Star* sent David Clark to a Sunday meet, run by Jim Hennessey of the Rodders of Havelock with the Lincoln Air Force Base Auto Club. He counted "75 or so dragsters" and "nearly 1,000 spectators." Most of the cars were stock cars, "just like the kind driven by" the crowd. "The races are run according to National Hot Rod Assn. rules," and crews checked every car. Hennessey explained the stock-car rules: "The car must have a stock exhaust, hub caps off, windows rolled up, and the driver must wear a crash helmet if the car is a convertible."[^ls581027]

Whitey Bolte, an Omaha truck driver with 24 trophies in two years, explained why he came: "You get the urge, no matter what kind of car you have, to see if you can beat somebody else." He added: "I can turn 101 in a quarter of a mile. In town that would be over 100 in three blocks. It's better out here." Major Dean Kirby, the Air Force sponsor, ran his own full racer at 121 mph. He gave the history: "We started holding drag races in February of 1957. We wanted to cut down traffic accidents. And we did. Then we had to stop for 6 months and the accident rate rose. So we're getting started again." Hennessey's verdict: "Grudge racing out here is the best thing to keep racing off the streets."[^ls581027]

## The timing association makes its case (1958–59)

Four days later the city's clubs made their case to the Lincoln-Lancaster Safety Council's counseling board, and their spokesman was a young Lincoln man who would soon make a national career of it. "Richard Wells, Lincoln Timing Association president," had been booked to speak on "The Drag Strip Proposal."[^ls581029] He told the board that a sanctioned strip meant a close safety check of the cars, supervision of the drivers and complete insurance at modest cost, while "outlaw" strips gave the public the wrong impression. Wells said Kansas City had the nearest sanctioned strip (see [the Kansas City Timing Association](kansas-city-timing-association.md)). "Lincoln now has 7 auto clubs with 175 members," he said, and membership could grow a good deal more if a sanctioned strip could be provided. Facilities at Arrow Airport might be used if the money could be found for the improvements NHRA approval required.[^ls581031]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup> Wells later left Lincoln to edit NHRA's *National Dragster* (see [Dick Wells](../people/dick-wells.md)).

The timing association spent the next year proving it was a civic group. A month after the hearing, a steering committee for a new go-cart club was put "under the direction of Dick Wells of the Lincoln Timing Assn."[^ls581129] In 1959 the association co-sponsored the city's vehicle safety check[^ls590523] and sponsored an auto show at the Fairgrounds Coliseum with "affiliated clubs in the region."[^ls590926] In January 1960 its members escorted Mothers March volunteers to the outlying parts of the city.[^ls600129]

Elsewhere in the state, local groups were already running drags. When a new stretch of Highway 10 opened near Minden in November 1958, "drag races were featured, sponsored by the Franklin Junior Chamber of Commerce."[^ls581126] In September 1959, 101 cars and a crowd of 1,400 came to the Jaycee Timing Association drags at Grand Island, where Don Moss of Omaha won top eliminator at 135.73 mph.[^ls590915]

By 1968 Omaha had a dragway of its own, and Jim Davis, "longtime president of the Missouri Valley Timing Association," became its manager.[^ls680210] By 1973 a sanctioned strip near Millard was drawing hundreds of spectators every summer.[^ls730531] In December 1971 the *Star* ran a brief headed "Wells President": a "former Lincolnite" named Wells had been "named president of the National Street Rod Association (NSRA)" and had "helped organize and produced the 1971 Street Rod Nationals in Memphis." The brief gives no first name. Reading it as Dick Wells is our inference from the Lincoln tie and the Nationals; see [Dick Wells](../people/dick-wells.md#q-3).[^ls711205]
