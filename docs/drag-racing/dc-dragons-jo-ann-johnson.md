# Chewing Her Nails in the Kitchen: Jo Ann Johnson and the D.C. Dragons (1951–58)

*A Washington hot rod club met in a police station, fined its own speeders, and took 45 minutes to decide whether a girl could join*

## "What we really need is a drag strip!"

In the spring of 1952 the *Evening Star* still had to explain to its readers what a drag race was: "two hopped-up stock cars being flagged off from a slow-rolling start down a straightaway course of measured length." The Arlington and Washington Hot Rod Club was racing at the Hagerstown Speedway in western Maryland.[^es520504]

A year later the paper's teenage columnist, Sharon Doran, took up the cause. About 1,000 teenagers in the Washington area were driving hot rods, she reported, and to belong to one of the clubs (she named "The Strokers," "The Shifters" and "The Draggins") you had to be an extremely safe, licensed driver; a ticket for speeding or reckless driving meant automatic suspension. "What we really need is a drag strip!" said three high school fans, Ray Bailey, James Mattia and Bruce Boxwell. Even the American Automobile Association, while "not in favor of hot rods, peeling or drag races," allowed that "it might be a good idea if the teenagers could have a drag run."[^es530828]

Readers wrote back. John Reistrup, 17, secretary of the "Timers" of Riverdale, Maryland, wrote that the Dragons had "the reputation of being the most able bunch of bull-throwers ever gathered together in one bunch," but agreed on one thing: "every hot rodder wants a drag strip." Then the Dragons' secretary, Jo Ann Johnson, answered for her club: "We, the Dragons, D. C., are having a campaign to obtain a drag strip. Our main problem is getting people to realize that our aims are constructive not destructive." The club wanted the strip, she wrote, "for ourselves and others who, as youths of a community, need help."[^es531002]

## Voted in

In July 1954 the *Star*'s Sunday picture magazine sent a reporter, Meredith S. Buel, to meet her. Jo Ann Johnson, 18, of 5616 Roosevelt Street, Bethesda, a former University of Maryland student and a tag-and-title clerk for a Washington car dealer, "was the first girl voted into a local hot-rod club known as the 'D. C. Dragons.'" Its members came from Washington, Maryland and Virginia and ranged from 16 to 25.[^es540704]

She had started two years earlier, asking questions of her next-door neighbor Al Smith, a Dragon, while he worked on his car, until one day he said, "Here, start cleaning these valves." "Now," she said, "I know how to adjust brakes and clutches, and to channel and chop." When she asked to join, the members "debated 45 minutes before voting her in—'while I sat in the kitchen, chewing my nails.'" A year later the club president, Arnold Stein, a 20-year-old pre-dental student at Maryland, said she had proved one of the most conscientious members. She was shopping a junkyard for an eight-cylinder engine for her engineless 1932 coupe. Two more young women had since joined: Jane Heater, 18, a recent Anacostia High graduate and now the club secretary, who was building up a $40 1939 coupe, and Charlotte Alderton, 19, a dairy clerk, who planned to build a car with Jo Ann.[^es540704]

Hot rodding, Jo Ann told the reporter, was "a supervised sport stressing mechanical mastery in a race against time," not a grim game of tearing through communities "peeling the wheels and fish tailing."[^es540704]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup>

## The club as jury

The Dragons met in the Bethesda police station and ran on the honor system. A member who got a traffic ticket reported it to the club, "which acts as a jury"; he was usually fined the amount of the ticket and could be sent to traffic school, and frequent violators were expelled. A Dragon was pledged to help motorists with car trouble.[^es540704] A St. Albans junior, Bill Ellis, put the rule more simply that November: the club "doesn't allow 'squirreling around'... and they fine you if you are caught speeding, the same fine as your ticket."[^es541126]

## A strip near Manassas

By the summer of 1954 the campaign had paid off, at least across the river. "The club recently acquired a half-mile drag strip near Manassas, Va.," the picture magazine reported; two cars raced from a standing start to the quarter-mile mark and used the rest to slow down. Dragons checked every car before it ran: in one photograph Jo Ann checks a tire while Jane Heater fills out the inspection list, and Charlotte Alderton takes information from a driver, Bernie Linder of Alexandria. Arnold Stein had thrown the races open to everyone, with a special invitation to the street racers: "We won't race those teen-age, driver maniacs when they pull up alongside on a street. We ask them to come down to the track where we show them what a true hot rod is like."[^es540704]

That November the Dragons sponsored a full day of drag races for the Eastern championships at Old Dominion Speedway, Manassas, under a sanction from the Automobile Timing Association of America, with entries expected from Virginia, New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Maryland and Washington. The favorite was Jim Schulties of Washington, who held the course record of 10.3 seconds in a 1932 Ford coupe.[^es541119] (The Old Dominion strip's own story, and the suburbs' long argument over drag strips, is told in ["Our Name Is Mud"](washington-suburbs-drag-strip-debate.md).)

## "To get racing off the streets"

In June 1956 about 100 organized hot rodders, led by John B. Raffa of 8500 Garfield Street, Bethesda, told the Montgomery County Council they backed a drag strip to be built with the county's proposed airport.[^es560614]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup> When a letter writer called the strip the "most ridiculous action of all,"[^es560704] Raffa, the Dragons' president and a Maryland student, answered in the *Star*: "Our club has been in existence for nearly five years and it was through us that the proposed program for a drag strip in Montgomery County was begun." The club had met many times with Forest Gustafson of the county recreation board, and the strip would be self-supporting. Racing on the streets, he wrote, was "our biggest fight and also the main reason we formed in the first place; that is, to get racing off the streets and run in an organized manner on a safe, off street strip."[^es560712]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Single source: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup>

The Dragons kept turning up. In September 1957 they joined the Metropolitan Auto Racing Fans Club at the Guy Mason Recreation Center to hear Johnny Parsons of Indianapolis fame,[^es570922] and at the 1958 Rod and Custom Show a Bethesda-Chevy Chase senior, Blake Thomas, "member of the Dragons D. C. Hot Rod Club," posed for the *Star*'s photographer.[^es580323]
