# Drag $1.00, Stag 75¢: Palmer, Alaska, Hunts for a Strip (1958–64)

*A Matanuska Valley weekly follows one small town's year of runway plans, rival sites, stock pledges and road races, and the private strip that opened in the trees the next summer*

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "We're going to have a dragstrip!"

    — E. V. Fish, president of the Palmer chamber of commerce, at a city council special session, *The Frontiersman*, Apr. 4, 1963.[^fr630404]

The ad on the front page of Palmer's weekly in March 1963 was for a dance: "ROAD HOGS DANCE," Friday, March 15, 7:30 till midnight, refreshments available. "Admission: Drag $1.00 Stag 75c."[^fr630314] Inside the same issue the paper explained what the dance at the Elks hall was for. The Palmer Road Hogs Car Club was holding it "as part of their campaign to obtain a drag strip in the Palmer area."[^fr630314b]

## A runway and a police chief (1958–63)

The idea was not new. In October 1958 the paper reported that an American Legion-sponsored Palmer Car Club was "now in full swing," with Bob Fritz as temporary president. A spokesman promised that it "will not be a 'gang of uncontrolled kids, racing up and down the city streets'"; a member who broke that rule would go "before a court of club members." And: "we hope to acquire a drag strip and hold drag events."[^fr581016]

By March 1963 the strip-seekers had a powerful friend. Police Chief Louis Bencardino was helping "the Palmer Car Club" with plans for a strip parallel to the east-west runway at the Palmer airport, and the Matanuska Valley Airmen had "indicated their willingness to cooperate."[^fr630307]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup> The club had been given a 1950 Chevrolet, which the boys were fixing up to raffle off at a later dance, and they were looking for a garage to meet and work in.[^fr630314b] When Rosco Taylor told the city council on March 12 that the strip could become "a neighborhood nuisance," the chief answered that racing would probably be held only one day a week, that the Airmen saw a chance to improve their runway, and that the strip "will be a help in handling our own juvenile problem."[^fr630314]

On Tuesday night, March 26, so many people came to the council meeting that more chairs had to be carried in, about 30 visitors for a subject that was not even on the agenda. In a standing count only three or four opposed the strip.[^fr630328] Bencardino described what the club wanted: a paved strip 60 feet wide and 4,000 feet long on the east-west runway, grandstands for 2,000 to 3,000 people, a control tower where the two runways crossed, and cars stopped whenever the tower signaled a plane coming in on the north-south runway. Senator E. L. Bartlett, he said, had promised to help in Washington.[^fr630328]

Former mayor Charles Logsdon was the loudest voice against. "I am thoroughly opposed to encouraging dragging that might interfere with flying," he said, and he listed the other sports the Valley needed, from a year-round swimming pool to dude ranching. His speech brought the strip's backers to their feet "in sometimes angry denunciations." A restaurant owner, Mrs. Howard Campbell, praised the "courteous and considerate" Anchorage racers who had eaten in her restaurants. "This town needs some business," she said.[^fr630328]

Two men from the Anchorage Timing Association spoke too, Ernst Brown and Pat Brouillet, about the Anchorage group's "futile attempts" to find a site near the city. Brouillet described a hastily planned drag event at Tanacross the Labor Day before that, he said, had drawn people from as far as 300 miles away, more than all the other Labor Day events in the region.[^fr630328]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup> Brown, the association's financial consultant, compared Palmer's chances with the Santa Anita horse race track in California.[^fr630328]

## Three sites and a groundbreaking (spring 1963)

There was a rival plan on the same agenda. A committee of the local Area Redevelopment group favored a private strip on 640 acres of state land on the Valley side of the Knik River bridge, planned by Lee Nelson of Anchorage: a strip 60 feet wide and 4,000 feet long, a sports car track and bleachers, $180,000 in all, with Nelson ready to put up $80,000 in cash. If the airport plan went ahead, the paper said, "Mr. Nelson will abandon his project."[^fr630328]

The airport plan did not go ahead. A week later Bencardino told a special session that the strip was moving to a tract outside the city limits, because of the controversy over the airport, the "exceedingly high price" of land next to it, and an offer of a tract big enough for a circle track and a "tilt cart track" (as printed) besides the strip, and even a new state fair with a coliseum; the Alaska Railroad had said it could build a spur. That was when the chamber of commerce president, E. V. Fish, rose to his feet: "We're going to have a dragstrip!" And, he added, "Not down by Knik Bridge where an independent developer would construct motels, a grocery and other commercial facilities." The council passed a motion to support the project "in any way possible."[^fr630404] Washington had, in the meantime, come through anyway: in April the chief reported that the FAA's Anchorage office had been told to help make the airport available, after a visit from Senator "Bob" Bartlett.[^fr630418]

Wasilla made its own bid. On April 28 its businessmen's association offered the Anchorage Timing Association about 100 acres of cleared land on Big Lake Road, and the association's members backed the site with only three votes against; members pledged to buy stock in a track corporation, and a team called the Swappers gave a $500 check.[^fr630502]

Palmer moved faster. In mid-May the mayor, two businessmen, the police chief and the chamber president, Barney Fish, broke ground for the Palmer Dragway on the proposed new Alaska State Fair site, about two miles south of the old fairgrounds, the first step toward a corporation called Palmer Dragway, Sports and Recreation Center Enterprise, Inc. They hoped to run the first race the weekend of July 21.[^fr630516] Two weeks later Mayor Ralph Moore bought the first share of stock from Mrs. Pat Wells, owner of the Matanuska Hotel.[^fr630529]

## Racing on the Matanuska Road (summer and fall 1963)

No strip was ready in July, so the racers used a road. On Sunday, July 14, the Road Runners, "Palmer drag racing club," ran drag races on a closed section of the Matanuska Road at the Hay Drier Road intersection, with the state highway department's permission, a bypass for traffic, and the Alaska Timing Association as sponsor. Admission was $1, and so was the entry fee.[^fr630711] In the same issue Laura Barnes of the Palmer area told the Anchorage chamber about "a private drag strip to be built near the Knik River Bridge."[^fr630711]

In August the project had a new name and a manager: Jim Jones, owner of the Alaska Speedway in Anchorage, would run the Palmer Raceway, with a 3,650-foot drag strip as its first piece, a two-mile sports car course, a half-mile horse track and a go-kart track on 175 acres of the Glen Woods property south of town. Drag racing "could begin late this year," Jones said.[^fr630815] That month the Road Runners, now billed as a "Palmer car club," sponsored the Matanuska Road Races for sports cars, what the paper called the last road race on Palmer's city streets: "Construction of the Palmer Raceway south of town is beginning, and future races will be held there."[^fr630822]

By November the raceway committee had $35,000 in pledges, at $100 a share. The directors of Koslosky's Department Store took 50 shares. The chairman, Lloyd Severns, "emphasized that the purchase of shares is an investment and not a donation."[^fr631121] In December the pledges stood at $38,000, and the committee wanted another $25,000 locally before it went looking in the surrounding areas.[^fr631212]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup>

## Down the peninsula and up the road

Palmer was not the only Alaska town where young drivers wanted a strip. In January 1962 the *Cheechako News* reported that Kenai teenagers had formed the Kenai Coachmen to repair jalopies in a borrowed tent two miles south of town, hoping someday "to get a supervised drag strip."[^ckn620112] A month later Soldotna's chamber sketched a park north of the town airport with picnic tables, a boat harbor "and drag strip."[^ckn620202] In June 1963 seven charter members formed the Soldotna Timing Association, whose bylaws made any member fined for speeding pay a matching sum into the club treasury. "Dragging should be done in its proper place on a drag strip or track away from the regular traffic," said its secretary, Ken Lancaster, who ran a car lot in Soldotna.[^ckn630607] In Fairbanks a man complained to police in 1962 that "cars and a bus had been using Ninth Ave. as a drag strip";[^jw620516] in Kodiak in 1958, two youths caught driving abreast on Mission Road near Max's Drive Inn were taken to court "on a charge of drag strip racing," and both were fined.[^km580517]

## Polar Dragway (1964)

The strip Alaska got first was the private one. In 1963, the *Frontiersman* wrote forty years later, Lee Nelson "found a plot of land in a forest just outside Palmer," leased it from the Bureau of Land Management, and by the end of the summer of 1964 had a quarter-mile strip "among the trees." Originally called Polar Dragway, it opened on Labor Day weekend 1964, with more than 500 fans and Governor William A. Egan cutting the ribbon.[^fr040907]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup> A woman who rode in the grader as a girl while her father leveled the land "north of the Knik River" remembered the doubters in 2014: "Everybody thought he must have a whole lot of money to waste."[^adn140817] The Nelsons owned it for 30 years.[^adn140817] Over those years it went by Polar Dragway, Thunder Valley Raceway, Valley Raceway, Big Valley Raceway and Polar Raceway.[^fr240712] In November 1994 a group of local racers bought the racing operation from Lee Nelson and incorporated as Top End, Inc.; the *Frontiersman* names them as Earl and Karen Lackey, Jack (Randy) Harris, Louie Kirk, John Childs, Jerry Schreiner, Gregg Gunnarson and Kristen Kinn, and the strip became Alaska Raceway Park.[^raceak][^fr240712] "It needed to change hands and it needed to get back into shape," Earl Lackey said in 2024. The Lackeys bought the other owners' stock and became sole owners in 1998,[^fr240712][^raceak] the year the paper's 2004 anniversary story gave as the sale from Nelson to Lackey.[^fr040907] It is still running, run today by the Lackeys' daughter, Michelle Lackey Maynor, as "a multi-faceted racing complex" with "a NHRA drag strip and NASCAR oval track"; the oval was added in 2016.[^fr240712]

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** **Was it one club or several?** In 1963 the paper calls the strip-seekers the Palmer Car Club (March 7 and 21), the Palmer Road Hogs Car Club (March 14) and the Road Runners, "Palmer drag racing club" (July 11) and "Palmer car club" (Aug. 22).[^fr630307][^fr630314b][^fr630711][^fr630822] The 1958 club was also called the Palmer Car Club.[^fr581016] We have not found a story that says whether these are one club renamed or separate clubs. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **What happened at Tanacross on Labor Day 1962?** Only Pat Brouillet's description to the Palmer council, as the paper reported it, tells us about the meet and its crowd.[^fr630328] A Fairbanks or Tok paper for September 1962 would confirm it. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** **Is the Lee Nelson of the Knik River plan the Lee Nelson of Polar Dragway?** The 1963 paper names "Lee Nelson of Anchorage" as the developer of a private strip on the Valley side of the Knik River bridge, and in July a "private drag strip to be built near the Knik River Bridge."[^fr630328][^fr630711] The 2004 and 2014 stories say Lee Nelson found the land in 1963 and leveled it north of the Knik River.[^fr040907][^adn140817] The names, year and place match, but no source we read says in so many words that the 1963 proposal became Polar Dragway. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** **What became of Palmer Raceways, Inc.?** The last report we have is the December 1963 pledge total of $38,000; stock subscriptions were payable "on or before March 20, 1964."[^fr631024][^fr631212] The Chronicling America run of the *Frontiersman* we could search ends in 1963, so we do not know whether any of the raceway was built. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
