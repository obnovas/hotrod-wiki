# Sand on the Track: Las Vegas, Henderson and Southern Nevada's Eight Drag Strips (1953–2000)

*A junkyard for a speed shop, a sheriff who sent his deputies to watch, and a desert strip where the wind kept bringing the sand back*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium (a hometown paper's 2011 retrospective built on racers' own memories, with a companion list that differs from it on dates; firsthand forum memories of the Henderson strip; one racer-historian's note from 1956 Nevada newspapers) · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> local newspaper retrospective (Jeff Wolf, *Las Vegas Review-Journal*, March 30, 2011, and its list "Dragstrips in Southern Nevada"); forum recollections (H.A.M.B., "Thunderbird Raceway- Henderson, Nevada circa 1960," 2011–22; Jalopy Journal post, 2011); a drag-racing database note drawn from 1956 newspapers (Mel Bashore, Draglist) · **Last verified:** 2026-10-30 (Round 27; quotes checked against the pages) · **Open issues:** Vegas Drive's years ⚠; the berm and the jet cars ⚠; AHRA Winter Nationals ◐; who built Stardust ? · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "No one wanted us to race (illegally) on the streets, and the police began to accommodate us."

    — Cecil Fredi, an original member of the Las Vegas Hustlers, to the *Las Vegas Review-Journal* (2011).[^rj11]

Before there was a strip there was Main Street. Club members gathered downtown, cruised, and settled challenges in illegal races on the edge of a town that was then only a few blocks from Main. "We lived 'American Graffiti,'" Steve Kirby, an original member of the Las Vegas Hustlers, told the *Review-Journal* in 2001.[^rj11] The old racers credit three clubs, the Hustlers, the Sinners and the Crapshooters, with the racing fever of the mid-1950s.[^rj11]

Their speed shop was a junkyard: Burt and Cecilia Gibbs's yard on North A Street. "We grew up in the junkyard," Cecil Fredi, an original Hustler, said. "We had the love to race without a budget." The Gibbses' son Fred was always there to help with parts, and raced his own Bantam-bodied roadster until he was killed in a 1961 car accident on the way home from the U.S. Fuel and Gas Championships at Bakersfield. "Fred was our catalyst, our mentor," Fredi said.[^rj11]

## The sheriff's drag strip

In 1953 a patch of pavement on Vegas Valley Drive was given over to organized, part-time drag races, "the first of Southern Nevada's eight dragstrips."[^rj11] The original club members remember Sheriff Butch Leypoldt assigning officers to watch over it; their favorite was a Las Vegas policeman, Dale "Gunsmoke" Swift. A flagman started the races, volunteers ran them with timing equipment borrowed from the Las Vegas Sports Car Club, and Jack "Ace" Hanley organized weekend events and started the Las Vegas Drag Racing Association.[^rj11]

The racing moved around town after that. The paper's story puts it on Vegas Drive in 1956, near today's municipal golf course, and on Highland Drive in 1957;[^rj11]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> a racer who walked a mile or so to Highland in 1957 or '58 for his first drag race remembered "a street running through an industrial area closed off for the Sunday drags," where he saw his first dragster, a Hudson six in bare frame rails.[^hambnv]

## Henderson: "hooterville"

In 1958 the city of Henderson provided the asphalt for what the paper calls Southern Nevada's first true drag strip, Thunderbird Raceway, near Dick Stewart Motors on Boulder Highway.[^rj11] A 2011 Jalopy Journal post describes it as an old World War II auxiliary runway operated by a local club, the Southern Nevada Charioteers, formed in 1954.[^jj11] The people who went there remember it differently from the history books: "My buddies and I were regulars at the henderson Drags (never called it Thunderbird raceway) until it closed," one wrote; another called it "hooterville." Joe Wells, of Wells Cargo and the Thunderbird Hotel, did the dirt work and paving, and the Charioteers and Dalby Shirley of the Henderson recreation department got it going. Charioteers took turns selling tickets at the gate.[^hambnv]

The desert never left it alone. "Henderson was a great track, only problem was that the sand blew onto it and made traction a challenge," a racer remembered. Southern California's best came anyway: at a meet in April 1959, right after the Smokers' meet that year, Tony Waters beat Ted Cyr in the final.[^hambnv] The high point, the Jalopy Journal post says, came in January 1961, when the AHRA held its first Winter Nationals there.[^jj11]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup>

The jet cars came too, and the stories about them do not agree. A banked berm served as the approach and the staging lanes. One regular remembered the track manager, Shirley, booking in a couple of jets, "time dims the memory," and the heat breaking up the asphalt on the berm and blowing "huge chunks" as far as Boulder Highway, several hundred feet south. Another remembered that the berm went in after Art Arfons's jet dragster blew the wooden fence onto Boulder Highway, with passing drivers dodging the pieces.[^hambnv17]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> The same man remembered Bob Bradley's top-eliminator go-kart, a Mercury outboard with no clutch, held up on blocks at the line and dropped at full throttle when the flag went up; it beat the early locals until the dragster men had the rules changed to require a clutch.[^hambnv17]

Henderson closed in 1964.[^rj11]

## Stardust, Jean International and the Sand-drome

For two years after that there was no approved strip, and racers went back to the streets. Then came Stardust Raceway at Tropicana Avenue and Rainbow Boulevard, believed to have been built by the Stardust hotel's owners.[^rj11]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup> Don Schumacher, who raced a Funny Car there with the casino's sponsorship, remembered one thing about the surface: "all I remember was how much sand was on the track." Stardust went dark in 1971, when Pardee-Phillips bought the land for houses.[^rj11]

The next eight years had no strip at all. The Southern Nevada Timing Association ran races south of town on old Highway 91 near Jean and Sloan. "We called it Jean International," said Ken Black, later a champion Pro Stock team owner. When the Speedrome opened across Las Vegas Boulevard from Nellis Air Force Base, he had a name for that too: "We called that the Sand-drome." It lasted 21 years, until the strip at Las Vegas Motor Speedway replaced it in 2000.[^rj11]

## Up north: a dragster built with airplane parts

Reno's racers had their own runway, the Douglas-Tahoe Airport near Minden. Mel Bashore's notes from 1956 Nevada papers describe Brent Tyler as "something of a mad scientist": for 1956 he moved his flathead to the rear of his dragster, with a turned-around Cord differential and Morris Minor torsion bars, and Warren Welsh turned the day's top speed, 118.03 mph, on May 27. Then the *Nevada State Journal* of July 1 ran the front-page headline "Man builds hot-rod with airplane parts." Tyler was arrested for grand larceny over $150 worth of parts from two planes parked at a Reno airport, until it turned out the owner's son had given him permission to take them from a wrecked Fairchild trainer, and the charges were dropped. That August the Cal-Neva Timing Association held half-mile races at Douglas-Tahoe.[^dl56]

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did the racing move to Vegas Drive?** The *Review-Journal*'s story says the first strip "lasted through 1954" and the racers moved to Vegas Drive "In 1956"; the paper's own companion list, published the same day, gives Vegas Drive as "1954-56" and adds a "Las Vegas Drag Strip" on the main runway of Sky Corral Airport in the late 1950s, which the story does not mention.[^rj11][^rj11list] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Did the AHRA hold its first Winter Nationals at Henderson in January 1961?** Only the 2011 Jalopy Journal post says so.[^jj11] A 1961 *Drag News* or Las Vegas paper would confirm it. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Was the berm there before the jet cars, or built because of them?** One regular (2011) describes the berm as part of the track's design when the jets' heat blew chunks of it toward Boulder Highway; another (2017) says it was installed after Art Arfons's jet blew the wooden fence onto the highway. Both are memories, decades later.[^hambnv][^hambnv17] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** **Who built Stardust Raceway, and when?** The *Review-Journal* says only that it was "believed to have been built by owners of the Stardust Hotel and Casino"; its list gives the years 1966–71 and calls it casino-owned.[^rj11][^rj11list] A forum regular remembers a later "Stardust International Raceway" built for the Can-Am series.[^hambnv] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
