# Somewhere to Blow Off Steam: The Sheriff, the Runway Racers and Pico Raceway, Rutland, Vermont (1951–52)

*A sheriff, a trucking man and a farmer's field*

## The runway problem

Pico Raceway was a stock car track, but by the local telling it was born out of drag racing. Young men were racing on the Rutland airport's runways, and instead of just running them off, the sheriff helped build them somewhere else to go.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>

Ed Fabian of West Rutland told the story at a Rutland Historical Society roundtable in 2007: the sheriff had been called to the local airport to stop young people from drag racing. "There was a big blowout down there," he said.[^haley07] Bill Ladabouche, a racing historian who grew up in nearby Proctor and worked with Fabian, filled in the rest. Al Romano, "young and mechanically-adept," and his buddies were getting on the nerves of everyone near the small airport because "they loved using the often-empty runways as a dragstrip – probably at night." Rutland County Sheriff Geno Franzoni confronted Al's father, Pasquale "Patsy" Romano of Romano Brothers Transportation. The excitable Romano "blurted out that these young men needed some place to blow off steam." Franzoni agreed, and the two decided to get into stock car racing. What they didn't have was land, so they went to a local farmer, Abe Newman, who offered part of his unused farmland.[^lad15]

## A Sunday vote and a lot of dust

They formed Pico Raceway, Inc., with Newman as president, Franzoni as treasurer and Romano as clerk, and built a half-mile dirt track on Route 7 South in 1951. Rutland Town voters approved Sunday racing 125 to 64 that August, and racing began later that month. About 4,000 people came the first day, to bleachers that held 1,800.[^haley07]

The surface was clay trucked in from Clarendon Springs, and it threw dust. People at the roundtable remembered the owner of a Pico Motel nearby complaining about the dirt, and some said you could hardly see the cars. The track got lights in 1952. "If it was paved in the beginning, it would have been a tremendous success," said C.J. Richards, who later bought some of those lights for his own Fairmont Speedway.[^haley07]

Al Romano raced there, of course. He built his own 1936 Chevy coupe, number 303, with the exhaust modified "to rasp and rattle in a very unique way," and announcer Red Wildey would call out "Al Romano in 3 oh 3- the Bumblebee!"[^lad15] Ladabouche, five or six at the time, remembered that the announcer worked for the Romanos: "his boss's car."[^haley07] The family ran a second car, number 111, to fill out the field, Ladabouche wrote, "and maybe occasionally run interference for Al."[^lad15] Rutland's Jerry King raced a 1930s Ford coupe numbered "5 Aces."[^haley07]

## Records in the trash

Pico was largely dormant after 1952, and its land later went to a General Electric plant.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Open question: see Open questions">?</a></sup> Al raced little after 1953 or 1954; the trucking company needed its mechanic.[^lad15] Around 1965, wearing his trademark cowboy hat, he came back to build a channeled, narrowed and lowered 1936 Chevy for a young Rutland driver, Chet Doaner, at Richards's revived Fairmont track.[^lad15] When Ladabouche's mother, who golfed with Romano, asked whether he had any Pico papers left, he said the family had disposed of everything five years before.[^lad15] In 2007 the only known Pico poster hung framed in the collection of Proctor's Dan Kearney.[^haley07] Ed Fabian once drove his 1934 Ford street rod up to show Al, with one of Romano's old racing numbers painted on the sides. Al lived until 2011.[^lad15]

The airport racing that started it all left no record we have found except the story itself: a sheriff who could have written tickets, and a father who said the boys needed somewhere to go.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Was the airport drag racing really why Pico was built?** Fabian said it was the impetus, and the *Rutland Herald* wrote that Pico was built "at least in part, to give those people a place to race."[^haley07] Ladabouche's fuller version, the sheriff confronting Patsy Romano, is introduced "as the story goes," and he credits Fabian with much of his Pico research.[^lad15] Both lead back to the same local circle; no 1951 newspaper account has been read. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** **When did General Electric build on the site?** Ladabouche's text says the land was used for a General Electric plant "by the 1970's," while a caption on the same page says the track, still visible in a 1957 aerial photo, "was obliterated in the 1960's by a General Electric plant."[^lad15] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
