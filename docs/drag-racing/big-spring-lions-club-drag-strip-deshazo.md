# Fifty-Five Days: Howard Deshazo, the Lions Club and a Drag Strip for Big Spring, Texas (from 1956)

*A town of oilmen, bankers and airmen said yes to a drag strip in under two months, and a young Texan built the timing van to prove it*

    **Confidence:** Low (everything comes from one first-person account by Howard Deshazo, posted in 2014 on the Highland Lakes Timing Association's site from a Rust and Chrome magazine post, which quotes his *Hot Rod* article of January 1958; we have not read that article or any Big Spring newspaper) · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> a participant's memoir, reposted by a local timing association; a quotation from *Hot Rod* (January 1958) as he gives it · **Last verified:** 2026-11-14 (Round 41) · **Open issues:** ◐ the whole account is one man's; ◐ "first mobile timing unit"; ? the date of the first event, and the strip's later years · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"

    — Howard Deshazo, on getting Big Spring's strip built, on the Highland Lakes Timing Association site, posted July 31, 2014.[^hl]

## A project of the month

Big Spring's drag strip is the story of a whole town saying yes at once. Howard Deshazo wrote that on November 5, 1956, at a meeting of the new Big Spring Lions Club, Capt. R. O. Franz, then stationed at Webb Air Force Base, moved that "A Drag Strip For Big Spring" be adopted as the club's "project of the month." In the passage he quotes from his own *Hot Rod* article of January 1958: "Less than 55 days later Big Spring had its first full-fledged drag event, backed by every civic organization in the city and sanctioned by NHRA."<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^hl]

He remembered the first strip committee's roster as "a Who's Who of Big Spring": a district attorney, a judge, a state representative, a news editor, a city commissioner, a bank president, a YMCA secretary, a school principal, physicians, auto dealers, a refinery president and the commander of the Air Force base, "who held the key to the whole project — a potential strip site."[^hl] A local oilman, "quite prominent in the oil business," with influence among the town's fathers, got interested and helped get the first drag organized.[^hl]

Two local clubs did the work, he wrote: the Aces Auto Club and the Tappet Tickers, members from 17 to 30 years old, in "the hot, dry plains of West Texas." The first event used flags for starts and stopwatches for times. It made enough money to buy scales, electronic timing equipment and other items. He said a few "older guys (25 – we thought that was old)" had formed a car club, and the 16- and 17-year-olds who hung around them picked it up.[^hl]

## A flat-front van, and then a V

Deshazo designed a mobile timing unit for the new electronic timing equipment, and an oil jobber, Ted Grobel, paid for it and supplied the workmen. Deshazo called it "the first mobile timing unit ever from scratch just for drag racing, perhaps anywhere in the country."<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup> A generator ran the timing gear, a public-address system and the air-conditioning.[^hl] It went on a 1949 Ford delivery van with a flathead V8, and the first version had a flat front. On trial runs the old Ford "simply couldn't compete with the wind drag," so back to the shop it went and came out with a "V" front. That fixed the drag and let the van cruise in the wind.[^hl]

With the van, he wrote, "We used the timing unit to hold drags in towns so small no one would ever believe could have happened." The *Hot Rod* piece, he said, was a letter he sent to the magazine: "Of course, not being a writer, I simply wrote a letter telling him all of the details. He simply edited it and put my name on it. Ha!" It put Big Spring on the map, he wrote, and brought visitors from the West Texas oilfields. Through Wally Parks he became an NHRA advisor for the area.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">?</a></sup>[^hl]

He remembered the place by its name. They had brought drag racing, he wrote, to "a place named after a spring, a spring that had been dry for a hundred years." The town let them try, and people turned out and paid. Deshazo had gone to the 1954 national meet at Caddo Mills, Texas, where the top speed in the quarter mile was 98.8 mph, "an astounding" number to him then, and that was where he decided he wanted in. The host of the 2014 post notes that Deshazo died in 2010.[^hl] A town that wanted a strip had a committee, a site and a timing van inside two months, and its manager had designed the van himself.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **The Lions Club, the 55 days and the roster.** All of it is Deshazo's own account, written decades later, plus his quotation of his own 1958 article. The page we read is a repost of a Rust and Chrome magazine item on the Highland Lakes Timing Association's site.[^hl] We do not know the date of the Lions Club meeting's minutes or of the first event; by his arithmetic it was before the end of 1956, but he gives no day. A *Big Spring Daily Herald* scan from late 1956 or early 1957, which the Texas Tech Southwest Collection holds, would settle it; its files refused our fetch. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Was it the first mobile timing unit?** Deshazo's own words, and he hedges them with "perhaps anywhere in the country."[^hl] We have no other account of the van. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
