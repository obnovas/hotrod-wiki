# No Lawn Chairs: The Oilers and the Race of Gentlemen on the Jersey Shore (Allenhurst and Wildwood, New Jersey, 1947–2021)

*A book about the first cars, an empty fall beach, and a club name borrowed, with permission, from Carlsbad*

## Tired of parking lots

The Race of Gentlemen is a car show that refuses to be parked. It runs prewar hot rods and old motorcycles down a beach in New Jersey, under the name of a California club from 1947, because one man got bored of sitting next to his car.

Mel Stultz built prewar hot rods and bikes in Neptune, near the Jersey Shore. *Hot Rod* wrote in 2013 that he was "sick and tired of going to the typical parking-lot car shows; spectacles of lawn chairs, light conversation, and stagnant rides never impressed him much." He told *SJ Magazine* the same thing in 2021: "I just found the car shows I was going to stagnant. It was an older generation that was into hot rods and customs, and for me the real fun was getting to the event and leaving."[^hr13][^sj] Starting in 2003 he had put on his own events, the Jalopy Jump and Jive and Motor Speedway drags, where, *Hot Rod* said, "no lawn chairs were allowed on the premises—period."[^hr13]

## A club name from Carlsbad

The club he ran it with had a history three thousand miles away. The Oilers had formed in Carlsbad, California, in 1947. One of their founders, Jim Nelson, co-founded the Dragmaster Chassis Company in 1959.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^hr13] (Dragmasters, the chassis shop, gave [Roland Leong](../people/roland-leong.md) his first California job.) A phone conversation involving the hot rod writer Robert Genat and Stultz's friend Mike Kliman put Stultz in touch with Nelson. They talked by phone and over the internet about traditional hot rodding, and Nelson asked if he would like to carry on the Oilers back East.[^hr13] The club's site says "the keys to the club were handed" to Stultz in 2010.[^trog] *Hot Rod* described the new Oilers' members as "all dyed-in-the-wool, full-blooded traditional hot rod folk," from West Virginia to upstate New York.[^hr13]

The idea for the race came from a book. In it, Stultz told *SJ Magazine*, he read that before board-track racing the first cars were tested on beaches: "If your brakes failed you could steer it into softer sand, and if something caught fire you could drive into the ocean." The club's site points to a one-mile beach race at Cape May in the summer of 1905 with Henry Ford, Louis Chevrolet and Walter Christie.[^sj][^trog] A couple of weeks later, Stultz said, he was sitting on the beach at Asbury Park and looked at it: "there's a giant straightaway with nothing on it. Man, that looks like a drag strip to me."[^sj]

## October 20, 2012

He pitched an eighth-mile race on the sand to two small beach towns near Asbury Park, Allenhurst and Loch Arbour. Their mayors, Dave McLaughlin and Fernicola of Loch Arbour, said yes.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^rider][^hr13] The idea was hatched in August and the race set for late October, when, *Hot Rod* noted, the weather on the shore "can be very iffy."[^hr13] Stultz, Tom Larusso and the Oilers handed out flyers and hand-painted the signs and billboards.[^rider] Cars had to be pre-1934, and bikes American-made, tank-shifted and pre-1950.[^rider]

There was a free beach party on Friday night, with a band and a bonfire.[^rider] The race was Saturday, October 20.[^aps] The race's Facebook page counted 160 invitees.[^rider][^hr13] By 10 a.m. the crowd was ten times that, and by 2 p.m. there were more than 3,000 people on the beach.[^hr13] The tide put the start a few hours behind, which only gave the crowd time to grow. Burke Construction brought rollers to pack the sand. The racers launched off plywood boards for traction and hugged the waterline, where the sand was hardest. The announcer stood on a perch built from old scaffolding and a World War II–era trailer. The New Jersey State Police showed up and, instead of stopping it, asked for helmets and a wider gap for spectators.[^hr13] Fifteen cars and fifteen motorcycles ran.[^hr13][^rider]

Stultz won the bike drags on his Indian and, *Rider* reported, handed his wreath to "The Flying Cubans," Matt Rush and Bill Applegate, who had raced a 1950 Panhead owned by Marty MacCollum all day. "We didn't receive a single complaint," Stultz said. "Thank God for New Jersey."[^rider]

## After the storm

Sandy, by then a post-tropical storm, came ashore near Brigantine, New Jersey, on October 29, nine days after the race.[^nhc][^hr13] (In 2021 Stultz remembered it as four days.)[^sj] "No beach, no cabanas, no nothing," he told *Rider* that winter.[^rider] About six months later, he said, someone from Wildwood, near Cape May, asked if the race would come south for a season.[^sj] Wildwood is a stone's throw from Cape May, the club's site notes.[^trog] "We got to the Wildwood beach and thought oh my god, we can never go back," he said. "There's a huge beach, tons of hotels, a 1950s scene." It has been held in Wildwood in early October since 2013.[^sj]

By 2021 it drew 20,000 to 25,000 people, by Stultz's count, and about 180 cars and motorcycles were entered. The rules had settled: car bodies no later than 1934, parts up to 1953, "the last year of the flathead," and motorcycles 1947 or earlier, all American. The track was still 660 feet. Racers shipped cars and bikes from Japan, Austria, Germany and New Zealand, and Stultz said "you can build a car for under $5,000 if you have the ingenuity."[^sj] Nick Hosmer, 30, who grew up helping his father build hot rods, had watched for years and that fall was running a 1925 Model T roadster called "The Irish Sausage." "You're not going to get any money if you win," he said. "It's just about glory and bragging rights."[^sj] That year the club was also building a little track for kids' miniature cars, "like a glorified pinewood derby."[^sj]

"Some people come at us like how dare you, that stuff should be in a museum," Stultz said. "But these machines were made to go."[^sj] He was tired of watching hot rods sit, so he put them back on the sand.
