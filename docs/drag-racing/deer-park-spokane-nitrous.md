# Bell Hoses and a Bottle of Laughing Gas: Deer Park, Spokane's Airport Strip and the Nitrous Secret (1950–1974)

*A town council that read the gate receipts, a parts-store dragster with a stub nose, and the anesthetic hidden under the cowl*

## An airport nobody needed

The Army built the Deer Park airport, north of Spokane, in 1943 as a place to land the biggest planes of the war; it was dedicated on Aug. 24, 1944, having cost $1,250,000. After the war it sat nearly idle, deeded to the town of Deer Park, and plans for aircraft plants there came to nothing. "The airport had natural race tract potential," the local historical society wrote.[^cdphs]

On March 15, 1953, under a contract the town council had let at its March 4 meeting, the Lilac City Timing Association held its first drags on the airport's southern runway. Its president was Dusty Rhodes of Colville, and its treasurer, Norman Davis of Spokane, said anyone could enter a "…hot rod, family sedan, jeep, truck, tractor or---what have you…".[^cdphs] Whether that was the first racing of any kind is a matter of words. The Spokane Roadster Racing Association had the council's leave to use the field on April 2 and April 9, 1950; the society's timeline calls those "the first drag races at the airport," but Spokane's drag racing historian Gary Hordemann, who dates the council's permission to its meeting of March 2, 1950, says that "This first use was not for drag racing, but rather for roadster racing."[^cdphs][^exch]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>

The first timing gear came off a gas station's apron. Carl Justice, an early Deer Park mechanic and racer, described it as "two hoses (the type that rang a bell as a car entered a service station) across the finish line."[^cdphs] Spectators watched from their parked cars until bleachers went up.

## Running it for the town

In October 1954, the society found, the Inland Empire Timing Association replaced the Lilac City club, with seven charter members and 140 active members, one of six clubs in the NHRA-affiliated Cascade Drag Racing Conference.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> When the NHRA's Drag Safari came through in the summer of 1955, Deer Park was its fourth stop from Southern California, and the visit made the September 1955 *Hot Rod*. The Smith and Rupert GMC-six dragster set top speed of the meet at 109.22 mph; Steve Pick's track record from 1954 was 114.[^cdphs] By then the club had bought electronic timers, set 66 feet either side of the finish line.

Deer Park read the gate receipts closely. For 1956 the club paid the town $50 "…in advance of each meet" and 40% of gate receipts over $400, and the Kiwanis Club had the concessions. The season opener of April 1958 drew an estimated 9,000 people; traffic was bumper to bumper on Highway 395, and in 1959 the council spent $1,250 on a radar speed detector for the police chief and four state patrolmen to use on race days. That season brought the town $3,500, from 17,000 spectators.[^cdphs]

Then came the fight. In late 1961 Don Zundel and Orval Moe (Orville in the society's own timeline and in *The Exchange*) asked to bid for the lease with a new club, and the minutes of Nov. 1, 1961 list their complaints about the Inland Empire group, beginning: "That Ned Kelly & cohorts are running the whole show." The old club would not raise its offer, and the contract went to Zundel and Moe's Spokane Timing Association.[^cdphs] Its 1962 two-day invitational lost $2,120, so the town waived its percentage for one extra race. Saturday night drags began in June 1963, and some spectators parked in farmers' fields and walked in free in the dark. On Oct. 6, 1963 a 17-year-old Mead High School senior was struck by pieces of an exploding clutch and died two days later, "the first injury to a spectator in six years."[^cdphs]

## The bottle under the cowl

The best story at Deer Park was a secret. In 1958 a Spokane racer, Dick Flynn, went looking in the library for a new wonder fuel, "a time-honored ritual among would-be pioneers like Barney Navarro," who had tried injecting straight oxygen and given it up after a meltdown. Flynn found nitrous oxide, the dentist's anesthetic, which frees extra oxygen in a hot cylinder. His friend Gary Harms laid a nitrous bottle in the tool tray of his supercharged 1940 Ford, ran a hose to an air-gun nozzle on his thigh and pressed the button off the line. Harms "won B/Gas at Deer Park on September 7, 1958, the only time he trophied at the track," and *Hot Rod*'s 2016 account calls it, "To the best of our knowledge, ... the first application of nitrous oxide in drag racing."[^hrnos]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup>

The dragster that carried it was the Thrifty Auto car, sponsored by Hank Clay's Thrifty Auto Supply, "a garden variety parts store that started selling speed equipment in the 1950s." Flynn hid two 6-pound bottles under the cowl, feeding gas to every intake port. On March 5, 1961 at Famoso, near Bakersfield, driver Don Stortroen made an 8.5-second run at 185.58 mph, fast enough for the track's top-speed record, and then, paired with Don Garlits in the first round, set the e.t. record at 8.28 seconds; Garlits threw a belt. The car was strangely short: before the meet, Fern Clay, backing out after a visit to the crew, had driven over its nose, and the crew cut it back to an 88-inch wheelbase and chained a floor jack to the front axle to keep it down.[^hrnos] Back home on April 9, 1961, Thrifty met the Kelly Automotive car of Jack and Jim Hordemann, which carried its own nitrous system, in what the article believes was the first match between two nitrous dragsters.[^hrnos] Stortroen was badly hurt in a U-joint explosion on Sept. 24, 1961, and later moved to California to work for Vic Hubbard.[^hrnos]

The rest of the country learned the trick from a big spread in the *Drag News* of May 20, 1961. Bill Huth, who had founded Speed Power Suspension, had teamed with Flynn and Jack Hordemann to sell nitrous systems as "Second Wind" (a *Hot Rod* caption says the ads ran in *Drag News* until its May 2, 1961 issue), but poor instructions left engines running lean, and they failed.[^hrnos] The line that lasted ran through a gift. In 1962 Ron Hammel, a Spokane expat then working for Tony Capanna, came home on a visit, and Jack Hordemann gave him a nitrous kit for his 1950 Ford pickup. From 1964 Hammel sold kits of his own through 10,000 RPM Speed Equipment, and Mike Thermos, "inspired by one of Hammel's kits," designed a system with Dale Vaznaian; in 1978 they founded Nitrous Oxide Systems.[^hrnos] By this account, then, the road to the nitrous business that racers everywhere now know runs from Spokane's racers, through Hordemann's kit and Hammel's shop, not through the parts store.

## Eagle Electric and 200 mph

John Mead, "the man behind the famed Thrifty Auto Supply car" and a founder of the Inland Empire Timing Association, went on to talk Dave Heerensperger of Eagle Electric, a Spokane electrical and plumbing business, into backing a new Top Fuel car for $3,000. (Mead remembered the Thrifty car as "the first in the world to run nitro."<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>) The sponsorship spun off Eagle Automotive, and Heerensperger's later Eagle hardware chain was sold to Lowe's.[^sr05] With Frank Rupert driving, the Eagle earned $1,000 to $1,500 a night at Deer Park, Puyallup, Shelton and Arlington. "Puyallup on Saturday and Woodburn (Ore.) on Sunday," Mead said. "And we'd be back at work on Monday."[^sr05] In 1965 they took it to the U.S. Nationals at Indianapolis, where Rupert set low ET and top speed in qualifying.[^sr05]

The Deer Park record had reached 194.80 mph in 1964,[^cdphs] and in 1965 the 200-mph mark fell there. Who got there first depends on whom you ask. Drag Strip List's editor, captioning a *Spokane Chronicle* clipping of Aug. 16, 1965, puts Rupert's 202.24 in the Eagle on May 2, "the first car to break 200 mph at Deer Park," and Gary Parmenter's first 200, 200.44 in the Krohn-Parmenter-Rue top fueler, on July 24.[^dslwa] Parmenter remembered it the other way: "We were the first to go 200 mph at the Park."[^dslwa] So did his family. When his brother and teammate Byron Parmenter died in Spokane in 2017, at 82, his obituary in the *Chewelah Independent* said that in the mid-1960s the Krohn-Parmenter-Rue team "traveled all over the northwest attending races. They were the first drag racers to break the 200 m.p.h. barrier at the Deer Park Raceway."[^bparm] The historical society, spelling him "Paramenter," lists him, Rupert and others among the dragsters that topped 200 that summer without saying who was first.[^cdphs]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-5"><a href="#q-5" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>

## The end, and the reunion

In late 1969 Moe announced a $2,000,000 racing complex in western Spokane County. A new operator, Deer Park Raceway Inc., signed a five-year lease at $7,000 a season, with a clause that let it walk away if a "competitive race track" opened in the county. California promoter Ashmore ran the strip in 1972, Leroy Karnes promoted 15 races in 1973, and when Spokane Raceway Park was ready in 1974, Deer Park's drag racing ended.[^cdphs][^exch] The runway is an airport again. Since Sept. 17, 2005, racers have held a Deer Park reunion each September; the first was organized after a funeral, Jim Hordemann's, brought the old crews together.[^sr05b][^cdphs] By 2025 the reunions had raised an estimated $50,000 for Hospice of Spokane.[^exch]

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Were the first drag races at Deer Park in 1950 or 1953?** The historical society's timeline says "1950-The first drag races at the airport were held by the Spokane Roadster Racing Association," with a top speed of 108.2 mph; its own narrative, and Hordemann's reading of the council minutes, put the first organized drags on March 15, 1953, calling the 1950 dates roadster (circle) racing.[^cdphs][^exch] A third source sides with the timeline: a Drag Strip List editor's note says the Spokane Roadster Racing Association "held several races at the Deer Park airport in 1950, the first organized drag racing in the State of Washington."[^dslwa] The 108.2-mph speed suggests at least some straight-line timing in 1950. Lead: the *Deer Park Union*, April 1950. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did the Inland Empire Timing Association take over?** October 1954, says the society; *The Exchange*, from Hordemann, says the IETA "held biweekly races every year from March through October every year from 1953 through 1961."[^cdphs][^exch] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Were Harms (1958) and the Thrifty car (1961) really the first to race on nitrous?** The claim comes from one detailed account, written by the brother of two Kelly-team racers, Jack and Jim Hordemann, which hedges it ("To the best of our knowledge"); we have not yet seen the 1958 results or the May 20, 1961 *Drag News*.[^hrnos] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Nitro or nitrous?** John Mead told *The Spokesman-Review* that the Thrifty car was "the first in the world to run nitro," which cannot be right for nitromethane, a fuel in use well before 1958; he very likely meant nitrous oxide, as *Hot Rod* reports.[^sr05][^hrnos] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-5" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Who made the first 200-mph pass at Deer Park: Frank Rupert or Gary Parmenter?** Drag Strip List's editor, in a caption on a *Spokane Chronicle* clipping of Aug. 16, 1965, gives Rupert first (202.24, May 2) and Parmenter later (200.44, July 24); we have not seen the *Chronicle* itself say who was first. Against it stand Parmenter's own memory on the same page ("We were the first to go 200 mph at the Park") and his brother Byron's 2017 obituary, which says the Krohn-Parmenter-Rue team "were the first drag racers to break the 200 m.p.h. barrier at the Deer Park Raceway." Both of those come from the Parmenter family, and neither gives a date: the obituary, a family notice posted under the byline "Administrator," repeats the team's own claim with no date, speed or source. The caption, with its dates and speeds from a 1965 newspaper, is the stronger evidence. The historical society names both without ranking them.[^dslwa][^bparm][^cdphs] Lead: the *Spokane Chronicle* and *Spokesman-Review*, May–August 1965 (both are scanned in the Google News Archive). <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-5" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
