# "Don't Tell Me Your Name, Tell Me Your Car's Name": U.S. 30 Dragstrip and the People Who Kept It (Hobart, Ind., 1957–1984)

*A promoter's radio ad, a starting line on the Lincoln Highway, a goodie booth across the road, a night watchman in a trailer, and the fans who kept the photographs*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium (the founding and the Christ years come from a drag-strip history book quoting the 1957 *Drag News*, and a local-history site; the closing and the reunions from the hometown daily and its wire story; the radio ad from its producer as told in *National Dragster*'s column; the sources differ on the first year and on who owned the land) · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> *The Times* of Northwest Indiana (2005, and 2012 by AP), Lost Indiana (local-history site, 2001), Jamie Janosz's reunion report (2012), readers' letters in *Hemmings Muscle Machines* (2011), Ron Pellegrini's own post (2000), Phil Burgess in *National Dragster* (2010), Tommy Lee Byrd's *Lost Drag Strips*, excerpted (2015) · **Last verified:** 2026-11-28 (Round 53) · **Open issues:** ⚠ 1957, or as early as 1954; ⚠ who owned the land and why it closed; ◐ the first funny car; ◐ one voice, two Sundays · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "When they call me I tell them, 'Don't tell me your name, tell me your car's name.'"

    — Jerry Trump of Crown Point, track photographer from 1981 to 1984, who kept 5,000 negatives, to *The Times*, 2012 (via the Associated Press).[^ap12]

## Where the great ones ran

A lot of drag strips are remembered for their records. U.S. 30 Dragstrip is remembered by the people who kept it. It closed after the 1984 season, and decades later its fans still had the photographs, the trophies, one of the cars, and the words of the radio ad.[^li01][^ap12]

By Byrd's account in *Lost Drag Strips*, the strip began as a group of investors' plan to take racing off the street, and it was built by the National Timing Association under the supervision of the Northern Indiana Timing Association. It sat just south of Gary, beside U.S. 30, the Lincoln Highway, on 138 acres, with 4,000 feet of pavement and Fosdick timing. Early on each lane was its own strip of asphalt, with grass between them. The *Drag News* of September 21, 1957 quoted N. Perry Luster, an insurance man for racing, calling it the "best and safest drag strip" he had seen, just before its grand opening on September 22, 1957. Local racers told Byrd it was running before that.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>[^ldb] The *Times* and Jamie Janosz's report give 1957 for the opening.[^ap12][^jj12]

The man most racers credit for its best years was Ben Christ. Byrd says Christ ran it with great confidence, also ran [Oswego Drag Raceway](oswego-drag-raceway-illinois.md) for several years, and owned The Gold Agency, a strip management company. Christ had a large stake in the AHRA for several years, so U.S. 30 was AHRA country, with national events, match races on Wednesday nights, and from 1969 to 1976 the Coca-Cola Cavalcade of Stars, his funny car show with its own bracket built for the crowd.[^ldb]

## One word, three times

The ad was Christ's idea. *National Dragster*'s Phil Burgess wrote in 2010, citing the *Chicago Tribune*, that Christ wanted to make sure everyone knew his business was open on Sunday. Steve Cronen of Starbeat Recording Studios in Deerfield, Ill., who produced the ads, said about 50 announcers auditioned and were turned down before Jan Gabriel, known for his calls at Santa Fe Speedway, came in. The script had two announcers trading the "Sundays!" Gabriel's tape recorder had a delay that let him do it alone, and Christ liked it.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup>[^nhra10] It ran mostly on WLS in Chicago: "Sunday! Sunday! Sunday! at the beautiful US 30 Drag Strip," and then, "Where The Great Ones Run!"[^li01] Lost Indiana has a recording from Larry Lujack's show on WLS at 5:05 p.m. on October 5, 1970, coming out of a Supremes song into the ad for that Wednesday's race.[^li01] Gabriel died in January 2010, at 69, at home in Lombard. It was, Burgess noted, a Sunday.[^nhra10]

The great ones did run. Lost Indiana names Don Garlits, Don Schumacher and Chris Karamesines.[^li01] Ron Pellegrini wrote in 2000 that, to the best of his knowledge, he built the first car known as a funny car, with a tube chassis, an all-glass body and a supercharged engine, and that he first ran it the third week of May 1965 at "U.S. 30 Drag strip in Gary, Indiana." He said he has the timing slips.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup>[^rp00] David Heacock, who raced there as a teenager in the 1960s, remembered jet cars, a rocket car that went 320 mph and a rocket-powered go-cart. "They'd race anything," he said.[^ap12]

John Sholty of Peru, Ind., wrote in 2011 about a Sunday in 1969 when Eddie Schartman's Cougar broke its transmission before a match race with Terry Hedrick's Supernova. So the track put the Nova against *No Big Thing*, a little Vespa with a 427, and the Nova spotted it the whole tree. By Sholty's memory, *No Big Thing* was past half-track when the Nova's last green lit, and the Nova still blew by it, at about seven seconds flat and 202 miles per hour, he thought.[^hmm11]

Everyone else came to be seen. Lost Indiana says fans often came in their own cars, worked on all week in garages and driveways for the "big show."[^li01] Chris DeYoung of Glenwood, Ill., raced there at 21 in the 1960s. "We'd live in the back of our trucks for the three-day meets," he said in 2012. "I remember taking baths in the creek."[^jj12] Milt Janosz came with his big brother, who raced, from the south Chicago suburbs. "I remember walking around and seeing all the cars and the smells of it. It was like seeing Hot Wheel cars full size."[^ap12] The goodie booth on the south side of the track was run by Mrs. Julie "Ma" Wright, whose home was across Clay Street from the pit entrance. Robert "Bobby" Kerr was night watchman and groundskeeper for more than 35 years, and lived in a trailer in the old parking lot.[^li01]

## The last season

The end came from several directions at once. Byrd says the neighbors' complaints brought a noise ordinance aimed at jet cars: a special permit for each event, four a year, and a 10 p.m. curfew.[^ldb] In 1984 Ruth Tice sold the AHRA in mid-season, and U.S. 30 joined other tracks that split off to form the American Drag Racing Association, which ran the rest of that year there. The operators tried to renegotiate their lease and could not, Lost Indiana says, because the landowner wanted the ground for development.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>[^li01] *The Times* put it as a leasing dispute and complaints about noise, traffic and safety.[^nwi05] The strip closed in the fall of 1984.[^ap12]

Hobart annexed the land in 1994.[^li01] That December Andy Tancos of Valparaiso, for the owner, Benjamin Christ of Bear Inc., asked the city council for a conditional use permit to reopen. Dozens of residents filled the room to protest, and the council said no unanimously.[^ap12] Investors had failed to raise the money in 1991.[^nwi05] In 2005 Mike Rachanski told the Plan Commission he wanted to bring it back with races, a repair shop and a museum; the ground was zoned residential.[^nwi05]

The racing stopped. The people did not. In 2001 the pavement was still there, with the AHRA logo between the lanes and the empty goodie booth on the south side. Kerr's trailer was still in the lot. He died on August 21, 2008.[^li01] Heacock, called the keeper of U.S. 30 memorabilia, filled a small barn and a garage on his Porter Township property with trophies, photos and clippings, and he usually had *No Big Thing* for his display, though Charlie Poague of Portage owned it.[^ap12][^hmm11] Jerry Trump still got calls from racers who wanted pictures of their cars.[^ap12] At the 2012 reunion in Schererville, Tony and Sue VerHulst brought *Old Injun*, a 1956 Pontiac with a 389 that had nearly 300 trophies by 1975. They had driven all the way to Oklahoma to meet its first owner.[^jj12] Christ, then 80 and living in Honolulu, said he had sold the property four years before. "There will always be an interest as long as there are automobiles and young people," he said.[^ap12]

The racing ended in 1984. What kept U.S. 30 was its people: a photographer who remembers cars instead of names, a keeper with a barn full of trophies, and a radio ad that told Chicago where to be on Sunday.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **1957, or as early as 1954?** Byrd quotes the *Drag News* of September 21, 1957 on a grand opening the next day, and says local racers told him the track was open before that.[^ldb] *The Times* (2012) says it opened in 1957.[^ap12] Lost Indiana says it was open by 1957, "perhaps as early as 1954," and Janosz writes that "some say" 1954.[^li01][^jj12] A 1954–57 Gary *Post-Tribune* or Hammond *Times* story would settle it. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Who owned the land, and why did it close?** Lost Indiana says the operators could not renegotiate their lease because the landowner wanted the land for development, and Byrd says the landowner did not renew the lease at the end of 1984.[^li01][^ldb] Heacock said it closed mainly after neighbors complained about the noise; *The Times* in 2005 said a leasing dispute and complaints about noise, traffic and safety.[^ap12][^nwi05] Yet the 1994 permit request was made for "owner Benjamin Christ, of Bear Inc.," and Christ said in 2012 that he had sold the property four years before. Byrd says it changed hands about a decade after 1994.[^ap12][^ldb] Whether Christ owned the ground in 1984, or bought it later, is open. Lake County deed records would answer it. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **The first funny car?** The claim, the date (the third week of May 1965) and the timing slips are Pellegrini's own, in his 2000 post; Lost Indiana and Janosz repeat that he "claims" it.[^rp00][^li01][^jj12] He places the strip in Gary. Who ran the first funny car is argued all over the country, and we have not seen the slips. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **One voice for both Sundays?** The 50 auditions, the two-announcer script and the tape delay come from Cronen, as Burgess reports him in 2010.[^nhra10] The [Oswego](oswego-drag-raceway-illinois.md) page credits Gabriel's WLS ads for that strip too, which fits Christ running both. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
