# Bonds From the Car Show: Minnesota Dragways and the Gopher State Timing Association (1953–76)

*The clubs earned the strip a meeting at a time, and paid toward it out of car-show money*

## Off the overpass

The Twin Cities' drag strip was not handed to the clubs. They spent five years earning it: meetings in a police precinct, rallies that obeyed every speed limit, a poll of their own traffic tickets. Then a service club built it, and the clubs bought bonds with their car-show money to help pay for it. It lasted until the suburb around it filled in.

The first try came before any of that. In 1953 a Gofur Timing Association held drags on an abandoned stretch of road south of Highway 10, between Elk River and Big Lake. It was narrow and had a curve in the shutdown. The timing was a stopwatch at the finish line, started by hand when the flag dropped. The races stopped after a fast '36 Ford coupe rolled while trying to stop, and because the Highway Patrol objected to the cars parked on the overpass to watch.[^gsta] The racing moved to the old University of Minnesota airport, across Highway 8 from Twin City Speedway in Lexington, until the university sent its police to end it.[^gsta]

In 1954 Captain Clifford Bailey of the Northside police precinct brought the clubs together, and the first meetings were held in the precinct itself. Twenty-five clubs answered, from the Road Knights and Throttle Jammers to the Bumper Buddies and the Dragons. They elected John Foster president, and he was re-elected through 1966.[^gsta] Foster was from LeMars, Iowa, and had come to Minnesota in 1946. He joined the Strokers as an adviser in 1954, and the club's 1957 history says he also found time to build "the most sanitary Merc in this part of the country."[^gsta]

## Proving it, one run at a time

The Gopher State Timing Association decided that rushing a strip was folly and that it needed a sponsor, so the work was public relations.[^gsta] Its first reliability run, in the early spring of 1955, sent 54 cars about 50 miles from the Columbia Heights Chalet to Faribault. Breaking a traffic law cost points, and Bob Bishop of the Bumper Buddies won.[^gsta] The Road Angels and Bumper Buddies ran an ice-racing track in winter. On Heart Sunday of 1957 they gave the whole gate, about $140, to the Heart Fund.[^gsta] In 1956 the association polled its clubs and found that members averaged less than half as many traffic tickets after joining as before.[^gsta]

At the 1957 banquet, with Chet Herringer and Shorty Booth of the Optimists Club in the room, it was announced that the drag strip would be a reality as soon as suitable land was found. That summer the Minneapolis Optimists said the land was located, "about four miles north of Anoka," with parking for about two thousand cars and a start "by Labor Day." The profits, once there were any, were to go to the Optimists' youth work.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^gsta] A later GSTA booklet says the meeting was arranged by Clarence "Shorty" Booth, a shop teacher at Vocational High School. It says the association, "through income from its car shows, bought bonds to help finance the building of the strip."[^erk]

## The Fastest Piece of Real Estate in Minnesota

Minnesota Dragways opened just outside Coon Rapids on June 28, 1959.[^srh] The booklet gives its motto: "The Fastest Piece of Real Estate in Minnesota."[^erk] John Evans, a volunteer with the Anoka County Historical Society, wrote in 2013 that the developers "seemed to have chosen the flattest, sandiest and most treeless swatch of Anoka County they could find." The entrance was near today's Main Street and Avocet, and the finish line near today's county compost site.[^abc13] The local racers ran first, in some of the same cars they drove up and down Anoka's main drag on Saturday nights, and many fans skipped the bleachers to stand at the chain-link fence by the finish. Don Prudhomme and Tom McEwen were sometimes on the bill.[^abc13]

A sign where the cars turned onto the strip read "McPheeters Lane" on one side and "Kirkbride Lane" on the other. In 2011 a racer on *Nitromater* asked what the names meant. The answer that came back, credited to Ron Johnson, was that the Optimist Club owned the track and that Doc McPheeters and Leo Kirkbride were two of its leaders who "Got 'er done."<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup> Another poster still had a Champion Spark Plugs "Welcome Race Fans" banner that Doc McPheeters had given him off a fence in '76, when he was a kid.[^nm11]

The racers' families came too. In May 1967 *The Minneapolis Star* wrote about Mrs. Charles Johnson, 22, of Chicago Avenue. Her husband's Sunday schedule read "7:30 a.m. to 6:30 p.m. – Minnesota Dragways, Coon Rapids." She raced in the Sunday powder puff derby and won her first trophy of that season two weeks before her baby was due. "The doctor knew I was racing and didn't say anything against it," she said, so she would race "as long as I could fit behind the steering wheel." Of her husband, an industrial spray painter for Control Data who had organized the 3-Two's Club at 15, she said: "He taught me how to line up the car, watch the lights and where to shift."[^erk] The association's own gas dragster crew, the booklet says, won "Best Appearing Crew" at the Nationals at Indy.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^erk]

The biggest crowd came in July 1972, when Evel Knievel jumped three vans and eight cars, less than two months before his Snake River Canyon attempt. About thirty thousand people came. A woman eight months pregnant passed out in the heat and came to in her car in time to hear a loudspeaker announcement about the "lady who needed assistance." Knievel came to her window himself to ask if she was OK. She was, but she never got back in to see the jump.[^abc13]

By then the end had a date. *BangShift* wrote in 2019 that the operators had agreed in 1970 to a six-year timeline to closure, and that noise and crowding had caught up with the place.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^bs19] The last flag dropped in 1976. For years the tower could still be seen from Main Street. Now it is a quiet neighborhood, and a drive north up Avocet Street covers about the same stretch the racers ran.[^abc13][^nm11] The clubs had started racing on a road with a curve in it, and they ended with a strip they had helped pay for. The town grew up around it, and that was the end of it.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Labor Day 1957 north of Anoka, or June 1959 in Coon Rapids?** The 1957 "GSTA Story" put the new strip "about four miles north of Anoka" and said it was "to be running by Labor Day."[^gsta] The strip that opened on June 28, 1959, sat on the Coon Rapids side, by Main Street.[^srh][^abc13] Whether the 1957 site fell through, or the plan simply took two more years, the sources we read do not say. A tribute to John and Margie Foster in the later booklet, which we saw only as a search-engine excerpt and could not read in full, appears to give 1958 for the opening; we have not used it. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Were the lanes named for two Optimists?** Only the 2011 *Nitromater* thread says so, and the answer there is relayed secondhand ("Answer provide by Ron Johnson").[^nm11] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Did the GSTA crew win "Best Appearing Crew" at Indy?** The booklet's 1960s chapter, by Charlie Johnson, says so and gives no year.[^erk] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Was the closing agreed in 1970?** Only *BangShift* says the operators "agreed to put themselves on a six year timeline to closure" in 1970, and it does not name the other party.[^bs19] A seller's listing of a track trophy says the last race was in November 1976, "because of municipal growth," which we have not used. The Coon Rapids city council minutes for 1970 would settle it. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
