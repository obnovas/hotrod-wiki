# Help Them Get a Drag Strip: Connecticut's Hot Rod Clubs and the Road to East Haddam (1954–63)

*Before Connecticut had a legal strip, its hot rodders handed out Halloween doughnuts, held polio dances and asked their neighbors for one*

## Holiday 88s on Main Street

In May 1954 the *East Hartford Gazette* scolded "East Hartford's finest" for letting two Oldsmobiles drag up Main Street. The hot rod fever, the paper said, "has spread from owners of souped up Model Ts with twin pipes, superchargers and overdrives to owners of Holiday 88s." A green Holiday and a brand new robin's egg blue one had come off the toll gate jockeying for position, then raced north up Main Street to the Town Hall.[^ehg540506]

That was the reputation the clubs set out to live down. In November 1955 the Road Knights, "a well known teenage drivers group who take pride in keeping their hot rods in A-1 condition," held a benefit dance at the Sunset Ridge Clubhouse for two schoolmates who had polio. They did it, the *Gazette* said, "in an effort to bring credit to the oft-maligned hotrodders on the road." High school officials approved; tickets came from Sal Dignoti or at the door.[^ehg551117]

## Show season at the Armory

In February 1958 several hundred young hot rodders revved their souped-up Fords in and out of the Armory parking lot at Hartford's eighth annual National Autorama. The *Gazette*'s reviewer thought the classics and rods outshone the customs, most of which he found "done in bad taste." The hot rods came in two kinds, "the 'street machine' and the out and out dragster," and the street machines were mostly "1929 to 1935 Ford coupes and roadsters fitted with anything from the original engine to a late model 420 hp Chrysler mill."[^ehg580227] That September, at the Cherry Park Fair in Avon, the hot rod exhibits were to be climaxed by "The Carps," a local hot rod club that would put together a completely dismantled automobile in 20 minutes.[^ehg580904]

A year later the Autorama came back to the Hartford Armory, and Newington sent Inga Dennis of 1268 Main Street, crowned "Miss Hot Rod of Conn." at Hartford's First Hot Rod Jamboree in 1958, to stand at the Nutmeg State Timing Association's booth. The new association, *The Newington Citizen* explained, had been set up "to gather all organized Hot Rod Clubs of Connecticut together under one head so that they can work as one body to better safety and hot rodding in the state."[^nc590305a]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup>

## "Help them get a drag strip"

On the back page of the same issue, Newington's own club, the Ringbusters, made its case. Club members did not tear up and down the streets, they said; a member who did would lose his membership. They helped stranded motorists and assisted the police with safety checks. But they could not test their cars "as is done in almost every state in the union," under police and National Hot Rod Association supervision. "Consequently, we are forced to go to neighboring states in order to compete in this activity." They asked the town for three things: "Help support your local Hot Rod Organization 'The Ringbusters'. Help them get a drag strip in Connecticut and make this show a terrific success with a large turnout of Newington people."[^nc590305b] The Ringbusters were already a fixture in town: that Halloween they handed out cider and doughnuts in front of the Town Hall, and in January they joined the Lions and the Sportsmen's Club for a clean-up and painting day at the Lions clubhouse.[^nc581106][^nc590122]

The idea reached town halls. In April 1959 an East Hartford councilman, Kay Ryalls, proposed a drag strip, and the council sent the proposal to the Police Board.[^ehg590430] Along the Berlin Turnpike in Newington, which the *Town Crier* later said had been "cited in the past as a speedway for hot rodding teena[gers]," Don's Automotive Specialties advertised "Speed and Hot Rod parts" in 1960, and by 1961 the shop's ad billed it as "Connecticut's Pioneer Dragger."[^tc600203][^tc610510][^tc621114]

## East Haddam, 1961

The man most often credited with getting the strip built is Frank Maratta, a Hartford body-shop owner and car-show promoter. As tech advisor for the Hartford Hot Rod Council, the New England Auto Racers hall of fame says, "he led the efforts that eventually resulted in the 1961 opening of the Connecticut Dragway."[^near] *Hot Rod* writes that he worked "with the NHRA, local police, and the newly formed Nutmeg State Timing Association," and that before then the only usable strips in New England were converted airfields at Charlestown, Rhode Island; Orange, Massachusetts; and Sanford, Maine.[^hr19] (Sanford's story is told in [Sanford's Hurri-Cain](sanford-cain-bannister.md).) His Model A coupe, the Rake, was meant to show what a safe, show-quality hot rod looked like; it won him two New England A/Gas championships.[^near][^hr19]

The strip was meant for Colchester. A Connecticut hot rodder found a *Hartford Times* page from January 26, 1959, in an old box of canning jars, with a short piece on an upcoming zoning meeting for the drag strip. A lifelong Colchester resident who grew up within hearing of the track remembered that the Colchester board "eventually said 'no', in part because of the expected traffic it would draw," and that Maratta had another parcel just over the line in East Haddam: "East Haddam got the tax money, Colchester got the traffic."[^hamb]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup> Ever after, the strip was usually described as being in Colchester; a 1998 state report put it there too.[^olr98]

On June 1, 1961, the *Gazette* promoted the June issue of *Connecticut Life*, which took readers to East Haddam, where "vivid pictures show motor-happy youth roaring down the state's first legal drag strip at speeds up to 147 miles per hour."[^ehg610601]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> Not everyone was pleased with the new spirit of speed. That September a letter writer demanding sidewalks complained that the town would rather "provide super drag strips for every hot rod monster in the area."[^ehg610921]

## Still explaining, 1963

Even with a strip, the clubs were still explaining themselves. In December 1963 the newly organized Eliminators Auto Club of East Hartford wrote to the *Gazette* about "the true meaning of organized hot rodding." They thanked everyone who had bought a ticket in their gas raffle (20 gallons, won by William Hannon of 142 Country Lane) "without really understanding what the purpose of our club really was," and added: "We hope that in the future our club members will not be confused with the careless drivers with hopped up cars that overrun the streets of our town."[^ehg631205]

The strip closed in 1979, reopened in 1980 as Connecticut International Raceway, and closed for good after the 1985 season,[^olr98][^ch14] though one 2013 report gives 1986.[^rdct13]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> In 1998 a state legislative analyst reported that Connecticut, which "had one of the first commercial drag racing facilities in the northeast," had no drag strip at all; its racers had moved on to New England Dragway in New Hampshire, Lebanon Valley in New York and Englishtown in New Jersey.[^olr98]

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Did Connecticut Dragway close in 1985 or 1986?** The 1998 General Assembly report says it ran "until it closed in 1985,"[^olr98] and Chevy Hardcore (2014) says it "closed forever after the 1985 season."[^ch14] RaceDayCT (2013) says it "operated from 1961 until 1986."[^rdct13] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When in 1961 did the strip open?** *Hot Rod* (2019) says it opened "in the spring";[^hr19] Chevy Hardcore (2014) says it "was started in June of 1961."[^ch14] The *Gazette*'s June 1 notice of *Connecticut Life* pictures already showing cars on the strip suggests racing had begun by late May.[^ehg610601] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Did Colchester turn the strip down?** The refusal, and the traffic reason for it, rest on one resident's memory in a 2020 forum thread; the *Hartford Times* clipping he was commenting on only announced a zoning meeting.[^hamb] The Colchester zoning minutes or the *Times* and *Courant* for early 1959 would settle it. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** **Who founded the Nutmeg State Timing Association, and when?** It was "newly formed" by early 1959,[^nc590305a][^hr19] but we have no founding date, officers or member club list yet. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
