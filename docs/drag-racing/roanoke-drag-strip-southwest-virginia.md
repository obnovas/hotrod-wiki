# Courtesy Cards and an Uphill Shutdown: The Roanoke Drag Strip and Southwest Virginia's Clubs (1950–1971)

*Clubs that met in police stations, a strip whose shutdown ran uphill, a Super Stock protest settled with a wrench, and a parts catalog from Chattanooga*

## Before there was a strip

Southwest Virginia had hot rods long before it had a place to run them. The Blue Ridge Institute & Museum at Ferrum College, which collected the region's racing history, says the earliest clubs were the Gas Gobblers (Roanoke, 1950), the Virginia Wheelers (Roanoke, 1953), and the Ridge Runners and the Appalachian Motor Club, both in the Abingdon area in 1955; more than 30 clubs formed in the region in the 1950s. The same exhibit's club list dates the Appalachian Motor Club to 1956.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Open question: see Open questions">?</a></sup>[^bri5]

They met in private garages, gas stations, community halls "and even police stations," and each club had a public spot too, usually a gas station or a drive-in, where members gathered and where anyone after a late-night street race would cruise by to find a challenger. Dues, car washes and dances paid the bills. Worried about what the public thought of hot rodders, some clubs sent speakers to civic meetings, and members carried “courtesy cards” which they gave to stranded motorists they helped on the highways.[^bri5]

The parts came the hard way. With no local speed shops, the museum says, early Southwest Virginia hot rodders used junkyard parts or ordered by mail, and "the regional mail-order 'bible' for the region's car builders was the catalog from Honest Charley's Speed Shop in Chattanooga, Tennessee." (That shop's story is on the [Honest Charley](../companies/honest-charley-speed-shop.md) page.) A few high school auto shops built hot rods as class projects. The regional vocabulary included a "drive-in cam," in which "the driver pulls out the choke so that the motor sounds as if it has a racing cam."[^bri1][^bri6]

To race on a real strip, they towed. Before organized drag racing came to the region, its hot rodders went to Burlington and Elizabeth City, N.C., and to Manassas, Va.; the Abingdon Ridge Runners sometimes towed "as far away as Chester, South Carolina," to the strip told on the [Chester](chester-drag-strip-south-carolina.md) page.[^bri2]

## June 1958 at Starkey

The Roanoke Drag Strip opened in June 1958. The Roanoke public library's Virginia Room files a photograph as "The Roanoke Drag Strip at Starkey Speedway. The dragstrip opened in June 1958," and the museum's list of the region's strips gives June 29, 1958, and NHRA.[^vroom][^bri3] Starkey, in southwestern Roanoke County, had been a racing ground since November 1950, when its quarter-mile track opened with an auto race; it seated 4,000, and that May it had hosted its first NASCAR Grand National race, won by Jim Reed.[^harris]

The first drag race, the museum says, drew more than 100 cars, a third of them hot rods and two-thirds unmodified stock cars, and more than 2,000 spectators.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup> A club ran it. A 1959 photograph caption in the exhibit says the Roanoke Dragsters "ran the track for the owners the first three years of its operation," and an umbrella group, the Eastern Auto Club Council, formed partly to help: the Loafers and the Eliminators of Salem, the Road Rebels of Welch, W.Va., the Ridge Runners of Roanoke County and Roanoke's Dixie Rebels.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^bri2][^bri5]

Other counties followed the same pattern. The Lakeview Dragsters ran the Lakeview Drag Strip near Bassett, in Henry County, whose first full season was 1958–59, and the Quarter Milers ran Oak Level, opened in 1960. In Alleghany County, Callaghan Drag Strip opened on March 8, 1959, and Hidden Valley on April 18, 1959; before Hidden Valley was finished, the drags there were held at the regional airport.[^bri3][^bri5][^bri2]

## "The car to beat"

Racers' memories give the place its shape. "I remembered it because of the fact that the shut down area was uphill and the return lane was a dirt road," Glenn Goss wrote. In 1958 Bob Bateman handed Dick Swecker a trophy there; Swecker's Chrysler-powered rail dragster set the most top-speed records in its class at Roanoke. Howard Linkletter of Staunton came with a chopped 1931 Ford coupe on a Cadillac engine.[^dslva][^bri3][^bri2]

By 1960 the fight was in Super Stock, and everyone remembered one name. Sandra Thomas Atkinson won Powder Puff trophies at Roanoke in 1960–61 in a 1960 green Ford called the "Green Monster," owned by Lynwood Craft of Roanoke, who "worked on his own car and maintained it to run." Mike Smith, who ran a '61 Ford to a best of 12.01 at 115 mph in 1961 and 1962, put it plainly: "Lynwood Craft was the car to beat in those days. in a '60 Ford."<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-5"><a href="#q-5" title="Open question: see Open questions">?</a></sup> Nick Smith, in a 1960 HiPo Ford Starliner, finally beat him, and was protested for it. He tore the engine down, was found legal, and spent three hours putting it back together. "It was worth it."<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^dslva]

Mike Smith also raced at Lakeview in 1961, where "the quarter mile was asphalt, but then it was dirt and gravel, tricky stopping." He wrote of dry ice packed in a metal box over the fuel pump to fight vapor lock, five sets of tires in a season, and a Sunday when a never-beaten Pontiac from North Carolina came up and lost to him on a track that was "dry, but the gravel was half mud." The runs, he added, "were started by a flagman."[^dslva] The museum says the flagmen "were often showmen with dramatic styles," until Christmas trees and timing lights replaced them.[^bri2]

## Big names, and the road behind the airport

The owners learned to sell tickets with outsiders. Southwest Virginia strips brought in Don Garlits, "TV" Tommy Ivo, E. J. Potter on his V-8 motorcycle, Art Arfons's jet-powered Green Monster and, at Roanoke, Richard Petty in his Plymouth Barracuda, "43 Jr." When Bristol opened in 1961, the region's fans could watch nationally ranked racers without leaving home, and in the 1960s, the museum says, the crowd's favorites shifted from dragsters, roadsters, coupes and gas-class cars to factory Super Stocks.[^bri2]

The strips never ended the street racing. "Be it the 220 straightaway in southern Henry County, Gratton Road in Tazewell County, or the airport road outside of Bluefield, nearly every Southwest Virginia community of any size has had—and probably still has—an illegal drag race site."[^bri2] Many of the clubs faded in the 1960s as the factories built muscle cars and the builders of the 1940s and 1950s took on jobs and families.[^bri5]

Starkey outlived its NASCAR years. Its last Cup race was in August 1964, local drivers kept running there afterward, and "the speedway closed for good in 1971." Ball fields cover the ground now. Not every strip's ending is so well documented: of Big Al's Dragway in Richlands, a granddaughter of its owners, Jerry and Norma Richardson, wrote that it was named for their son Allen, and that "the land is completely residential now."[^harris][^dslva]
