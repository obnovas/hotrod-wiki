# So All My Friends Can Drag Away: Great Lakes Dragaway and Wisconsin's Club-Built Strips (Union Grove, 1955– )

*A word coined for friends, ditches dug on Saturday mornings, a tower built from scrounged wood, and a dragster called the Lucky Ladybug*

## Drag away

The name was a joke between friends. Marge Bennett, RACER reported in 2017, was working in California around the Second World War when she first saw drag racing. When she came home to Wisconsin she and her husband, Lynn, decided to buy land for a strip, and the present co-owner, Randy Henning, remembers how she put it: "We're going to build a drag strip so all my friends can just drag away."[^racer17] *Competition Plus* tells the same arc: the Bennetts saw "hot rod fever" overtake Southern California's youth after the war, bought farmland on the Kenosha/Racine county line, and enlisted "over a dozen Milwaukee area clubs" to help build it. When it was finished in 1955, the clubs ran the racing as the Great Lakes Timing Association.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^cp20] WUWM calls it "now the oldest continuously operating dragstrip in the United States"; a Kentucky strip says the same of itself.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^wuwm]

That is why it is a Drag*away*, not a Drag*way*, and why it is not quite in Union Grove. The track is in the town of Paris; Union Grove is where the post office box is. "It's really in Paris," Henning said, "but our racetrack is strong."[^racer17]

## The Herriges brothers

A Cudahy family tells it from the ground up. "The idea of the drag strip came from Harlyn," his widow, Sue Herriges, told WUWM in 2016.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup> Harlyn Herriges was working with police officers in Cudahy when street racing became a problem; he met with the sheriff and organized what WUWM, telling the family's story, calls "the first official drag race held in Wisconsin," at Timmerman Airport in Milwaukee.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup> It was a one-time race. Officials looked at the east side of Mitchell Field for a permanent strip, and dropped the plan when Union Grove announced its own.[^wuwm]

So the Herriges brothers went to Union Grove to help. Russell remembered Saturday mornings digging ditches and laying drainage pipe. "Of course the dragstrip needed to have an announcing tower," Sue said. "Harlyn and his brother Dave and Russ built the announcing tower. I don't know where the wood came from, but knowing Harlyn, he may have scrounged it up." Harlyn raced his own dragster, the *Lucky Ladybug*; Russ did safety checks; they all took turns announcing; Sue raced once ("It was an exciting race, and I came in second"); and Russ's wife, Claudette, climbed into the announcing tower while she was pregnant.[^wuwm]

It ended with a death. A man was killed in a car that Russ had inspected and passed, and for the family that was the end of the Dragaway. They turned to musical theater and founded the Franklin Players, later the Suburban Players. To Sue's knowledge Harlyn never went back, and neither did Russ. "I'm glad that that happened," she said of the strip, "because that dragstrip made a lot of people happy."[^wuwm]

## Club signs by the tower

The clubs never really left. A September 1961 photograph from the track's archives shows a dragster passing the old mid-track tower with "signs for many of the car clubs active at the time" beside it.[^cp20] In 2011 Wisconsin Hot Rod Radio went looking for the original club members in southeastern Wisconsin, recording interviews and scanning membership items, with help from the historian Kurt Rymkus and members of the Artist Rod & Kustom, Mellotones and Cruisn' Angels clubs; its notes call the track the place that "was started to get those hot rod kids of the 50's off the streets."[^whrr] In 2019 a plaque honoring one of the founding clubs, the Milwaukee Artists Rod and Custom Club, with an original club plate and the members' names, went up in the track's general store, with several members' descendants on hand.[^cp20]

The racers remember the rest. Mark Johnson saw Bob Smith's *Untouchable* match-race Chris Karamesines's *Chizler* at Union Grove in 1962: "I've been a Greek fan ever since!" Vince Seliga, sponsored by Dan Miller Dodge of Zion, Illinois, hit as many strips as he could "to keep our sponsor happy." Bob Pavlovich won his class all four days of the 1967 NASCAR Nationals in an injected '60 Corvette; his tow bar broke on the way, so he drove the Corvette the rest of the way, and when a man in a Beetle cut across the staging lanes and hit it, Pavlovich found him afterward "trying to look inconspicuous hiding under a table" at the snack bar. "We worked it out."[^dslwi] The flashiest promoter, RACER says, was "Broadway Bob" Metzler, in wild sunglasses, airbrushed T-shirts and Zumba pants, who brought in concerts, parades, the circus and Evel Knievel.[^racer17] When a flood buried the strip in mud, both towns turned out with a fire truck and more than 100 volunteers with squeegees.[^racer17]

## Up the state

Wisconsin's other strips were smaller and stranger. At the Lake Geneva Speed Center, which Jerry Richards remembered as an airstrip in 1964, "Several times I could see a plane very close in my rear view mirror as I approached the lights"; the planes carried skydivers. The manager had a new maroon Cobra and asked Richards's crew to run him even up.[^dslwi] In the northwest, the Twin Ports Drag Racing Association came down from Duluth "every Sunday" to Tenora Drag Strip at Comstock, Ronald McDonald wrote; one of its racers was Rod Anderson, father of the Pro Stock champion Greg Anderson. A caption on the club's jacket patch says the association was organized in 1971 and in 1974 began looking into a strip eight miles south of Superior near the Apollo Bar, "selling shares of stock for $25 per share" to build it.[^dslwi] At Eau Claire, Dr. Ralph Frank opened Amber Green Dragways in June 1969; it became a home for Twin Cities racers as Minnesota's strips closed.[^cp20] Years later an Appleton paper called the Fox Valley's [KK Sports Arena](kk-sports-arena-kaukauna.md) the state's second strip, in a week when Union Grove was holding its eighth annual national meet.[^kk]
