# Run at Your Own Risk: George Ray's Wildcat Hot Rod Drag Strip, Paragould, Ark. (1961– )

*A man who didn't like a Tennessee strip built his own, and ran it his way until he died*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium (the building and the layout come from the 2005–06 National Register nomination, which drew on e-mails with Ray and a friend; the rest comes from two local TV reports, a funeral-home obituary and a forum post; the length of the concrete and the reason Ray left Tennessee are told two ways) · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> National Register of Historic Places registration form (Arkansas Historic Preservation Program); obituary (Heath Funeral Home, 2009); local TV news (KAIT, Jonesboro, 2009 and 2011); a tourism-association page; BangShift (2009); H.A.M.B. forum (2014, 2018) · **Last verified:** 2026-11-14 (Round 41) · **Open issues:** ⚠ 2,960 feet or 2,600 feet of concrete; ⚠ why Ray left Halls; ◐ the prize fighter; ◐ the pith helmet · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "What he always told us was that he didn't like the way a lot of other operators run their tracks so he built his own."

    — Tom Cray, who raced there from the first season, to KAIT, July 14, 2011.[^kait11]

## A bad day at Halls

Most strips of the early 1960s have been abandoned, enlarged or torn down. George Ray's, in Paragould, is still the strip he built, and the National Register of Historic Places calls it "virtually unaltered." It exists because one man was unhappy with another track.[^nrhp]

Ray was born on July 8, 1924, in southeastern Missouri. The National Register nomination says that as a young man he was a prize fighter, and "supposedly" quit after seeing a man beaten to death in a money fight in Portageville, Mo.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup> He worked as a lumberman and drove a Caterpillar tractor. In the 1940s he went to work for Dr Pepper in Paragould, and through the 1940s and 1950s he worked on other people's cars and did some hot rodding of his own.[^nrhp]

In the late 1950s he drove to the airport track at Halls, Tenn., about 65 miles northeast of Memphis, the nearest one to Paragould. He did not have a good experience. The nomination says he was unhappy with how the operators treated him.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^nrhp] A 2009 KAIT report says he began racing in Tennessee and built his own strip when he realized the track there favored certain drivers.[^kait09] So he went home to Paragould and bought a plot of ground east of town.[^nrhp]

## Concrete, a catwalk and his own rules

Local politicians tried to stop the strip. The nomination says county and city officials "fought to stop the track's construction and operation, but to no avail."[^nrhp] What Ray built was a concrete strip, 2,960 feet long and 31 feet wide, by the nomination's figures.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^nrhp] A catwalk on log piers crossed the track at the starting line, so spectators could look down on the cars. There was a plain ticket booth and a small concession stand. The track opened to the public in November 1961, and the first races were a quarter mile.[^nrhp] Ray added a race booth on the catwalk around 1967, and bleachers in 1972. As cars got faster and needed more room to stop, the race distance was shortened, first to 1,000 feet and then to an eighth of a mile. The season has run from the first Sunday in March to the third Sunday in October, and the nomination notes the track has run continuously since opening.[^nrhp]

The rules were Ray's. KAIT reported in 2009 that cars raced by engine size, horsepower and the like, and that he made his own rules.[^kait09] In 2011 Tom Cray, who drove an orange 1940 Willys coupe there and started when the track opened, said Ray "classed the cars," made sure racers were running safe and "pretty much run the rules." The signs said to race at your own risk.[^kait11] There was no Christmas tree at first. Steve "Pop" Wallin, who came in 1962, said Ray stood out between two cars with a checkered flag. "And he'd point at you and point at the other car and then he'd throw that flag just like it started in the 50's." A man in a booth at the far end turned on a light for the winner, or Ray walked into the winning lane and waved the flag.[^kait11]

One H.A.M.B. poster, who remembered going as a youngster, wrote that he saw Ray in a pith helmet, running between the cars as they launched and spinning around to see the finish. He also remembered an NHRA inspector listing everything wrong with a white hot rod and calling it unsafe. It was Ray's personal car, and Ray told the inspector it had "only gone down that track a 1000 times."<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^hamb18] Ray raced himself. His dragster was parked behind the starting line in 2011 beside an empty lawn chair.[^kait11]

## Three generations in the stands

Wallin told KAIT that his father had brought him to the strip to get him to stop racing on the streets, and that he later brought his own children, starting in tenth grade. Trey Williams, 17, was a second-generation racer who had been coming since he was a year old. Cray ran against the clock beside him in a time trial, the oldest racer next to one of the youngest.[^kait11]

George Ray died on December 5, 2009, at 85. His obituary says he was the owner and operator of the drag strip for 49 years, and that memorials could go to Cars for Kids.[^obit] His wife, Bonnie, kept running the track.[^kait11] BangShift wrote that he had "cemented himself as a drag racing legend by maintaining everything, even the rules at the strip, as if it were still the early 1960s."[^bs09] By 2011 the strip was an eighth of a mile, with the walkway over the track and many of the older buildings still there.[^kait11] Cray told the station it was "the only game in town." Ray had gone to a strip he didn't like, come home and built one. Many of the first-generation strips in his region, the nomination says, have since been abandoned, left to fall apart or torn down. His is still the one he poured.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **How long was the concrete?** The National Register nomination gives 2,960 feet long and 31 feet wide.[^nrhp] KAIT's 2011 report says Ray "poured 2600 feet of concrete in a 30 foot wide strip" and opened in November 1961.[^kait11] The nomination also says the first races were a quarter mile and were later shortened to 1,000 feet and then an eighth, while KAIT's 2011 story says only that it "was a quarter mile for one thing." <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Why did Ray leave Halls?** The nomination says only that he "did not have a good experience" and disliked how the operators treated him. It adds that "one version of the story" has him telling the track's promoter that Arkansas racers were not being treated fairly, after which, by that version, local police told him later that day he was no longer welcome as a racer in Tennessee. That version is credited to a Geoff Stunkard piece, "Nitro Hallucination," which we have not read.[^nrhp] KAIT's 2009 report says the tracks there favored certain drivers.[^kait09] Cray's 2011 version is that Ray did not like how other operators ran their tracks.[^kait11] These are three descriptions of one grievance, and none gives a date. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **The prize fighter.** The nomination's biography of Ray, including the Portageville fight, rests on e-mails between Ray, a friend and the nomination's author, and the nomination itself says "supposedly." Nothing else we read mentions it.[^nrhp] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **The pith helmet and the NHRA inspector.** One H.A.M.B. post from 2018, by a member from Pocahontas, Ark., who wrote that he was a youngster when the strip had no Christmas tree. KAIT's account of Ray flagging the cars, with no helmet, does not contradict it.[^hamb18][^kait11] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
