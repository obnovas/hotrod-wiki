# Free Admission and a Hot Dog: Becky White's Years at Farmington Dragway (Mocksville, N.C., 1963– )

*A deal at the gate, a Royal typewriter, a velvet dress from a home economics class, and a track that told its neighbors no twice*

## The deal at the gate

Farmington Dragway opened in Davie County in 1963,<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup> and it has raced at a quarter mile, a fifth and now an eighth.[^racer17f] Becky White remembered the year for her own reason: "it opened in 1963…the same year my first daughter was born." She lived seven miles away, close enough that "if the wind was blowing in the right direction, I could hear the cars," and she had pictures of all three of her girls at the track before they were six months old.[^bw10]

In April 1979, after what she called several years of barely coping, she drove to the strip hoping it might help get her "out of the funk." Her friend Larry Allen was working the gate and pointed out the owners, Jerry Joyce and Norman Drouillard. She did not mean to make a deal, she wrote, but she heard herself make one: free admission every week in exchange for a race story in the local paper. "Five bucks I can use to pay on rent or groceries for my kids!" They said yes. Her girls were under sixteen and got in free too, "and I got a hot dog once a week!"[^bw10]

She was studying auto mechanics at Rowan Technical College at the time (Rowan Technical Institute, her résumé says), and was, as she put it, "A woman. Writing about drag racing? What a hoot!" Some people took a while to believe she "really wasn't there looking for a boyfriend or husband." She went home from the track and typed on "my old Royal typewriter my dad bought me about 1957 or '58," five or six versions of each story, "each with a different headline and each going to a different small town newspaper." She mailed them on Sunday and carried one to the *Davie County Enterprise* on Monday, deadline day. Her first big story was on Jack McClamrock, who had been her hero since she first saw him race, "probably in 1963." Drouillard published the *King Times-News*, so a lot of her work ran there.[^bw10][^bwacc]

## A velvet dress and the neighbors

The IHRA people noticed, and she was invited to the November banquet in Gatlinburg. She could not afford it. Drouillard gave her a check for $100, "A lot of money back then. But not enough." Her girls put in their allowance, Teresa made her a velvet dress as a home economics project, her mother bought dresses for the younger two, and Margie and Eldee Hutchins, whom she had written about that year, gave them a ride. "I thought I was, as the saying goes, pooping in high cotton."[^bw10]

In 1980 the track's neighbors tried to close it. She went to every meeting, spoke, and wrote up the meetings and "the way we 'racing folks' were being treated," and she believed the coverage shamed them into dropping it.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup> That year the track paid her "$15 a week and a hot dog!!!" Farmington gave her a Booster Award in 1981, the year she helped start *Quick Times Racing News* with Barry Jenkins in Pageland, South Carolina, and then ran it alone from that September.[^bw10][^bwacc]

## Banished, then thanked

It did not last at Farmington. One weekend in July 1981, when she got to the track, Drouillard said she would have to pay to get in. She was still working there. She left the kids at Farmington, all of them still under sixteen, and went to Shuffletown. "For the first 30 miles, I cried. Then I got mad." She wrote her first "nasty" editorial about a track operator, about him; after that she paid at the gate and stopped writing for the track. They did not speak again until about 1984, after she had spent months in *Quick Times* going after K&K Insurance, which she said "was killing the small tracks." In the pits at Rockingham that spring, she wrote, "Norman looked straight at me and said, 'I want to thank you for all the stuff you've been writing about our insurance problems, it has really helped all the small track operators!'" Drouillard died on December 8, 2009; her memorial post ends, "Thanks, Norman."[^bw10] Her own list of what came next includes a 1990 "Doorslammer" coloring book "with hand drawn pictures…ALL autographed by the drivers of the cars."[^bwacc]

The neighbors came back. In May 2011 the *Davie County Enterprise Record* reported that the dragway "opened in the early 1960s – before many of the people who complain about the current Farmington Motorsports Park moved here," and quoted the owners' lawyer, Grady McClamrock Jr.: "They moved in knowing the dragstrip was there."[^er11] By 2017 the race director and former co-owner was Mark Joyce, who claimed that "per square mile, North Carolina has more drag strips" than any other state.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^racer17f]

Farmington was not the Piedmont's first. Glenn Goss's aunt and uncle raced on the old Fairchild Airport at Burlington around 1952–53, beside the armory on Highway 70, where a Walmart stands now. Years later, teaching driver's education in Concord, Goss asked a student why her street was so wide; it had been the Concord Drag Strip, and he had been there as a teenager, "but had completely forgotten about it."[^dslnc]
