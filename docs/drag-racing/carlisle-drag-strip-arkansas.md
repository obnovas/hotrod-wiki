# Second Sunday at Carlisle: The Arkansas Timing Association's Bomber-Field Strip (1955–1970s)

*A runway built on Granite Mountain rock, a garage speed shop run by a railroad engineer, a rice field past the finish line, and a protest that ended at a tire iron*

    **Confidence:** Low (the long history is one racer's, written decades later; the airfield's wartime origin is confirmed by an encyclopedia entry, and other racers' and a spectator's memories agree on the strip and its touring names, but split on when it opened; no Arkansas newspaper page was read) · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> Paul Brower's history of the strip and its racers, and other recollections, on *Drag Strip List*; Lance Miller's memoir blog (*A Sequence of Events*); Wikipedia on Carlisle Municipal Airport · **Last verified:** 2026-11-09 (Round 36) · **Open issues:** ⚠ first meet April 1955, racing in 1957, or opening in summer 1958; ◐ the tire-iron protests; ◐ the Middle Eliminator challenge race · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

## Behind the airport

Before there was a strip, Little Rock had a road. Paul Brower, who raced at Carlisle for years, wrote that there was "a certain degree of 'organization'" in the 1950s when anonymous drivers gathered on Fourche Dam Pike, "the narrow two-lane road 'behind' Adams Field," the Little Rock airport. Their show place was the Snappy Service Drive In, "the meeting/show place for many hot rodders of the day."[^brower]

The real thing came from the war. The Army Air Forces built an auxiliary field near Carlisle in 1942–43 for Stuttgart Army Airfield, Auxiliary No. 1, unstaffed, for practice landings and emergencies, and abandoned it after the war.[^wikicar] Brower wrote that its asphalt runways were laid in an "L" over thousands of loads of crushed stone, "said to be" hauled from Granite Mountain in Little Rock to carry bombers flown by student pilots from Stuttgart. A similar field at Hazen, about 15 miles away, could not be reached from Highway 70, "so Carlisle was chosen by the Arkansas Timing Association as the site for the state's first 'formal' drag strip."[^brower]

The ATA was five men from Little Rock and North Little Rock: O. D. Brazil and his brother Charles, Jim Reynolds, Woodie Taylor and Richard Thompson. It ran under NHRA rules and classes, and a pit pass bought NHRA insurance. At first you had to be an owner, driver or crew member to get one, "until promoters figured out that there was a small profit to be made on each pit pass." Brower dates the first drag meet to April 1955.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^brower]

## A railroad engineer's speed shop

The founders raced what they built. Woodie Taylor, a Missouri Pacific railroad engineer, ran Twin City Speed Shop out of his garage in North Little Rock; his first hot rod was a '48 Chevrolet pickup with a Studebaker V8 ("Yes, Studebaker"), and he later put a Scotty Fenn Chassis Research K-88 under a B/Dragster that ran in the 120-mph, 11-second range. Dick Beard, a North Little Rock salvage-yard operator and a Little Rock Dragsters member, was there from the start with an Oldsmobile-powered chopped and channeled '34 Ford whose short crossmember was saved by a flathead head stud and "a clenched-fist-size weld." His roll bar was black water pipe with threaded elbows.[^brower]

O. D. Brazil started on a Triumph motorcycle ridden by Ray Stancil. Pat Stancil Downing remembered watching her husband in 1957 beat a Memphis "fuel burner" on the Triumph at about 126.[^dslar] In 1958 Brazil built a purple '34 Ford altered coupe with a Dodge engine, then in 1959 put in a 392 Chrysler that had a sad history: Jim Reynolds and Little Rock garage owner Howard Strickland had raced it in a '57 Dodge, and while towing to Carlisle on old Highway 70 in a rainstorm, Reynolds crashed, fatally injuring his wife. Brazil later teamed with Brower, and their cars were all purple.[^brower]

Brower's own dragster, on the old Beard chassis with an aluminum body, a Chrysler on six Strombergs and an Iskenderian cam, matched the national record of 144.6 mph at 10.46 on its first lap. Over two seasons it won all but two class trophies at Carlisle, plus "$25 savings bonds!", and in 1963 it upset Raymond Godman and John Albright at the NHRA Division Four championship race in Memphis, 160.62 at 9.91, for $750 and a box of Sears Craftsman tools. The tools, Brower wrote of himself, "he still has today."[^brower]

## The Memphis crowd

Carlisle raced on the second Sunday, April through October, and Brower wrote that "the latest stuff always seemed to come from Memphis." Godman, a Memphis insurance agent who raced from a wheelchair after Korean War injuries, set strip and national records there and later promoted Lakeland, Memphis's strip, which is on the [Memphis](../places/memphis.md) page.[^brower] Little Rock meat packer Mike Heim's roadster team won the top spot on the *Drag News* Middle Eliminator list by beating a Des Moines roadster two out of three at Carlisle, "(See some promoter involvement here?)," Brower added, and held it for over a year. The list, and *Drag News*'s founder, are on the [Doris Herbert](../people/doris-herbert.md) page.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^brower]

The touring pros came for match races against Godman, Brazil & Brower and the Louisiana fuelers: Don Garlits "on several occasions," Art Malone, Tommy Ivo, Chris Karamesines and most of the Texas fuel cars. A Little Rock boy named Lance Miller, whose father, Tommy "T" Miller, raced his everyday GTOs there, remembered Garlits giving him a spark plug, and a Sunday when his father and a friend took their low Pontiac down flooded farm roads to sneak in at the pits for free.[^brower][^miller]

## A tire iron and a rice field

At Carlisle, as everywhere, the stock cars outnumbered the hot rods; Brower thought about half of all 1955 Chevrolets ever made were raced. Was there cheating? "Is the sky blue?" A $25 deposit forced a competitor to tear down, and most protests ended with somebody backing down and "leaving the strip in a symphony of profanity." Babe Sanders of North Little Rock, who won regularly in an Impala, settled several by guarding his hood with a tire iron; Babe, Brower explained, "was a 1958 version of Arnold Schwarzenegger. Only bigger and meaner."<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^brower]

Taylor's old Chassis Research car came back in the late 1960s with a blown Chevrolet, and its last pass ended "into the rice field on the north side of Carlisle Drag Strip just beyond the finish line"; the driver walked away. Bill Deaver's methanol Corvette, converted in secret in Beard's garage, smoked across the Broadway Bridge to the Snappy Service, won Little Eliminator at Carlisle that Sunday, and later died on "the famous road behind the airport."[^brower]

It stayed a part-time strip on a working airfield. Miller remembered crop dusters using it during the week, concession stands in portable trailers on race weekends, and that it was open all through the 1960s and early 1970s; his last visit was no later than 1975.[^miller]
