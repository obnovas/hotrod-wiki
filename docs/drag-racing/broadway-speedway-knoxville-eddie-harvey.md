# No Roll Bars at Broadway: Eddie Harvey, the Broadway Speedway and Knoxville's Drag Strip (1948 to the late 1970s)

*A $55 poster, a parts man's photo albums, and a speedway that became a stockyard*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Low (the speedway's story comes from a Knoxville columnist's 2003 interviews with two of its drivers, a family business's own page and a 2014 local racing history; the dates of the end, and of Harvey's drag strip, differ) · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> Jack Neely's "Secret History" column in Knoxville's *Metro Pulse* (2003); the Eddie's Auto Parts family page; a funeral home obituary (2014); David McGee's *A History of East Tennessee Auto Racing* (History Press, 2014) · **Last verified:** 2026-11-18 (Round 43) · **Open issues:** ◐ how big the crowds were; ⚠ 1957 or 1958; ⚠ when Harvey's drag strip opened · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "We didn't have no roll bars. If you mentioned putting a roll bar on your car, you were considered a sissy."

    — Eddie Harvey, Broadway Speedway driver and owner of Eddie's Auto Parts, to Jack Neely, *Metro Pulse*, 2003.[^mp03]

## A poster from an attic

In Knoxville the man behind the parts counter on Broadway was one of the heroes of the race track out the same road. Eddie Harvey drove midgets at the Broadway Speedway with a leather cap and no roll bar, sold parts for 67 years, and when the speedway was gone he built the next track himself, farther out Maynardville Highway. Its grandstands now stand at Knoxville's drag strip.[^mp03][^ob14][^mcg14]

Even a Knoxville historian had forgotten the speedway. In 2003 Karen Daniel, a programming director at the Knoxville-based Do It Yourself Network, paid $55 online for a racing poster a Georgia antiques dealer said had come out of a Knoxville attic: "SATURDAY NITES / 8:30 P.M. / STOCK CAR RACES / BROADWAY SPEEDWAY / KNOXVILLE," with cartoon cars and the words "SMASHING CRASHING ROUGHNECK DRIVERS." She guessed it was Knoxville, Iowa, and asked a raceway historian there, who found it in a book: Knoxville, Tennessee.[^mp03] Jack Neely, who wrote the *Metro Pulse*'s "Secret History" column, had never heard of it either. At the library he found it at the Knoxville end of Maynardville Highway, just past Fountain City, not on Broadway itself. Sterling Lafayette "Fate" Irwin, a Corryton man and Democratic politician who had been assistant finance commissioner under Gov. Gordon Browning, set it up with a couple of partners in May 1948.[^mp03]

## A leather cap and goggles

Neely found Harvey at Eddie's Auto Parts, an "off-Broadway institution," still in the parts business after several decades. Harvey keeps yellowed photo albums in the back with the rebuilt carburetors, and he doesn't show them to everybody: "People will see a picture of themselves, pull them out, put them in their wallet, and that's the last you'll see of them."[^mp03]

In its first years Broadway was mainly a midget track. Harvey said he usually took the quarter-mile dirt oval in 17 seconds, and a program was usually four races of 25 laps each.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup> "All you had was a leather cap, with goggles," he said. "If you turned over, you'd either break your neck or break your shoulder." Then, matter-of-factly: "I broke my neck. Everybody broke their neck." He showed Neely the scar under his chin from the wreck where his chin went over the windshield.[^mp03]

When people tired of midgets, the stock cars took over. They began as a ten-lap "added attraction" and became the main event. The rivalry everyone came to see was Breezy Waddell against Tootle Estes, who drove a '40 Ford; Waddell said they were friends who played up the feud for the fans. Waddell's '36 Ford ran as "Mr. X," his sponsor's idea. "I wished I'd had a number, like everybody else," he said. He still had the car in his garage on Clinton Highway in 2003.[^mp03] Estes, McGee's history adds, worked for Harvey, "and he'd tell you he delivered moonshine," according to a fellow racer.[^mcg14] Harvey's own family page says he had been a bootlegger for about eight years, never caught, before he saved enough to open his first parts store, where a couple of piles of coal out front told customers he had what they were looking for.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^eap]

The drivers looked after themselves. In 1952 they formed the East Tennessee Hardtop Racing Association, at one time over 300 members, with Waddell as president for a while; a couple of dollars of "pit money" from each driver and crew bought some injury insurance.[^mp03] Waddell said nobody was ever killed at Broadway, "but it wasn't for lack of trying." He was scalded once when a radiator hose broke in a Ford with no firewall, and it put him in St. Mary's for a while.[^mp03]

## Drag racing for a while

Broadway also drew national midget and stock car races.[^mcg14] McGee writes that the place later added a half-mile oval and a drag strip.[^mcg14] Neely's version is that stock car racing gave way to drag racing "for a while, but by the late '50s, Saturday nights on Maynardville Highway were getting quieter."[^mp03] Some said the crowds were dwindling; Waddell said Irwin started demanding a cut of the pit money.[^mp03] McGee dates the end to after the 1957 season.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^mcg14] When Neely went looking in 2003, the track site, just past a Krystal near Halls Crossroads, was the Union Livestock Yards, with the Stockyard Cafe at the end of the road.[^mp03]

## Harvey's own track

Harvey's favorite place to race, he told his family's interviewer, was Broadway. How often did he win? "not that often, cause it was hard to win."[^eap] He also liked to tell about a race in Atlanta he won and then let a friend take the credit, since back home nobody would hear about it anyway.[^eap]

In 1965, McGee writes, Harvey sold land to the state for an interstate and used the money to build Knoxville Raceway, a four-tenths-mile paved oval off Maynardville Highway, several miles past the old Broadway site. It went to dirt in 1974 and was later reconfigured into a drag strip that opened in 1978; the big concrete grandstands at Knoxville Dragway are left from the oval.[^mcg14][^mp03] His family's page says he opened Knoxville Dragway in 1964 and still owned it.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^eap] Harvey died April 7, 2014, at 91, owner of Eddie's Auto Parts for 67 years and a member of the Tennessee Racing Hall of Fame.[^ob14]

The poster that started Neely's search said "ROUGHNECK DRIVERS." One of them sold parts on Broadway for 67 years and built the town its next track.

## Open questions & disputed details

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **How big were the crowds?** In the one account we have, some claimed as many as 10,000 came to the speedway; Harvey said the biggest crowds he saw were about 2,500, most of them standing.[^mp03] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did Broadway close?** McGee's track list says "It closed in 1958," while a photo caption in the same book says the speedway ran events "from 1948 to 1957" and the chapter says it closed after the 1957 season. Neely gives only "by the late '50s," and Waddell didn't remember.[^mcg14][^mp03] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did Harvey's drag strip open?** The Eddie's Auto Parts page says he opened Knoxville Dragway in 1964; McGee says he built Knoxville Raceway as an oval in 1965 and that it became a drag strip in 1978.[^eap][^mcg14] Whether the drags at Broadway ran on a separate strip is also unconfirmed.
