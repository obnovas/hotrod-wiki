# From Strip to Runway: Cumming Dragway (Forsyth County, Ga., 1963–88)

*Most strips in this archive began as airfields. This one went the other way.*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> county weekly (*The Forsyth County News*, Cumming, Ga.): display ads, race reports, a columnist's note, a front-page land-use fight, letters and a 2015 obituary, 1963–2015; one suburban weekly ad (*The Kennesaw Neighbor*). Newspapers read as page OCR from the Georgia Historic Newspapers search service · **Last verified:** 2026-10-22 (Round 23-East) · **Open issues:** who built the strip and when ?; Houston or Huston Platt ⚠; the 218 mph track record ◐; which "first annual" championship ⚠; when racing ended ?; how the airstrip fight ended ? · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "The drag strip was a noisy, Smelly and dangerous place. Its closing was welcomed by all."

    — A letter to the editor, *The Forsyth County News*, Jan. 25, 1984.[^fcn840125b]

On Jan. 2, 1984, a reporter for *The Forsyth County News* photographed a small plane taking off and taxiing on a 41.58-acre tract on Hurt Bridge Road. The tract was, the paper explained, "the former site of the Cumming drag strip." Three months earlier the county planning director had written to the owner: "The reports were that the dragstrip is being used as a landing strip for aircraft. I drove by the property today and found that to be true."[^fcn840118]

In most of the stories on this site, hot rodders talk their way onto a runway. In Forsyth County the runway came last.

## Super Stocks on Highway 20 (1963–68)

The first notice we have found is a display ad from late August 1963: "FORSYTH COUNTY DRAGWAY, Cumming, Ga. Located 4 miles west of Cumming on Hwy. 20." The Sunday, Sept. 1, card was a "BIG SUPER STOCK RACE" for "$800 CASH," and the ad named the cars: Hubert Platt's "The Georgia Shaker" and Houston Platt's "The Dixie Twister," both '63 Chevys, against a '62 Dodge called "Slowpoke," a '62 Pontiac called "Passionate Pancho" and a Plymouth called "Midnight Special."[^fcn630829]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup>

By 1965 it was Cumming Dragway, and it booked names from beyond drag racing. In May the paper reported that Curtis Turner, "once the non pareil of Southern dirt-track racing," had been signed to race the NASCAR Grand National champion Richard Petty "Saturday night at Cumming Dragway."[^fcn650513] In July the ad promised Phil Bonner, "Big Daddy," in a new Falcon against Don Nicholson, printed beside an electric cooperative's "COMMUNITY OWNED" slogan.[^fcn650715] In August "track owners" brought in the Lanier Skydivers of Gainesville for a free fall from 5,200 feet before an afternoon of drag racing.[^fcn650812]

In July 1967 the columnist Tony Maddox reported that Mickey Swims "has acquired the Cumming Dragway." Swims owned one of the dragsters too, and told Maddox it could "accelerate to 120 MPH in one-fourth of a mile. OK, Mickey, I'll take your word for it."[^fcn670713] That September Swims, as promoter, matched Houston Platt against Phil Bonner and brought in Bobby Thomas to race the clock with a "1,200 horse power rail-type dragster."[^fcn670914] The next March came a "CUMMING DRAGWAY GRAND OPENING," with a thrill driver from Montreal billed to "TAKE CAR OVER 60 PASSENGER SCHOOL BUS."[^fcn680321]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup>

Not every story was a show. On June 1, 1968, Jerry Garrett and his wife Ellen were racing their dragster at Cumming. After the final race he turned the car around to head back to the lot, and it "swerved out of control and went over a bank." A back injury from the crash, the paper wrote that September, "eventually led to the coma-like state he is in today"; he had been that way since June 21.[^fcn680905]

## Funny cars and pro stocks (1970–73)

A year after [the Yellow River crash](yellow-river-covington.md) brought Georgia a race track licensing law, Cumming's ads were selling safety. A 1970 motorcycle-drag ad promised "Liability Insurance For Spectators" and "Safety Department Licensed," and the paper named Mickey Swims as the strip's owner.[^fcn700806][^fcn700806b]

The early 1970s were the strip's loudest years. In March 1971 Hubert Platt beat Ralph Clanton's "big Money" Camaro, driven by Randy Payne, for $3,000 in a best-of-five match race, while the drag-bike rider E. J. Potter was "given a speeding ticket by Deputy Sheriff C.W. Yarbrough, all in fun of course."[^fcn710318] Two weeks later the paper reported "speeds up to 218 mph, the fastest ever at the Cumming Dragway": Jim Hundley's "Starrliner" set "the fastest time of the day, also the track record," a 5.78 at 218, beating Julius Hughes' "Zot #4."[^fcn710401]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup> In April, with Marinus Tavenier on the microphone, Houston Platt's funny car beat Dennis Kirkland's "Sagittarious" and Dee Simmons' "Thunder Road" Corvette.[^fcn710408] Two weeks later the same paper spelled him "Huston Platt."[^fcn710422]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>

By 1972 Swims was owner and Tavenier promoter, and the program had turned to pro stock: a "Super Pro-Stock Spectacular" with a purse of nearly $4,000, drivers "from as far away as Texas," and trophies for spectators who wanted to "try out their family cars."[^fcn720907][^fcn720914] A motocross track was laid out beside the strip.[^fcn720803] In October the strip billed the "First Annual Georgia State Pro-Stock Championship," and the second, for $3,000, was set for the next July.[^fcn721026][^fcn730718]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-5"><a href="#q-5" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup> In May 1973 William Parris of Rome beat Hubert Platt's new 351 Pinto with a 9.56; the Pinto had shifting trouble with a newly installed Lenco.[^fcn730516] That fall the ad still read "RACING EVERY SUNDAY."[^fcn730926]

## A landing strip on Hurt Bridge Road (1979–88)

By May 1979 the paper's headline was in the past tense: "CUMMING DRAGWAY USED TO HOST MANY FAMOUS DRAGSTERS."[^fcn790502]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-6"><a href="#q-6" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup> In 1980 a yard sale was advertised "near Cumming Drag Strip," a landmark now rather than a track.[^fcn800423]

Then came the planes. The owner, Lamar Suddeth, had tried in October 1983 to rezone the tract for multi-family housing and run into a petition with 180-plus names. After the January 1984 photographs the county sued "to enjoin Suddeth from using the property as a landing strip for aircraft" until he had an airport permit.[^fcn840118][^fcn840125] Not every neighbor minded. One letter praised the "clever and excellent planned use of the old drag strip as a small air plane 'airpark,'" and said the owner had given surrounding owners a chance to comment.[^fcn840125b]

The fight ran on for years. In April 1987 a superior court judge upheld the county's denial of an airport rezoning, and Suddeth said he had leased the airstrip to the U.S. Army; Army Reserve officials, the paper reported, had "a different story."[^fcn870426] That December deputies answering a neighbor's call "observed five different types of aircraft on the strip's apron," and Suddeth ordered them off his land.[^fcn871213] By May 1988 Suddeth had asphalted the old strip "and marked it as a runway"; his two building permits listed the "construction of two 20,000-square foot poultry houses," and the county was asking for an injunction.[^fcn880525]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-7"><a href="#q-7" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup>

In 2000 the paper looked back at Cumming's Sunday nights, the dirt track at the foot of Sawnee Mountain and "the rumble of a drag strip off Hurt Bridge Road near Aaron Sosebee Road." Its verdict on the strip took one sentence: "The dragway became a landing strip for a private plane."[^fcn000326] When Hubert Platt died in 2015, the same paper called him "a Cumming resident who was one of the state's most decorated drag racers."[^fcn151002]

For the other Atlanta-area strips of the same years, see [Yellow River Drag Strip](yellow-river-covington.md) and [From Speed Shop to Strip](atlanta-speed-shop-dragway.md).<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-8"><a href="#q-8" title="Open question: see the bottom of the page">?</a></sup>

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** **Who built the strip, and when?** The August 1963 "Forsyth County Dragway" ad is the earliest notice we have found, and it names no owner.[^fcn630829] The 1965 paper speaks only of "track owners."[^fcn650812] In August 1964 a Forest Park paper listed "Lamar Sexton and Pete Robinson, Cumming Dragway" among the members of the Atlanta Drag Strip Association, which was staging a charity drag race for cystic fibrosis; a note by David Dilbeck on a Georgia drag racing history site gives the same names as the association's Cumming members.[^fp640825][^gdr1964] Neither says they built or owned the strip. Whether the 1964 "Lamar Sexton" has any connection with Lamar Suddeth, the tract's owner in the 1980s, is an open lead; we have found nothing that links them. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Houston or Huston Platt?** The same county paper prints "HOUSTON PLATT" in 1963 and "Houston Platt" in April 1971,[^fcn630829][^fcn710408] then "Huston Platt" two weeks later.[^fcn710422] The [Yellow River](yellow-river-covington.md) page tracks the same question. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Was 218 mph the track record?** The 5.78 and 218 mph come from one race report, which calls it "the track record."[^fcn710401] We have not found the run in a drag racing paper or a later Cumming report. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** **Why a grand opening in 1968?** The strip had run since at least 1963, so the March 1968 "GRAND OPENING" may mean a season opener under the new owner, or a reopening after a closure. The ad does not say.[^fcn680321] A "73 GRAND OPENING" ad of March 1973 suggests the phrase was used for season openers.[^fcn730308] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-5" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Which championship was the first?** A May 1972 ad bills a "GRAND OPENING First Annual Georgia State Championship" for May 7;[^fcn720504] the October 1972 paper calls the Oct. 29 meet "the First Annual Georgia State Pro-Stock Championship," and the July 1973 meet is "the second annual."[^fcn721026][^fcn730718] They may be different events. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-5" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-6" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** **When did racing stop?** The last race ad we have read is from September 1973, and by May 1979 the paper wrote of the strip in the past tense.[^fcn730926][^fcn790502] We have read only the headline of the 1979 piece, not its text. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-6" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-7" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Open question.** **How did the airstrip fight end?** We have not read past May 1988, so we do not know whether the injunction was granted or whether planes kept landing.[^fcn880525] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-7" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
