# A Dime at the Gate: Beech Bend Raceway on the Barren River (Bowling Green, Ky., 1946– )

*Ten cents at the gate, three teenagers and a road grader, and a drag strip the histories date to 1951, 1956 or the mid-1960's*

Charles Garvin charged ten cents at a picnic ground in a bend of the Barren River. Three teenagers started the racing with a county road grader, and the boys had to do the work. When he heard about drag racing, he sent D.A. Faxon to Atlanta to investigate. The sources disagree on the year: 1951, 1956, or the mid-1960s. Dallas and Alfreda Jones bought the racetracks in 1984, and the family still runs them.

## Ten cents and a road grader

Long before engines, Beech Bend was a picnic ground: a stand of beech trees in a big "U"-shaped bend of the Barren River, about two and a half miles northeast of Bowling Green, where families had come to swim since the 1880s.[^kmhof][^bb04] Charles Garvin bought it, "about 1941" by the park's own history and the Kentucky Motorsports Hall of Fame's, or "for $12,990.98 in 1942" in the telling of Robert Dickey, who wrote a book about him.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^kmhof][^bb04][^bgdn] He charged ten cents at the gate, began with a live pony ride and added a ferris wheel bought at the Chicago World's Fair.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-8"><a href="#q-8" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^kmhof][^bb04] Garvin did not believe in borrowing money, Dickey said; it was always "pay as you go."[^bgdn]

The racing started with three teenagers. On a summer day in the mid-1940s, the hall of fame's history says, Elmo and Wayne Guy, who were brothers, and their friend Billy Hudson paid Garvin his dime, rode their motorcycles into a big meadow and raced each other in a large oval through the grass and weeds. On the way out they made him an offer: Hudson worked for the Warren County Roads Department, and if he could get a road grader, could they make a flat track? Garvin agreed, "but the boys had to do the work." He promoted his first motorcycle race in 1946.[^kmhof] Dickey credits the original dirt track to someone else, Raymond McClard, the local Harley-Davidson dealer and later a four-term Warren County jailer.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^bgdn]

Stock cars came in 1948. The first drivers were young men like the Guys, and the owners were older businessmen. Ernie Wright of Wright's Machine Shop wanted a car but not the driving; he asked McClard, the motorcycle dealer, for riders, reasoning that you had to be "a little foolhardy" to race a stock car. Elmo Guy, who "didn't even drive a car," took Wright's '32 Chevrolet with a bored and stroked flathead, felt four wheels under him for the first time, and opened it up. In those days drivers scrounged football helmets and airplane lap belts and wore a handkerchief over nose and mouth against the dust; Elmo drove to Nashville and bought a real Berlin helmet from a speed shop. When "the Nashville boys" kept taking the prize money home, the locals went to Garvin, "who was the law," and got one rule imposed: only one carburetor per motor.[^kmhof]

## A man sent to Atlanta

Then came the drags. "When Garvin heard about drag racing," Dickey said, "he sent D.A. Faxon to Atlanta to investigate," and before many weeks the quarter mile was built, in 1956. The track's manager, Broc Porter, gives the same year in more detail: "the first race held in September of 1956," on a strip that "was actually dirt and was half a mile long, including the shutdown area."[^bgdn][^dz20] Not every history agrees. The hall of fame's page says the strip "was first opened in 1951," starting from the start-finish line of the dirt oval, and the park's own 2004 history says the "present day dragstrip" was added "Sometime in the mid-1960's."<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^kmhof][^bb04]

A soldier saw it on dirt. Don Jensen, stationed at Fort Knox, went to Bowling Green in August 1957: "They were running 1/4 mile on dirt, slight downhill and narrow, about 80 cars." There was a rail with a six-cylinder Ranger aircraft engine, and a '56 Chevy "just bought in So Cal" took top eliminator at 90 mph. Jensen was wearing a 140 mph club jacket, "and no one there would beleve a car could run 140+ in 1/4mile."[^dslky] That memory does not sit easily with the visitors bureau, which says the graded strip "was paved before the end of the first season"; the hall of fame dates the paving of strip and oval to 1962.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-5"><a href="#q-5" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^visitbg][^kmhof]

In the 1960s the strip drew racers from three states. Joe "Muggs" Tichenor brought his '48 Anglia AA/GS in 1966. The Wandel brothers of Grandview, Indiana, ran a '56 Ford Fairlane, a '51 Henry J and finally a '48 Thames panel truck with a Borg-Warner four-speed in B/Gas, "at Beech Bend in Bowing Green, Kentucky, and at Owensboro." Dave Huber won Competition Eliminator in a Chevy-powered C/D dragster and remembered that "Morris Smith was a great track manager and the Garvins built a great track, campground and park."[^dslky]

## Turned around, then saved

The park peaked in the 1960s and faded in the 1970s, when attractions like Opryland drew the crowds and Garvin's health failed. After he died the amusement park closed; the oval and drag strip kept running under a local manager, and a company with ties to the country singer Ronnie Milsap reopened the park for two seasons from 1981 before it went back to the Garvin family.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^bb04] Somewhere in those years the strip was turned around. "Most fans and racers don't realize the track actually faced in a different direction until early in the 1970s," Porter says; at about the eighth-mile mark you can still see "a patch of the old racing surface pointed toward where the current pits are." The hall of fame puts the new starting line in 1971.[^dz20][^kmhof] The covered, chair-back grandstands that went up then came, Porter says, from Sulphur Dell, the Nashville ballpark where minor league games were played until 1969.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-6"><a href="#q-6" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^dz20]

In 1984 Dallas and Alfreda Jones bought the racetracks, and in 1987 the overgrown amusement park beside them.[^bb04] Their grandson Porter started at about 14 on the track crew, "working the waterbox, then the staging lanes, and eventually the starting line"; his uncle Clay Jones, who died in 2014, could run the computer or announce "in a pinch." In 2003 the park was chosen to host the NHRA Hot Rod Reunion.[^dz20][^bb04] Dickey's line about the place, "It is said to be the oldest continuous operation drag strip in the USA," is a claim Union Grove, Wisconsin, makes too; see [Great Lakes Dragaway](great-lakes-dragaway-union-grove.md).<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-7"><a href="#q-7" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^bgdn]

Ten cents at the gate, three teenagers and a road grader, and a man sent to Atlanta when Garvin heard about drag racing. The histories date the strip to 1951, 1956, or sometime in the mid-1960's. Dallas and Alfreda Jones bought the racetracks in 1984, and the family still runs them.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did the Beech Bend drag strip open?** The Kentucky Motorsports Hall of Fame: "first opened in 1951." Robert Dickey (2009): "a reality in 1956." Broc Porter, the track manager (2020): "the first race held in September of 1956." The visitors bureau also gives 1956. The park's own 2004 history: "Sometime in the mid-1960's the present day dragstrip was added adjacent to the oval track," which may mean the realigned strip rather than the first one. Don Jensen's August 1957 memory is the earliest firsthand account we have. The *Park City Daily News* for 1951 and September 1956 would settle it.[^kmhof][^bgdn][^dz20][^visitbg][^bb04][^dslky] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did Garvin buy Beech Bend, 1941 or 1942?** The park's 2004 history and the hall of fame: "In approximately 1941" and "About 1941." Dickey: "after Charles Garvin bought it for $12,990.98 in 1942." Dickey also says the park was "very much a one-man operation" in 1943.[^bb04][^kmhof][^bgdn] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Did Garvin die in 1979 or 1980?** The park's 2004 history: "Mr. Charles Garvin passed away in 1979 and the amusement park was closed." Dickey, who represented him: "until his death in 1980" and "Charles Garvin died in 1980."[^bb04][^bgdn] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Who laid out the first track, and how long was it?** The hall of fame credits Elmo and Wayne Guy and Billy Hudson, with a county road grader, and calls the oval a "1/3 mile dirt track"; the visitors bureau also lists a 1/3-mile oval. Dickey says Raymond McClard "laid out the original dirt track for motorcycle races in the late 1940s." Porter calls the first track "a 3/8-mile dirt oval," and the 2004 park history "a 3/8 mile paved oval."[^kmhof][^visitbg][^bgdn][^dz20][^bb04] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-5" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When was the strip paved?** The visitors bureau: "Originally a simple graded surface, the stretch was paved before the end of the first season." The hall of fame: "In 1962 the Drag Strip and the Oval track were paved." Jensen remembered it "on dirt" in August 1957.[^visitbg][^kmhof][^dslky] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-5" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-6" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Did the covered grandstands come from Nashville's Sulphur Dell?** Only Porter's account in *Dragzine* says so.[^dz20] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-6" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-7" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **The oldest continuously operating strip in the country?** Dickey: "It is said to be the oldest continuous operation drag strip in the USA." The visitors bureau is softer: "one of America's oldest, continuously operating drag strips." WUWM and RACER say the same of Great Lakes Dragaway, which opened in 1955. Which claim holds depends on q-1 and on what "continuous" means.[^bgdn][^visitbg] See [Great Lakes Dragaway](great-lakes-dragaway-union-grove.md#open-questions-disputed-details). <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-7" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-8" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Which World's Fair did the ferris wheel come from?** The Kentucky Motorsports Hall of Fame: in 1946-47 he "added a swimming pool and a large ferris wheel that he purchased at the Chicago World's Fair." The park's 2004 history: the first mechanical ride was "a large ferris wheel purchased at the Chicago World's Fair." Robert Dickey told the *Daily News* that Garvin saved until he could "pay cash for his first Ferris wheel and the classic carousel from the 1904 St. Louis World Fair."[^kmhof][^bb04][^bgdn] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-8" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
