# A Family Portrait on the Fender: Dave's Dream, the Chimayó Lowrider That Went to the Smithsonian (New Mexico, 1969–2024)

*A used Ford, a husband's unfinished plans, a car club's help, and a blessing at the Santuario before the truck left*

## A used LTD in Chimayó

Dave's Dream is a custom car that a whole family finished after the man who started it was gone. David Jaramillo began building it in Chimayó, New Mexico, in the 1970s. He died in 1978 with the work undone. His family and friends from a local car club carried it on, and in 1992 the town sent it to Washington as the first lowrider, to its curator's knowledge, ever to go into a museum.[^dn][^si]

The car started out as an ordinary 1969 Ford LTD. Richard Martinez bought it new that year. In 1975 his cousin, David Jaramillo, bought it used, with its big V-8, and, like a lot of young men around there, started improving it a little at a time.[^dn] He flared the fenders, added chrome exhaust pipes and a custom grille, and frenched the taillights, with removable velvet covers.[^dn] On sunny days, lowriders cruised the highways around Chimayó, Richard Martinez said, so low "you make the snakes cry with envy."[^dn]

## Finished by the family

Jaramillo died in 1978, killed in an accident in a different car, the Smithsonian says.[^dn][^si] On the rear fender, above the wheel, the car carries a painted portrait of the family as it was that year: David, his wife Irene and David Jr., who was 6. It was Irene's favorite part of the car.[^dn][^si] The museum's description puts the mountains of their hometown behind them, and names that hometown as Española.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^si]

The rest of the car is what came out of that family and its friends. Dennis Martinez, another cousin, helped restore it with the Los Bajitos car club.[^dn] By the 1992 description, it wore two coats of paint, black and a candy-apple red lacquer mixed with iridescent flakes, that together looked purple, with a broad gold stripe down the side and ribbons, butterflies and stars over the body.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^dn] Inside was crushed red and black velvet and a television set in the back. A roadrunner and a yucca were painted in the wheel wells, and the license plate said "Chimayo."[^dn] Batteries in the trunk ran two hydraulic pumps that raised and lowered each end and rocked the car side to side.[^dn] The museum says the family, friends and club members finished the conversion between 1990 and 1992, including the hydraulics that let it hop.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^si] It won trophies at shows in the towns north of Santa Fe.[^si]

## A blessing before the truck

The Smithsonian found it more than two years before it left. Curators from the National Museum of American History, looking for things that showed life in the Rio Grande Valley for an exhibit called "American Encounters," bought it.[^dn] Before it went, the car was blessed by a priest at El Santuario de Chimayó, the town's historic shrine, with more than 500 people looking on.[^hc][^si]

On April 5, 1992, the *Deseret News* reported that it would be loaded into a truck that Monday and shipped to Washington. "This is the first low-rider in the history of the world, to my knowledge, that's going into a museum," said Bill Withuhn, the museum's curator. Irene Jaramillo said of her husband, "I don't think he'd be able to believe it."[^dn]

The car has stayed in the collection. In May 2022 the museum put it back on display on its first floor, and that showing closed in August 2024.[^si]

It is in a museum now, but it was never built as a museum piece. It was a used Ford a young man in Chimayó started fixing up a little at a time, and his family and his friends finished it for him.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Sources disagree.** **How much was done before 1978?** The 1992 *Deseret News* story says Jaramillo "customized it before his death in 1978" and lists his changes (fenders, exhaust, grille, taillights); it credits Dennis Martinez and the Los Bajitos club with helping "restore" it.[^dn] The museum's pages say his family and local artisans completed the modifications he had planned after his death, and one curator's essay dates that work, including the hydraulics, to 1990–92, after the museum had bought the car.[^si] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Sources disagree.** **Chimayó or Española?** The newspaper, the museum's exhibit page and its press release all call Jaramillo a Chimayó man.[^dn][^si] The museum's visual description places the mountains in the fender portrait in the family's hometown of Española.[^si] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Sources disagree.** **What color is it?** The 1992 story describes black and candy-apple red lacquer with iridescent flakes that "impart a shimmering purple hue." The museum's visual description calls the base color black with silver flecks.[^dn][^si] Both may describe the same paint in different light. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
