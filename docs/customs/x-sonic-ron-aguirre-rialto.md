# "Hi, Lester": Ron Aguirre's X-Sonic and the Lifts That Beat the Ticket Book (Rialto, Calif., 1957–1964)

*A new Corvette, a cop on a motorcycle, a body man's Port-A-Power, a welder father, and a car that rose on a button*

## Too low for Rialto

One of the first sets of hydraulic lifts on a custom car<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> did not come from a speed shop. By Ron Aguirre's own account, it came from a traffic cop who kept writing him tickets, and a hydraulic ram he saw at a friend's body shop.[^krx][^hem22]

Aguirre, of Rialto, was a member and president of the Krankers of San Bernardino. He bought a metallic brown Corvette new in 1957. Right away it was lowered, with the front coils cut and wooden blocks in the back, and given side pipes, and Ed Roth at The Crazy Painters gave it scallops and flames.[^krx] The earliest known photo was taken outside the Orange Julius up the hill in Crestline, with Aguirre in his Krankers jacket.[^krx]

California's lowered-car law, Vehicle Code 24008, passed in 1957 and was not enforced until 1958. It outlawed any car with any part lower than the bottom of its wheel rims. Aguirre had the lowest car in Rialto, and a local motorcycle officer, Lester Groves, nicknamed "Sandy," made ticketing him a priority.[^krx] Then Aguirre saw a body man at a friend's shop pushing out a dent with a hydraulic Port-A-Power, and thought of putting a ram between the spring and the frame. His father, Louie, a welder by trade, made the cups Ron designed to hold the rams. Ron had hoses run into the car and set the hand pump on the hump between the seats.[^krx] *Lowrider* magazine later reported he had the first version in within three months of buying the car.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>[^krx]

In a letter to a member of the LayItLow forum, Aguirre told what happened in 1959 at Ruby's Drive-in, the local hangout.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup> About 100 of his school friends were waiting to see what would happen, and Sandy was parked across the street. Aguirre pulled out low, with his side pipes scraping, and told the buddy beside him to pump. He didn't get twenty feet before the red lights came on. No one called him Sandy to his face, so Aguirre greeted him by name: "Hi, Lester what seems to be the problem"? The officer said the car was too low. "But Lester," Aguirre told him, "it isn't too low any more, I took your advise and raised it to legal height." In those days, he explained, police checked a car by sliding the ticket book under it. This time it slid under without hitting anything. "OH... revenge was so sweet," he wrote.[^krx]

## A button instead of a pump

The hand pump was slow, and it did not lift the car evenly. Louie knew of an aircraft surplus place south of Los Angeles called Palleys, and over time the two of them bought 12- and 24-volt pumps, cylinder lines and equalizers, until the car went up and down smoothly at the push of a button.[^krx] Kustomrama credits Ron's younger brother, Filbert, with much of the electrical work and with what it calls the first documented wired hydraulic remote switch for a custom car.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-5"><a href="#q-5" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup>[^krx]

The lifts came out in public at the Cut-Outs of Long Beach's first Custom Car Spectacle, May 30–31, 1959, at Veterans Stadium. A concrete barrier had to be cleared to get onto the track, and every other owner blocked his car up on wood. Aguirre pushed the button, raised the car and drove over.[^krx] Seventeen Krankers cars went to that show. Hilton Vail, second in line behind the Corvette, told Kustomrama in 2019 that a highway patrolman turned around to chase the low car; Vail honked, Aguirre raised it, and the officer, stopping them anyway, said, "I would have sworn coming at you that your car was illegally low."[^krx]

By then the Corvette had a 1958 Dodge's quad headlights, an Opel grille, molded fiberglass fins and ribs, tuck-and-roll in the side coves, and an interior of gold Naugahyde by Tom Sterkel.[^krx] It wore Roth's red and gold panels for exactly one day, at the Disneyland Car Club Day on September 5, 1959, because Aguirre did not like them; Larry Watson repainted it Rainbow Pearl.[^krx] At the Bakersfield Motor & Boat Show that November the sign called it the "Exsonic." Bill Brown of Rialto cast finned aluminum hubcaps for it, and in June 1960 it was on the cover of *Motor Trend*, from the National Roadster Show.[^krx]

## The bubble top

By 1960, Kustomrama says, Aguirre and Roth wanted to build "feature" cars that were paid to appear, not just win trophies. Aguirre wanted a plastic bubble top. Surplus airplane canopies were cracked and foggy, so he blew his own, the way he had blown his taillight lenses at the House of Plastics in San Bernardino. He found an oven big enough in El Monte, built a plywood platform with an air chuck, and his father made a metal ring to his pattern. With 40 to 50 C-clamps and seven or eight helpers, he heated the plastic, clamped it and pumped in air a little at a time. It came out right the first time. After Aguirre showed him how, Roth put a bubble top on his next car, the Beatnik Bandit.[^krx] Hemmings says the X-Sonic inspired Roth to start making his bubble tops.[^hem22]

Watson painted the car six times as Aguirre kept changing it, by his own account in Pat Ganahl's book. Kustomrama says it got Watson's first fade and his first Metalflake, gold flake that Aguirre had brought back from New York.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>[^krx] The bubble-top car had a console with buttons for the steering, doors, top, ignition, lights and front ride height. An electric motor on a toggle switch did the steering, which Aguirre said made it hard to drive on the freeway, and after that he did not cruise it much.[^krx][^hem22] Tom's Top Shop in San Bernardino stitched its later seats in black Naugahyde and silver llama.[^krx]

Others wanted lifts. Watson told Roy Abendroth about them, and Abendroth drove his 1955 Buick to Fontana, where Aguirre put lifts in the front around 1961.[^kra] By 1965 Ron and his father ran Aguirre Styling at 1915 San Fernando Road in Los Angeles.[^kra] Bill Hines met Aguirre and went home to put lifts on the Buddah Buggy; that story is on [the Hines page](../people/bill-hines-leadslinger-hydraulics.md).

Aguirre last showed the X-Sonic in 1964, stored it and sold it.[^hem22] In the early 1990s a stripped hulk turned up at a Corvette restorer's shop, bought as a parts car, with most of the hydraulic system still under it. Pat Ganahl recognized it, and the owner decided not to take it apart.[^krx][^hem22] Aguirre died in May 2011. On May 30, Roger O'Dell posted on the H.A.M.B. that Ron's wife had called to say he had died that Thursday; he had had bone cancer for a long while.[^hamb11] In November 2021 Dave Shuten of Galpin announced he had the car, and he showed it, still unrestored, at the Los Angeles Auto Show and the 2022 Grand National Roadster Show.[^krx][^hem22] Plenty of builders went on to make cars that rise and fall on a switch. This one started because a Rialto kid wanted the ticket book to slide under his car.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Was the X-Sonic first?** Kustomrama says the car "was for many year acknowledged as the first car ever using hydraulics to raise and lower the front suspension," and forum posts at Aguirre's death called it the first.[^krx][^hamb11] Hemmings says two men in postwar California, "seemingly separately and unbeknownst to one another," built the first such systems from surplus parts: Aguirre, and Jim Logue, a North American Aviation employee in Long Beach in 1957, who took his idea from Citroën's suspension for his 1954 Ford, the Fabulous X54.[^hem22] The story says "one of the first." <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did the first lifts go in?** *Lowrider* (June 1995), as Kustomrama reports it, says within three months of buying the car in 1957. Aguirre told Pat Ganahl, for *The Rodder's Journal* No. 50, that the hydraulics were in by October 1958, the date on construction photos; and in his own letter he said it was 1959 before he could "raise a lot of Hell with the system."[^krx] Hemmings has him getting the idea "around the same time" as Logue's 1957 car.[^hem22] Those can all be true of different versions, but no dated photo or receipt for the first install has turned up. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When was Watson's first flake?** This archive's [Larry Watson](../people/larry-watson-bellflower-paint.md) page dates his first Metalflake job and first fade on the X-Sonic to 1960. Kustomrama's X-Sonic page puts the first fade on the first bubble-top version (shown April 1961) and the gold Metalflake at "circa 1961-62," with the flaked car at the 1961 Detroit Autorama.[^krx] Watson also said, per Kustomrama, that it was the first Metalflake job in California; we have only his word for that. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **The stop at Ruby's.** The drive-in story, the 100 friends, the officer's nickname and the ticket-book test are Aguirre's own, from a correspondence with a LayItLow member that Kustomrama quotes; we have not read the forum original.[^krx] Vail's highway patrol story from 1959 is a separate, similar account.[^krx] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-5" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Filbert Aguirre's switch.** The credit for the first documented wired remote switch and an early under-dash switch panel comes from Kustomrama, citing the 46to64 site, which we have not read.[^krx] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-5" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
