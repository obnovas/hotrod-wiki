# Customizing With Paint: Larry Watson, the Bellflower Clock and the Panel Paint Job (Bellflower, California, 1955–2010)

*A teenager in his parents' driveway, a drive-in that didn't believe him, and a Thunderbird he thought looked too big*

!!! abstract "Article record"

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "He could pinstripe a straight line, and it was unbelievable."

    — Teddy Zgrzemski, a painter's helper at Barris Kustoms, to Kustomrama, 2021.[^kr]

## A bicycle outside Barris

Larry Watson is the painter who made paint the custom. Other men cut and welded a car to change it. Watson, starting as a teenager in his parents' driveway in Bellflower, changed it with tape and color, and the paint styles he worked out there and in a string of small shops, scallops, panels, seaweed flames and lace, are still copied.[^hr][^dp]

He was born July 21, 1938, and his family came to California from Oklahoma when he was a child.[^kr] He grew up in Bellflower.[^hr][^dp] In 1955, at 16, he was sitting on his bicycle outside Barris Kustoms in Lynwood, watching Von Dutch stripe cars. One day a friend, Gary McNaught, took him down to watch Von Dutch work, but Von Dutch had moved his operation, and the man striping that day was Dean Jeffries, on Johnny Zupan's 1949 Mercury. Watson stepped over the rope and asked where he bought his brushes. "What do you want to do? Put me out of business?" Jeffries answered, and wouldn't tell him.[^kr]

So Watson bought brushes at an art store and copper paint at Jackson Paint Store and spent two days striping his own 1950 Chevrolet. Then he drove it to the Bellflower Clock Drive-In, where nobody believed he had done it himself. Some thought he had gotten Von Dutch to do it and sign Larry's name. Harvey Budoff called the bluff and pulled his 1950 Ford into the Watson driveway after school one day. It was Larry's first paying stripe job, and he hid a little drawing in the back of the glove box, an eyeball on a spoon, dripping blood, that Budoff's girlfriend eventually found. After that, Kustomrama's account goes, "every time he got home from school there would be three or four cars sitting in the driveway ready to be striped."[^kr] Keith Christensen, whose Gene's Muffler shop in Paramount would later stand next to Watson's, met him that year at a Coachmen car club meeting, where the members went outside at the break to look at the striping on his little Chevy. "It was Chocolate brown on a tan car and the quality of work was outstanding," he told Kustomrama in 2020.[^kr]

At school it was the same. A classmate at Bellflower High School, Jim Williamson, later learned that the flame and pinstripe designs inked on the desk in front of him had been drawn by Watson, according to an excerpt of his 2010 obituary in the *Victor Valley Daily Press*.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^vv]

## Flames in six hours, then two

The driveway turned into a business by small steps. His first flame job, on Buzzy's Ford, took six hours of walking back and forth to get the two sides even. Lying in bed after doing a new 1956 Oldsmobile, he hit on laying the flames out with tissue paper and a bright red china marker, and the job dropped to two hours. His first scallops, in 1956, went on Bob Schremp's 1954 Chevrolet, to cover a run that Branson Auto Body in Artesia had left under the trunk and another on the hood.[^kr] *Hot Rod* says he has been credited with inventing scallops, "to cover up some paint runs, as the story goes."[^hr]

On July 21, 1956, the night before the third annual Norwalk Motorcade, he striped a Lakewood Creators member's Mercury in imitation gold. By his own count, 22 pinstriped cars were entered in that show: four by Von Dutch, seven by Jeffries and eleven by Watson.[^kr] That November a drunk driver hit his Chevy, and he rebuilt it into the Grapevine, the custom *Hot Rod* later called one of the world's favorites.[^kr][^hr]

## A shop under the off-ramp

The papers say he opened Watson's House of Style in Long Beach in 1956, at 18.[^dp][^hr] Kustomrama puts his first shop in September 1957, after he finished school.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^kr] Either way, the early shops were rough. Christensen remembered a little garage near Nance Chevrolet on Bellflower Boulevard: "He would wet down the floor of an old garage for the sake of dust, and painted inside. It wasn't long before the fire department came and close him down." Roger O'Dell told Kustomrama in May 2020 exactly where it had stood, on Palm Street, where the westbound 91 freeway off-ramp for Bellflower Boulevard runs today. "The shop area is buried under the off ramp."[^kr] After another small garage on Park Street, Watson moved in next to Christensen's muffler shop on Lakewood Boulevard in Paramount, where a group of backyard helpers installed an old paint booth he had found. "Watson was finally legal," as Kustomrama puts it.[^kr] Showing his cars at cruise spots like the Bellflower Clock built him a following at home, and the custom car magazines carried his name to the rest of the country.[^dp][^hr]

## Too bright, so he taped it

In 1958 he set out, in Kustomrama's words, to start a trend of "customizing with paint." He wanted a new Cadillac and couldn't afford one, so he bought a new Ford Thunderbird, after telling a salesman at Downey Ford to call him when the first one with a tuck-and-roll interior came in. Bill Hines and Bill DeCarr at Barris nosed and decked it and rounded the corners. Watson shot six coats of pearl over a fine silver metallic, and he thought it came out too bright; the shine made the car look too large. To fix it, he taped off the body's panel lines with 1-3/4-inch tape and shot candy apple burgundy over the rest. Many think the panel paint job was born on that car, Kustomrama says, though he had already body-paneled Zeno Stephens' 1955 Mercury in Tahitian Red, a car he never saw again.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^kr]

The Barris shop noticed. George Barris had him paint scallops over the mistakes on a candy job, and in 1958 asked him to run the Barris paint department while Barris, Jeffries and Bill Carr went east on a two-month tour. Watson said yes, partly because a pizza parlor was being built in front of his own shop. He stayed about four months.[^kr] Teddy Zgrzemski, the painter's helper, remembered him striping the Thunderbird golden tan, deciding a couple of days later that he didn't like it, and going over all of it in lavender, "and you could never see what the original color was."[^kr] In 1960 or about 1961–62 he laid his first Metalflake job and his first fade on Ron Aguirre's Corvette, the [X-Sonic](../customs/x-sonic-ron-aguirre-rialto.md)<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-5"><a href="#q-5" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>, and painted it six times as Aguirre kept changing it.[^kr] He put a payphone in the shop office, Dave Ellis recalled, because people kept using the business phone for personal calls.[^kr]

## Hollywood, and a museum in a shed

At the peak, in 1966, he sold his shop to go to Mexico and act, with his wife.[^kr][^dp] While he was gone, two other "Watson" shops opened, in Downey and Van Nuys, and when he came back late that year he opened again at 17412 Lakewood Boulevard and handed out flyers warning about the impostors.[^kr] From 1967 to 1985 he appeared in more than 140 projects.[^dp] Kustomrama counts 141 shows, among them *Columbo* and *MacGyver*.[^kr] In the late 1970s he left flyers on hot rods and customs at car shows, hoping to cast them in a movie he wanted to make, *The Spray Booth*. It was never finished.[^kr]

He never left the paint behind. When Ed Roth's Orbitron was found and restored in 2008, Watson, who had painted it years before, was consulted on the original color from memory.[^hr] That October he and Gene Winfield held the first of their custom car and hot rod gatherings at Winfield's shop in the Mojave Desert.[^kr][^hr] He kept a museum of his magazine spreads, photos and trophies at his home in the high desert.[^vv][^kr] He died of brain cancer on July 20, 2010.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^dp][^hr]

The scallops started as a cover for a paint run. The panels started as a cure for a car that looked too big. The man who made them started in a driveway, with brushes from an art store, because a better-known striper wouldn't tell a 16-year-old where to buy his.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did he open his first shop?** *The Downey Patriot* (2010) says that by 1956, at 18, he had opened Watson's House of Style in Long Beach, and *Hot Rod* (2010) also says 1956.[^dp][^hr] Kustomrama says he opened his first paint shop in September 1957, after graduating, and its own photo captions put the House of Style in North Long Beach by 1957 and a small shop at 1016 E. Artesia.[^kr] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **How old was he?** *The Downey Patriot* says he was 69, born in 1938, and that he died on July 20.[^dp] Kustomrama prints July 21, 1938 and July 20, 2010, and says he died "at the age of 70."[^kr] *Hot Rod* gives 1938 and the morning of July 20, and no age.[^hr] Counted from Kustomrama's two dates, July 20, 2010, is one day short of his 72nd birthday, so he was 71. A search excerpt of the *Victor Valley Daily Press* obituary, which we do not quote, has been read as putting that death a day short of turning 71. This review got HTTP 402 again and did not reopen the page.[^vv] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **The desk at Bellflower High?** Jim Williamson's memory of the inked desk is in the *Victor Valley Daily Press* obituary of July 29, 2010. That page is behind a paywall (HTTP 402) and the Internet Archive copy refused our request, so we have read it only as a search-engine excerpt, and we do not quote it.[^vv] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Which car got the first panel job?** Kustomrama says many credit the 1958 Thunderbird, but that Watson had already paneled Zeno Stephens' 1955 Mercury, citing *Barris Kustom Techniques of the 50's*, Vol. 4.[^kr] *Hot Rod* and *The Downey Patriot* credit him with panel painting without naming a first car.[^hr][^dp] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-5" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When was the first Metalflake and the first fade?** Kustomrama's account of Watson dates both, on the X-Sonic, to 1960.[^kr] Kustomrama's X-Sonic page dates the first fade to the first bubble-top car, shown in April 1961, and the gold Metalflake to about 1961–62.
