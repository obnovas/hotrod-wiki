# So People Could See the Upholstery: Tony Nancy, the Sherman Oaks Trimmer Who Went Racing (Los Angeles, 1933 or 1929–2004)

*Twenty-five cents an hour, a roadster to show off the stitching, and a shop that somehow stayed open through it all*

    **Confidence:** Medium (his racing record is in many magazines; the trim-shop side comes from the American Hot Rod Foundation's profile, which quotes his engine builder's interview and his own book, a *Hemmings* remembrance and a Goodguys column; they differ on his birth year and on why he started racing) · **Source types:** Oral history (Tommy Sparks to the American Hot Rod Foundation); a fan's memorial page; *Hemmings* (posted 2018); Goodguys' Fuel Curve; Tom Madigan's book *The Loner*, as quoted by the foundation · **Last verified:** 2026-11-19 (Round 44 citation review) · **Open issues:** ⚠ his birth year; ⚠ why he went racing; ⚠ the date and engine of the Buick roadster; ⚠ how he got the name "The Loner"; ⚠ when the first Wedge crashed; ◐ Clark Gable's padded top · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

## A trade that paid a quarter

Tony Nancy was a car upholsterer first. That is what makes his racing story unusual: one of the best-known drag racers of the 1960s was a trimmer who, by his engine builder's account, went racing to advertise his stitching, and who kept his trim shop open through all of it. "How he ever kept his upholstery shop going was a mystery," *Hemmings* wrote after he died.[^hem][^ahrf]

He was born November 14, 1933, by the American Hot Rod Foundation's account, and went to Hollywood High School.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^ahrf][^hem] A neighbor, Greg Fisher, who raced a '32 roadster, took him to the dry lakes to watch the hot rods. He built his first car, a '29 coupe, at 15, swapped the body for a roadster, and joined the Throttle Merchants of West San Fernando, a Russetta Timing Association club.[^ahrf] Goodguys' Fuel Curve says he started younger in the trade itself: at 14 he had a job at a local upholstery shop, and the $40 he earned bought the '29.[^fc]

He was street racing as a teenager, and the foundation says a local police officer advised him to join the Marine Corps. He served at the end of the Korean War.[^ahrf] When he came home he went to work learning what was then called the trimming trade. The first job paid 25 cents an hour, the foundation says, at a time when sanding cars paid a dollar, and a trimmer fixed his own mistakes on his own time and his own money.[^ahrf] One of those jobs, by Fuel Curve's account, was in the upholstery shop at Hillcrest Cadillac in Hollywood. Clark Gable had flipped his Cadillac coupe and brought it in, and Nancy suggested a padded California top to hide the damage. Other Cadillac owners started asking for one.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-5"><a href="#q-5" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^fc]

## A roadster to carry the stitching

His first shop of his own was Hollywood Custom Auto Interiors, at Gower and Santa Monica, near Tommy Sparks's engine shop. It folded. He went back to working for others, and then opened again in the San Fernando Valley, at Woodman and Ventura Boulevard.[^ahrf] Fuel Curve says he had his own trim shop in Sherman Oaks by 1956.[^fc]

Sparks told the foundation how the racing started. Sparks had a drag-racing Willys that brought customers to his shop, and Nancy wanted the same thing for his. "He wanted to race a roadster, though, so that people could see his upholstery work at the racetrack. So, I made a deal with him, and we took the entire driveline out of my Willys," Sparks said, and they put it in a '29 roadster Nancy had bought.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^ahrf] Nancy numbered it 22, because, he said in Tom Madigan's book, he had already built twenty-two street and dry-lake roadsters before his first real racing one. It was 22 Jr. until his father died.[^ahrf]

A year after he set up shop, the roadster was on the cover of *Hot Rod*'s December 1957 issue.[^ahrf] It was blazing orange and still ran a flathead, four years after Ford had stopped building them, and it held its class ET and top speed records with runs of 135 to 136 mph in the high 10s.[^hem][^fc] The next one was a featherweight '29 on a Kent Fuller tube frame with a Buick nailhead. On a magazine cover with it was his wife, Lillian, and the Model A grille shell had a plexiglass insert with a prancing seahorse.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^hem] Fuller had his shop in Nancy's own building, and so did the engine builder Ed Pink.[^ahrf] In August 1962 the magazines ran "Tony tailors a Tiger," a 1,340-pound T roadster in Lincoln Matador Red with striping by Dean Jeffries, so light it was under the weight for its NHRA class.[^hem]

## The Loner

At the 1963 NHRA Winternationals he won Competition Eliminator in a blown, Plymouth-powered T roadster, the only NHRA national event title of his career, and his first dragster, with an aluminum body by [Jimmy Summers](jimmy-summers.md), won Best Engineered at the same meet.[^ahrf][^hem] He was one of the first with a rear-engined dragster, the Steve Swaja-designed Wedge, and he went upside down through the lights at Sandusky, Ohio, in it.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-6"><a href="#q-6" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^ahrf][^hem] Wedge II was ready in time for him to captain the U.S. team in England in 1964.[^ahrf][^hem] Later, at the Turin auto show in Italy, 400,000 postcards of Wedge II were given away.[^ahrf]

They called him "The Loner." *Hemmings* says it was because he could field a car by himself; in Madigan's book Nancy said it came from wanting to be completely different.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^hem][^ahrf] In 1970 he moved to Top Fuel and won the Bakersfield U.S. Fuel and Gas Championships against a 32-car field, which *Hemmings* calls the last time anyone did it with a front-engined dragster.[^hem] Revell and Wynn's sponsored him, and Revell made model kits of his cars.[^hem][^ahrf]

## Back to the shop

After the 1976 March Meet he quit racing to run the upholstery business full time.[^hem][^ahrf] His customers included Clark Gable, James Garner, Steve McQueen and Burt Reynolds.[^hem] There is a story that he threw McQueen out of the shop; the foundation says they were good friends, and that Nancy reupholstered many of McQueen's cars, including his '57 Jaguar XKSS.[^ahrf] In the 1980 movie *The Hollywood Knights*, when a character asks if a car's color is "piss yellow," the answer is "no, it's Tony Nancy yellow."[^ahrf]

About thirty years after he raced it, he found his Top Fuel car, 22 Sr., in Alaska, and friends including Harry Hibler, Bill Carter, its original painter, and Ed Pink helped him restore it. By then, the foundation says, he was too sick to handle the push start.[^ahrf] He was the grand marshal of the Goodguys March Meet at Famoso in 2003, and he died November 11, 2004.[^hem][^ahrf]

He raced to show off his upholstery, and the racing made him famous. When he was done racing, he went back to the trim shop on Ventura Boulevard.

## Open questions & disputed details

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When was he born?** The American Hot Rod Foundation says November 14, 1933, and Fuel Curve says 1933.[^ahrf][^fc] A memorial page on a racing fan's site heads its tribute "11/14/1929 - 11/11/2004" and then reprints the foundation's text with 1933.[^wag] The front-matter `years` span is `[1929, 2004]`. That field is `[first, last]`, two integers, and 1929 is the earlier year a source gives, so the span covers 1933. It does not decide the question. The title says 1933 or 1929. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Why did he go racing?** Tommy Sparks told the foundation Nancy wanted a roadster to show his upholstery at the track, modeled on Sparks's own Willys.[^ahrf] Fuel Curve says that by 1956 he was doing leather upholstery for race cars and that his view changed "when a customer took him to the drags at San Fernando."[^fc] The two may both be true; neither mentions the other. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When was the Buick roadster on the cover, and how big was the Buick?** *Hemmings* says "April 1960" and a 4-inch-stroked 450-cubic-inch nailhead; Fuel Curve says 1960 and a 450.[^hem][^fc] The foundation says "the April 1962 issue of HOT ROD" and a 482-cubic-inch Buick.[^ahrf] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
