# The Color of a Taillight: Joe Bailon and Candy Apple Red (Hayward, California, 1946–2017)

*By one account, salt in the paint and Christmas-tree flakes through a reworked spray gun, and a red that anyone could copy once they read the instructions*

## A taillight on a wet street

Candy Apple Red, the deep, glowing red that custom painters still lay down, came out of one man's chase after the color of a taillight. Joe Bailon spent about ten years on it, and when he finally had it, in 1956, what he had was a method more than a product. Once a shop had bought his paint and read his instructions, it could paint hundreds of cars without buying another drop from him.[^ahrf][^kr]

Bailon was born March 18, 1923, in Newcastle, California, the youngest of ten children.[^ahrf] The Bay Area sunsets and the orchards across from his childhood home shaped his eye for color, *Hemmings* wrote, and in 1937 he put scallops on the first car he had a hand in customizing, a Model A.[^hem17] After a stint in the Army in World War II, he was driving one night behind another car and became entranced by its glowing taillights. "It was so pretty," he said. "I thought, wouldn't it be nice to see the whole car the color of that taillight?"[^ahrf][^hem17] Kustomrama, drawing on a Spencer Murray magazine story, puts it as a taillight "reflected on a rainy street."[^kr]

## Salt, Christmas flakes and a frenched license plate

He got his first painting job because of a license plate. Bailon had frenched the plate into his 1936 Ford, a trick he had seen Tommy the Greek do on a 1940 Mercury. Parked downtown in Eureka one day, he came back to find a man leaning on the car, curious about who had done it. The man owned Pitt's Paint Shop, and he hired him.[^kr]

He was already chasing sparkle. Before Candy Apple, by the Murray account, Bailon tried to copy the flaky, transparent finish on the sides of a set of Slingerland or Ludwig drums. He mixed salt into paint and got a fair result. Then he tried the silver flakes people sprinkle on Christmas trees, and had to rework a spray gun to get them through the nozzle. Candy Apple did so well afterward that he forgot about the flake idea, "Then before he knew it someone else came along and 'discovered' Metalflake."<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^kr]

## Miss Elegance and the dashboard

The car that made his name was a wrecked 1941 Chevrolet. In a 2012 interview he said it had hit a Navy tow truck and he bought it for $50; the Murray story says it came out of a junkyard after being hit by a train and burned.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^kr] He built a dashboard out of sheet metal, cut holes for the dials, bought every one from Stewart-Warner, and had it chromed in San Francisco for 50 bucks. He wired it all himself. "It was lights all over," Kustomrama's account of the interview says, and he "had no clue what he was doing." He won his first show with the car still in gray primer.[^kr]

The local papers took it seriously. Before the 1952 National Roadster Show in Oakland ([The Show Circuit](../media/show-circuit.md)), show manager Mary L. Slonaker announced a rivalry between George Barris of Los Angeles and Joseph Bailon of San Leandro, whose car had a handmade body, a $1,160 dashboard and a built-in cocktail bar, *The Alameda Times-Star* reported. Bailon brought five entries from the Customs of San Leandro, a "real challenge" to the reigning Kustoms of Los Angeles.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^kr] The Chevy won its first national award there.[^kr] In February 1953 *The Fresno Bee* photographed Bailon and his wife, Margie, with two trophies from the Speedorama in Fresno: Most Popular Automobile from the spectators, and Finest Custom Construction from the judges.[^kr] The car became Miss Elegance. He sold it in the early 1950s, and it was crushed in the 1970s.[^hem17]

The shop was not much. Dick Falk, who started working with him around then, told Kustomrama in 2019 that Bailon was working out of an old one-car garage on Alice Street in Hayward. "It had cement on the floors, but there was a lot of dirt also."[^kr] By then Bailon was picking up jobs all over the Bay Area, and he outgrew the garage and built his own shop.[^kr]

## Gold powder on the bench

He had been at the red for about ten years when it came together. In 1956, he said, he "threw some gold powder on the bench, mixed it with Sherwin-Williams extra-brilliant maroon and clear lacquer, and there it was at last."[^ahrf] Put in painter's order, as *Hemmings* described it, that is a coat of gold, then red, then a clear coat with some red mixed in.[^hem17] Kustomrama describes the red layer as a dye, not a paint.[^kr]

One of the first cars to get it belonged to a Hayward Satan's Angels member, Jerry Sahagon. He worked hand in hand with Bailon on his 1951 Chevrolet club coupe, with a grille of two 1954 Buick bumpers and seven Mercury teeth, and when the body was done it went into Bailon's own Candy Apple mixture. By the account Kustomrama takes from a 1957 *Car Craft* feature, it was the first car ever painted Candy Apple red, finished in about five months for $500.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^krs] In May 1960 it was in the first Hayward Custom Auto Review, held at Perry and Key Body Shop, and the *Daily Review* ran its photo on May 7.[^krs]

Bailon sold his paints in several shades and the instructions to go with them, but Candy Apple "wasn't any tangible product, but a method of application," Kustomrama writes.[^kr] The shops kept using the method without buying his paint.[^hem17] It was everywhere anyway. In 1960 the National Roadster Show put him among the nine first members of its new Hall of Fame, with Barris, Robert E. Petersen and Wally Parks.[^kr] In 1974, in St. Paul, Minnesota, a Bailon-built custom from 1956 still made a sidewalk full of moviegoers stop and stare ([Back to the 50's](../street-rodding/back-to-the-50s-msra-st-paul-1974.md)).

## Back to the radical customs

Later in the 1960s he followed Barris to Southern California to build cars for Hollywood stars and films. He closed that shop in 1984 to go back to the radical customs of his youth.[^hem17] He always regretted selling Miss Elegance, and in the early 1980s he started building another one.[^kr] John Sweeney, who had him on the *Cruisin' Talk* radio show in Sacramento after Bailon moved back to Auburn in the 1990s, remembered that Bailon asked him to help with the re-creation, thinking the KXOA station "had a big promotion budget (not true)."[^cn17] The car was about three quarters done in 2012.[^kr]

Bailon died September 25, 2017, at 94, two days after a stroke.[^ahrf][^kr] Miss Elegance 2 was still unfinished; in December 2018 Kevan Sledge of Sledge Customs became its caretaker.[^kr] At the San Francisco Rod, Custom and Motorcycle Show, the Joe Bailon Elegance Award trophy is topped with a Candy Apple Red apple that Bailon hand-painted himself.[^ahrf]

The red is in every paint shop's vocabulary now. What it came from was a man who wanted a whole car the color of the taillight in front of him, and who gave the method away with the paint.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Where did the salt, the Christmas-tree flakes and "Metalflake" come from?** The drum finish, the salt, the Christmas-tree flakes and the reworked spray gun come from one account, Spencer Murray's "Joe Bailon Custom Comeback," as summarized by Kustomrama.[^kr] *Hemmings* says only that he experimented with adding silver and metalflake to paint at his first painting job.[^hem17] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **How was the '41 Chevy wrecked?** Both versions reach us through one compiler, Kustomrama: Bailon told Jonnie King in 2012 that it had hit a Navy tow truck and he paid $50 for it; Spencer Murray's story says it had been hit by a train, burned and almost cut in half.[^kr] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **How many gauges?** All three counts reach us through Kustomrama: Kustomrama's account of the 2012 interview has 14 holes for the dials; *The Alameda Times-Star* (Jan. 31, 1952) gave 15 gauges and 32 push buttons; an *Oakland Tribune* photo story (Feb. 17, 1952) gave 13 gauges, and a $1,180 price where the other reports say $1,160.[^kr] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Was Sahagon's Chevy the first Candy Apple car?** Kustomrama says so, citing *Car Craft*, July 1957.[^krs] We have not read the *Car Craft* feature, and *Hemmings* names the 1958 Candy Bird as one of Bailon's most famous candy cars without calling anything the first.[^hem17] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
