# A Street Rod Wife and a Two-Year Wait: Don's Trim Shop and Jay's Hot Rod Upholstery (Hixson, Tennessee, and Pine Island, Minnesota, 1956–2026)

*An upholsterer who made his name at the shows, the wife who went with him, and a Minnesota trimmer who learned it before YouTube*

## Known at the shows

The inside of a street rod is somebody's handwork, and very often that somebody runs a small family trim shop a long way from the big shop towns. Two of them show how it goes. The trade is learned by hand and handed down, and the shop's name gets known by going to the shows.

Don Angland was born in 1932 and served in the U.S. Air Force. His wife, Barbara, traveled with him through his Air Force years, and they were married 74 years.[^lg][^ctn][^tfp] In 1956, his obituary says, he "laid the foundation for what would become a pillar to the street rod community," in a trade he loved, upholstery.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^ctn] The shop came to be called Don's Trim Shop, in Hixson, Tennessee.[^ctn][^ab]

What made the name was the road. "Traveling to car shows all across the U.S., it wasn't long before hot rod enthusiasts, builders, and collectors knew his name," the family wrote.[^ctn] Barbara went too. When she died in 2023, at 89, her obituary said she "was a street rod wife, always traveling to car shows with her husband and many great friends they met along the way." The two of them traveled through 48 states. She also liked camping at Tellico, reading by the creek.[^tfp]

The shop's work turns up in other builders' credits. When Big House Customs, a Mustang shop in Dayton, Tennessee, rebuilt a '70 Mustang fastback that had been torn in half in a wreck, it did the interior with Don's Trim Shop: a 14-point rollcage around a pair of PROCAR seats with swing-out door bars.[^ab] Don Angland died at home in Hixson on June 16, 2026, at 94. His son and daughter run the shop, the family wrote, and keep up "the artistry he taught them."[^ctn]

## Learned by mistakes

Jay Peterson came to it differently. His godmother, Pat Nelson, upholstered furniture. He loved old cars and had "no desire to do body work or engine repair," and there were not many people around Pine Island, Minnesota, who did car interiors, so he took that up. He opened Jay's Hot Rod Upholstery in 1998.[^pb]

There was no YouTube to learn from. "You kind of just stumble your way through it," he said. At first he took anything, from boats to tractors.[^pb] He had finished two-year technical programs in mechanical drafting and machine tool theory, and some of those skills come in handy. A job usually starts with a sketch, sometimes drawn in chalk right on the car's interior, and he builds in fiberglass, aluminum, plastic and wood. A car takes about 160 hours.[^pb]

By 2022 he worked almost only on custom vintage cars, five or six interiors a year with his son, Cole, who was 21, and he had about a two-year wait. Customers came from Florida and Arizona. The oldest car had been a 1923 Ford T-bucket. He had used Italian leather, German carpet, snakeskin and ostrich, and once a deer hunter brought in a cured deer hide for his project.[^pb] His interiors had gone to the Louisville Street Rod Nationals and the SEMA show, and some of the cars had won the Minnesota Street Rod Association's Street Rod of the Year.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^pb] He was starting to see more cars from the 1970s. "The stuff that we liked when we were young is starting to get redone," he said.[^pb]

In Hixson the trade went from a father to his son and daughter; in Pine Island it came from a godmother's furniture, and the son now works beside his father. Both shops got known the same way, one car at a time, at the shows.
