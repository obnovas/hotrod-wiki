# Three Shops and a Freeway: The Alexander Brothers (Detroit, 1957–1969)

*Two brothers from a trade school, customs built a few miles from the factories, and a city that kept paving over their shop*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** High (Mike Alexander's family obituary, *Autoweek*'s 2010 obituary of Larry and a 2019 profile built on Mike's own telling agree on the cars, the awards and the closing; they differ on when and where the brothers started) · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> Mike Alexander's family obituary (Martenson Funeral Home, 2014); Dale Jewett's obituary of Larry Alexander in *Autoweek* (2010); Ronnie Schreiber's profile for Hagerty (2019), from his talks with Mike; Wesley Wren's search for the shops in *Autoweek*, quoting Mike's interview with the Mad Fabricators Society; Kustomrama's Bill Hines page, from interviews with Hines's nephew · **Last verified:** 2026-11-26 (Round 51) · **Open issues:** ⚠ when they went to trade school; ⚠ how many times the highways moved them; ◐ the $300 for the Deora · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

## Built near the factories

The Alexander brothers were Detroit's own show-car builders. They worked a few miles from the automakers, for local kids and for the automakers themselves, and they won the Autorama's top prize three times. What sets their story apart is how it ended. Every shop they had was in the path of Detroit's freeways.[^awshops][^aw10]

Larry and Mike Alexander grew up in the Detroit area during the Depression and the war. After Army service, both learned body work at the Wolverine Trade School on the GI Bill.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>[^hag19][^obit14] Mike leaned toward the mechanical side, but Larry talked him into learning body and paint too. They started on nights and weekends in their parents' garage, shaving trim and filling holes, then moved to the two-car garage at Larry's new house.[^hag19] Ronnie Schreiber, who talked with Mike for his 2019 profile, points out a Detroit difference. Salt and winters left fewer old cars to work with than in California, so Detroit customizers often worked on newer cars, cutting out rusted panels and replacing them with reshaped metal.[^hag19]

In 1957 they quit their day jobs and opened the Alexander Brothers Custom Shop near Northwestern Highway and Evergreen Road.[^hag19] Mike remembered it as a small shop with cracks, a dirt floor and "a $50 compressor that pumped more water and oil, I think, than air." It was not open long before it was knocked down for the freeway system, and they moved to 13131 Littlefield, at Grand River.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>[^awshops]

## The Silver Sapphire and the hovercraft

One of their early customers was a teenager. In 1955 Clarence "Chili" Catallo bought a 1932 Ford three-window coupe for $74 and brought it to the Alexanders for body and paint. They channeled and sectioned it and built a nose with four stacked headlights. Catallo drove it to Los Angeles in 1958, worked sweeping floors at George Barris's shop, and had the top chopped there. It was on the cover of *Hot Rod* in July 1961 and, as the Silver Sapphire, on the cover of the Beach Boys' *Little Deuce Coupe*.[^hag19][^aw10]

Their break into the national magazines came from a repair job. In 1961 Barris brought his XPAK 400 hovercraft to the Detroit Autorama, and it would not run properly. He asked who the best mechanics in town were, and the Autorama crew sent him to the Alexanders, who fixed it in time for the show. Back in California, Barris talked them up to the editors and called them the "East Coast division of Barris Industries." Mike, telling the story to Schreiber, laughed about it. He was grateful.[^hag19] In Detroit they also traded work with Bill Hines, the Leadslinger. Overloaded with candy paint jobs, Hines hired the brothers for body work, and they took candy lessons and paint in return. Their first big candy job, on the Mroz brothers' 1950 Ford, was done under his eye, Kustomrama says.[^kr-bh]

Littlefield turned out the Victorian, Sy Gregorich's 1955 Crown Victoria, and the Grasshopper, a 1931 Model A pickup.[^awshops][^aw10] Hines and his 14-year-old nephew, Teddy Zgrzemski, did the Victorian's scallops in 1960. Teddy got $15 for the pinstriping, and years later he still called it "The nicest car the Alexander Brothers built I thought."[^kr-bh] Then the freeway came again, and they moved to 21249 Schoolcraft Road. Wren writes that this time they were promised the city would not take the land.[^awshops]

## Three Ridlers in five years

At Schoolcraft they won the Autorama's Ridler Award three times: in 1965 with the Venturian, a 1956 Chevrolet for Bobby Massaron; in 1967 with the Deora; and in 1969 with Top Banana, a 1923 Ford Model T.[^aw10][^obit14] The Deora started as a Dodge A100 that Chrysler handed them, drawn by Harry Bradley, who was moonlighting from General Motors.[^awshops][^hag19] They used Mustang taillight housings turned sideways for exhaust ports, and a Ford station wagon roof and glass hatch for the windshield. Dodge leased it and toured it.[^hag19] Mattel made it one of the first Hot Wheels in 1968.[^aw10] Larry said they were paid $300 for the rights.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see the bottom of the page">◐</a></sup>[^awshops] They also built for Ford's Custom Car Caravan and fabricated American Motors' AMX-GT concept.[^hag19]

The Schoolcraft shop was marked for the freeway too. Larry went to Ford in 1968 as a metal model maker. Mike finished Top Banana there, and the business closed in 1969 when the building was razed.[^aw10][^awshops] Schreiber writes that custom work was labor-heavy and not very profitable, and the brothers could find steady work in the industry.[^hag19] Mike ran body shop operations at American Sunroof Corporation, became one of its vice presidents, and was awarded 19 patents for retractable hardtop roofs.[^hag19][^obit14]

Larry died on August 25, 2010, at 79.[^aw10] Mike came back to the Autorama one more time: at 76, in 2012, his Vision 33 roadster, drawn by Chip Foose and built with his son, was one of the Ridler's Great Eight. He lived on Grosse Ile, and he died on July 18, 2014, at 80.[^hag19][^obit14] The Autorama still gives the A. Brothers Award, which the brothers set up, for work that is "clean and neat."[^hag19] When Wren went looking for the shops where the Deora and the Silver Sapphire were built, he found the addresses. "FYI, they're just roads," he wrote.[^awshops] The cars survived. The shops became freeway.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did they go to trade school?** Schreiber writes that both learned body work at the Wolverine Trade School "under the GI Bill after completing Army service in the late 1940s and early 1950s."[^hag19] Larry's *Autoweek* obituary says they "learned their auto bodywork skills in trade school in the mid 1950s and began working in their father's garage."[^aw10] Wren calls the first garage their parents' "one-and-a-half-car garage"; Schreiber calls it a one-car garage.[^awshops][^hag19] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **How many times did the highways move them?** Wren writes that the first shop "was knocked down by Detroit to expand the freeway system," that a second expansion forced them off Littlefield, and that Schoolcraft was "eventually marked for demolition for highway expansion."[^awshops] Schreiber writes that at the end they were "Faced with having to move their shop for the second time due to highway construction."[^hag19] Schreiber puts the first shop "just outside Detroit"; *Autoweek*'s obituary says the original shop was "in Detroit."[^hag19][^aw10] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Were they paid $300 for the Deora?** Only Wren reports it: "According to Larry, they got a whopping $300 for the rights to Deora."[^awshops] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
