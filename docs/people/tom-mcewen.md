# Tom McEwen: The Mongoose, from a Borrowed Oldsmobile to Hot Wheels (1937–2018)

*A Long Beach kid in his mother's Oldsmobile, a name borrowed from Kipling, and a toy-company rivalry that put Funny Cars in living rooms*

## A borrowed Oldsmobile

Tom McEwen was born on January 14, 1937, in Pensacola, Florida, according to the Motorsports Hall of Fame. A 2018 remembrance by V. Moore says he moved to California with his mother and younger brother, Richard, after his father's early death, and grew up in Long Beach.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^mshf][^moore18]

He began by sneaking his mother's 1953 Oldsmobile out to race at Santa Ana before he was old enough to have a driver's license, the hall of fame says. NHRA and Hemmings date the start to 1953, at age 16, in that same '53 Olds at Santa Ana Dragstrip in Irvine; NHRA adds a '54 Olds at Lions Dragstrip in Long Beach. He moved up through gas coupes, supercharged gassers and altereds. The hall of fame says Art Chrisman coached him in dragster driving, and that he later drove for owners including Ed Donovan, Gene Adams, Lou Baney, Chrysler and Ford.[^mshf][^nhra18][^hemm18]

In 1962 he drove Adams's Shark car, which NHRA calls one of the first dragsters to use a streamlined body with an enclosed chute pack. In 1963 he was runner-up to Art Malone at the Bakersfield March Meet in the Broussard-Garrison-Purcell-Davis car.[^nhra18]

## An animal that kills snakes

On September 12, 1964, at Lions, McEwen beat [Don Prudhomme](don-prudhomme.md) two straight sets. Prudhomme was in the Greer-Black-Prudhomme dragster; McEwen was in Ed Donovan's Donovan Engineering Special. The win, NHRA wrote, inspired "what may be the most famous match-race pairing in the history of drag racing." The hall of fame says Donovan, "recalling Kipling's 'Jungle Book,'" nicknamed him the Mongoose after that match. NHRA and Hemmings say the name was also a device to entice "the Snake" into a high-exposure rematch. He would sign autographs with a dollar sign in place of the "s."[^nhra18][^mshf][^hemm18]

Two more Lions matches followed in 1965. McEwen, in the Yeakel Plymouth, beat Prudhomme and Roland Leong's Hawaiian two rounds to one, then lost two straight in the second meeting. In 1966 they met once, at the Winternationals: Prudhomme's B&M Torkmaster Special took a 7.59 to 7.69 win, and they did not meet again for the rest of the decade. McEwen stayed on the West Coast while Prudhomme toured. He won the 1966 *Hot Rod Magazine* Championships at Riverside and the 1968 Stardust National Open in Las Vegas, and at the 1968 Orange County PDA Meet he ran a 6.64 in Kenny Lindley's dragster, which NHRA called the lowest elapsed time ever at that point.[^nhra18]

He theorized, the hall of fame says, that "a big following and good performance is better for sponsors than big wins and no following." In the 1960s he took on what Greg Sharp called an Ali-like persona, vowing in ads what he would do to opponents, flashing a "V" for victory from the cockpit and playing "a part much like the villain in wrestling."[^mshf]

## Wildlife Racing

McEwen's mother, Sybil, was a secretary at Mattel; the rest of how that meeting happened is told on the [Prudhomme page](don-prudhomme.md). In mid-1969 he and Prudhomme formed Wildlife Racing. The Mattel deal ran from 1970 through 1972. Kids raced Snake and Mongoose Hot Wheels on orange tracks; Hemmings says it was McEwen who came up with the idea to approach his mother's employer. In 1973 the team ran Carefree sugarless gum. They dissolved the corporation at the end of that season.[^nhra18][^hemm18][^prudhomme]

"We were a good team; we complemented each other," McEwen said. "Don was the serious guy, spent a lot of time with his car, and I was more like the wrestlers today; saying how bad I was going to beat him to build interest in the deal."[^nhra18]

The first major win NHRA credits him with is Top Fuel at the 1972 Bakersfield March Meet. The hall of fame adds Funny Car at Lions' "Last Drag Race" that same year, "where he had probably made more passes than any other driver." In 1973 he scored his first NHRA national-event victory in Funny Car at the SuperNationals at Ontario Motor Speedway, "outlasting the quickest Funny Car field in history," NHRA wrote.[^nhra18][^mshf]

He won four more NHRA national events after that, NHRA says, for five in a career of more than 35 years. One of them was the 1978 U.S. Nationals Funny Car final against Prudhomme, after the death of McEwen's son. How the sources name the boy, how old he was, and how many days before Indianapolis he died do not line up.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> The hall of fame says McEwen ran the best time of his career that day and that the two men "shared an emotional, tearful embrace in the shutoff area." It also says he spent hours visiting children in hospitals and raised money for the Leukemia Society in the boy's memory. He later won the 1984 Big Bud Shootout and Top Fuel at the 1991 Summernationals, becoming, NHRA says, one of a short list of drivers to have won in both nitro classes.[^nhra18][^mshf][^hemm18]

Moore's remembrance, the only source this round that gives the later family details, says another son, Joey, died at 35 in a car collision, and that McEwen won three AHRA Funny Car championships, in 1976, 1978 and 1983 (see Open questions). In 1985 he was in the first Funny Car field at [Firebird International Raceway](../drag-racing/firebird-international-raceway-chandler.md) outside Phoenix, and lost in the first round to Ed McCulloch after a magneto was installed incorrectly. At [Orange County International Raceway](../drag-racing/orange-county-international-raceway.md)'s opening day in 1967 he had set the new strip's first track record, a 7.02, then red-lighted in the final; he did not make the eight-car Funny Car field at that track's Last Drag Race in 1983.[^nhra85][^ocir][^moore18]

He was voted No. 16 on NHRA's Top 50 Drivers list in 2001 and was inducted into the Motorsports Hall of Fame of America and the International Drag Racing Hall of Fame. In 2018 he helped put together the NHRA Legends Tour and had already appeared in Las Vegas and Houston. He died in June 2018, at 81; NHRA and Hemmings give June 10, the hall of fame gives June 11, and Moore writes that he died in his sleep sometime Sunday night or Monday morning, June 10–11.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> Moore says he was survived by his son Tom and his daughter Katie.[^nhra18][^hemm18][^mshf][^moore18]

From a Long Beach hospital bed in 1970, [Don Garlits](don-garlits.md) watched *Star Trek* with McEwen and sketched the rear-engine car; that story stays on Garlits's page.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Did Tom McEwen die on June 10 or June 11, 2018?** NHRA's obituary, posted June 11, says he "died June 10. He was 81." Hemmings, the next day, says he died "On Sunday, June 10." The Motorsports Hall of Fame page gives the death date as June 11, 2018. Moore writes that he "died in his sleep sometime on Sunday night or Monday morning 10-11 June 2018."[^nhra18][^hemm18][^mshf][^moore18] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **What was his son's name and age, and how soon before the 1978 U.S. Nationals did he die?** NHRA's 2018 obituary says "the death just a few days earlier of his son, Jaime." The hall of fame calls him "his teen-aged son Jamie" and says he "had died from leukemia only weeks earlier." Hemmings, quoting Prudhomme's April 2017 *Hemmings Muscle Machines* column, says McEwen "had lost his 14-year old son, Jamie, to cancer" the week before Indianapolis. Moore says "Jamie died at the age of 13 of leukemia." Moore also writes that a movie tribute was to McEwen's "memorable victory over Don Prudhomme during the 1988 NHRA Funny Car US Nationals at Indianapolis, ten days after the tragic death of his youngest son." NHRA, the hall of fame and Hemmings all put that final in 1978, so Moore's year is off; the "ten days" is a fourth interval beside a few days, a week and weeks. The sources agree the boy had died of leukemia (or, in Hemmings' wording, cancer) and that the Indianapolis final was against Prudhomme.[^nhra18][^mshf][^hemm18][^moore18] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Do the Long Beach childhood, the AHRA titles and the second son's death rest on more than one telling?** The move from Florida after his father's death, the brother Richard, growing up in Long Beach, the AHRA Funny Car championships of 1976, 1978 and 1983, and the death of a son named Joey at 35 in a car collision all come from V. Moore's June 12, 2018, remembrance. The hall of fame gives the Pensacola birth and is silent on Long Beach; NHRA and Hemmings do not mention the AHRA titles or Joey.[^moore18][^mshf] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
