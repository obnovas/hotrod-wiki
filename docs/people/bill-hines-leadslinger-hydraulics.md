# "Bondo Is Poison": Bill Hines, the Leadslinger Who Put Lifts on Customs (Ecorse, Lincoln Park and Lynwood, 1941–2016)

*A Downriver body man who worked the old way, and helped start the newest trick in customs*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium (most of the Michigan years come from his nephew's memories, recorded by Kustomrama between 2019 and 2021; his career and death are in two 2016 obituaries; the hydraulics dates disagree) · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> family and customer accounts gathered by Kustomrama (nephew Teddy Zgrzemski, 2019–21; Bob Nugent, 2019; Dave Ellis, 2020), with older interviews it quotes (*Custom Rodder*, 2004; Driving.ca, 2014; *Autoweek*, 2015); obituaries in *Hemmings* and *Street Muscle* (2016) · **Last verified:** 2026-11-24 (Round 49) · **Open issues:** ⚠ where his first gas station was; ⚠ 1961 or 1962 for the first lifts; ◐ Michigan's first lifted car · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "Go through with the front bumper 2 inches off the ground. 10 minutes later. 12 inches."

    — Teddy Zgrzemski, on cruising the drive-ins in the Nomad his uncle Bill Hines lifted, told to Sondre Kvipt of Kustomrama in 2020.[^kr-hyd]

## A tin building with a dirt floor

Bill Hines worked the oldest way there was. He shaped bodies in lead and said filler was poison. He is also remembered as the Godfather of Hydraulics, one of the first men to put lifts on customs in Southern California. And by his nephew's account, he carried the lifts back to Michigan on a family Nomad.

He was born in Erie, Pennsylvania, in 1922, was raised at first in Jackson, Tennessee, and moved to Detroit in 1932.[^hem16] He left school, and in 1941 he rented a garage in Ecorse, Michigan, for custom bodywork. His first business, his nephew Teddy Zgrzemski remembered, was a gas station in Ecorse, and after that he had three shops there.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^kr-bh][^hem16] "Six days a week," Teddy said. "The body shop was his whole income."[^kr-bh]

In the late 1940s he painted used cars at a Nash dealership in River Rouge owned by Vick Sawitskas. Vick's body men could replace anything that unbolted, but the heavy work, quarter panels and lead, went to an outside man in a tin building with a dirt floor. That was Hines. Vick's son Dick hung around and watched, and Hines taught him to work lead. Dick went on to become the customizer Dick Dean.[^kr-bh][^hem16]

Hines built Marty Ribits's 1934 Ford, which won Best Custom at the 1953 Detroit Autorama.[^kr-bh][^hem16] Local work paid by the job. In 1956 Bob Nugent had him paint a new set of fender skirts black for $15, and heat his front springs for the same $15.[^kr-bh] At his shop on Dix Toledo Highway in Lincoln Park he finished a 1950 Ford a customer had given up on, and it became his own car, the Bat. It had huge fins with 1956 Ford taillights in them, and it made the cover of *Rod & Custom* in March 1959.[^kr-bh]

## Detroit to Lynwood and back

In April 1958 he moved the family to California in a house trailer he pulled behind the Bat. At Barris Kustoms the manager, Gene Simmons, saw the Bat and asked who built it. Hines said he had. It was a Wednesday. Hines offered to start Monday. "How about tomorrow?" Simmons said, and Hines started the next day.[^kr-bh] It did not last. "George didn't know me and gave me the brush off, so I went back to Michigan," he said in 2014.[^kr-bh]

He spent 1959 in Detroit. He gave Jerry Yatch's new 1959 Impala a Candy Apple Red paint job, which his nephew believed was the first in Michigan, and he walked his friends Mike and Larry Alexander through the formulas and their first candy job.[^kr-bh] Teddy was 14 in 1960 when he taped off the scallops on Sy Gregorich's Crown Victoria for his uncle to paint. He pinstriped the edges the next day. "I got 15 dollars for doing it," he remembered.[^kr-bh]

In October 1960 Hines went west for good. George Barris would not pay him more than $100 a week, so he opened his own shop on the side, at night, on the lot where Eddie Martinez had his upholstery shop in Lynwood.[^kr-bh] In time it became Bill Hines Kustom Auto, and he ran it full time for 23 years.[^sm16] "In California, half the work is done out on the pavement," Teddy said. "Uncle Bill would put hydraulics in and everything sitting out in front of the shop, not in the shop."[^kr-bh] In 1961 he bought a house in Garden Grove for $17,000. On the way to work every morning, Teddy remembered, they drove under the Disneyland Monorail. He never moved.[^kr-bh]

## The Buddah Buggy and the Nomad

Hines met Ron Aguirre, whose [X-Sonic](../customs/x-sonic-ron-aguirre-rialto.md) Corvette went up and down on hydraulics, and he put a full set of lifts on Tats Gotanda's 1959 Impala, the Buddah Buggy. It could rise and drop 8½ inches.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^kr-hyd][^kr-bh] After that his was one of the first shops in Southern California to specialize in lifts, and customers came down from Northern California.[^kr-hyd][^sm16] Richard Zocchi of Concord had Hines lift his new 1964 Buick Wildcat. Ronnie Baird drove his 1965 Impala down from the Pittsburg area in 1965 or 1966 for lifts front and back.[^kr-hyd]

In 1964 or 1965 Teddy bought a 1957 Nomad that had come from California, and he wanted it lifted. Nobody in Michigan did hydraulics then. Hines made up the parts ahead of time and brought them along on a vacation to Michigan. They had no arc welder, so they borrowed the one at the shop next door, Wyandotte Frame and Axle. They lifted only the front, because Teddy liked a California rake. He drove it every day for two or three years, towed a ski boat 195 miles up north to his mother's place, and never once added fluid. Teddy was pretty sure it was the first lifted car in Michigan.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^kr-hyd]

By the early 1980s, with costs rising, Hines stopped putting in hydraulics, and went on restoring old cars and building customs.[^kr-hyd] He worked with an El Producto cigar in his mouth and held to two rules: "Bondo is poison, and every custom should be Candy Red with Tan or White accents."[^sm16] A new generation met him on Jesse James's *Monster Garage* in 2004.[^hem16] In 2015 he told *Autoweek*'s Mark Vaughn he would never retire: "No, I'll have a garage long as I live. Period."[^kr-bh] He had a heart attack in December 2015 and died at home on May 20, 2016, at 94.[^sm16][^kr-bh] The man who said Bondo was poison spent his life in lead, and he is remembered for putting customs on lifts.

*See also:* [Larry Watson](larry-watson-bellflower-paint.md), who was minding the Barris paint department when Hines arrived; [Joe Bailon](joe-bailon-candy-apple.md), for Candy Apple Red; [We're the Slowest Ones](../places/intruders-phoenix-lowriders-cruising.md) and [Dave's Dream](../customs/daves-dream-chimayo-lowrider.md), where the lifts went next; [The Alexander Brothers](alexander-brothers-detroit-customizers.md), who traded body work for his candy lessons.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Was his first gas station in Ecorse or Lincoln Park?** Kustomrama, from Teddy Zgrzemski, says "Bill's first business venture was a gas station in Ecorse," then three shops in Ecorse, then a new shop in Lincoln Park.[^kr-bh] *Hemmings* says that after leaving school "Hines opened a gas station in Lincoln Park, Michigan," where he began doing custom bodywork and paint.[^hem16] *Street Muscle* says he started at "a local gas station in Lincoln Park" after dropping out in 1941.[^sm16] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Were the Buddah Buggy's lifts put in in 1961 or 1962?** Kustomrama's Hines page says "In 1962, after meeting Ron Aguirre," and its hydraulics page dates it 1962, but a caption on the Hines page says he restyled the car "in 1961" and that it "was the first car that Bill installed hydraulic lifts on."[^kr-bh][^kr-hyd] Kustomrama also spells the car both "Buddah" and "Buddha"; *Hemmings* has "Buddah."[^hem16] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Was Teddy's Nomad the first lifted car in Michigan?** Kustomrama calls it "the first known lifted car in Michigan" on Teddy's word, and its caption says "what might be the first hydraulic lifted car in Michigan."[^kr-hyd] We have no Michigan source from the 1960s. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

*A note on the dates:* Kustomrama says Hines went west in April 1958 and stayed "about nine months," and also that he went back to Detroit "in the fall of 1958." The story here says only that he spent 1959 in Detroit.[^kr-bh]
