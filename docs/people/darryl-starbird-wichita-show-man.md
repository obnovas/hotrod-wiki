# More From One Show Than a Year in the Shop: Darryl Starbird, Wichita's Customizer and Show Man (Wichita, Kansas, and Afton, Oklahoma, 1954–2025)

*A chicken-coop body shop by the tracks, a rented hall, 3,000 people, and a family car show that outlived the custom craze*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium (the Wichita years come from the *Wichita Eagle*'s interviews with him in 2011, his family's shop history and two Goodguys profiles, one drawing on his autobiography; the dates and show counts are his own and drift between tellings) · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> the *Wichita Eagle* (January and September 2011); Donna Starbird's obituary (*Wichita Eagle*, 2021); the Star Kustom Shop's own history, written by his grandson's shop; FOX23 Tulsa (2025); the *Goodguys Gazette* (2018) and Fuel Curve (2022) · **Last verified:** 2026-11-21 (Round 46) · **Open issues:** ⚠ when he built the Predicta; ⚠ how many shows he has put on; ◐ the million Predicta models · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "I made more money with that one show than I made for the entire year with the shop, so, I decided to go into the car show business."

    — Darryl Starbird, to Hurst Laviana of the *Wichita Eagle*, 2011, on his first show in 1957.[^we11]

## A chicken coop by the tracks

Darryl Starbird is remembered for bubble tops, but the thing he built that lasted longest was a hometown car show. He put on the first one in Wichita in 1957 to explain what a custom car was. It paid better than the shop, so he kept doing it, and his family is still doing it.

He was born in Topeka in 1933. During the war his father, Austin, went to work at Boeing in Wichita, and the family moved there.[^gg] At 10 he and his older brother Kenny set out to build their first car, a 1926 Model T coupe Kenny bought for $25. It had no title and no receipt, and the police took it.[^gg] At about 14 he learned to braze with the welding equipment his father had at the house. He bought and sold old cars, painting fenders and fixing them up, and sold so many that he needed a Kansas dealer license at 16.[^fc][^gg] His first custom was a '41 Ford convertible in high school, with a Continental kit, a '48 Chrysler rear bumper and fender skirts.[^gg] He did a '47 Cadillac next, before he had a shop. "I still have that car," he said in 2022. "I found it after many years and bought it back."[^fc]

He finished at Wichita East High in 1951, tried California and came home, and married his high school sweetheart, Donna Gray, in 1953.[^we11b][^gg] He studied aeronautical engineering at what is now Wichita State and worked nights at Boeing.[^we11b][^gg] In 1954 he quit school and opened the Star Kustom Shop at 734 E. Mt. Vernon. It was an old chicken coop with a dirt floor, and it sat so close to the railroad track that one night a train took off the back end of a '51 Ford.[^ska][^gg] He was 21.[^ska]

Customizing was, in his family's words, "a virtually unknown craft" in an airplane town, so he told people about it, and told the magazines.[^ska] "I spent my last $350 to buy a Rolleiflex camera," he said. He photographed every job in the shop as a how-to story, for *Rod & Custom*, for Petersen, and for about a dozen small magazines out East. "The first few years I made more money selling stories than doing the work."[^fc] He met Spence Murray, *Rod & Custom*'s editor, at a car show in Kansas, and Murray wanted Midwest material.[^gg]

## The show at the Forum

In 1957 he rented space in the old Forum in downtown Wichita to promote the shop and put up some posters. "I didn't buy any advertising," he told the *Eagle*. "I would've been happy if I had drawn a couple of hundred people. Much to my amazement, I had 3,000 people show up."[^we11] More than 50 cars were on display.[^we11b] He had done it, he said in 2022, "primarily just to show people what custom cars were all about, because nobody really understood what you meant when you said 'custom car.'" Then he went home, bought a new '59 Buick and customized it.[^fc] The one show made him more than the shop had all year, and he went into the car show business.[^we11]

The cars got wilder. His best-known, the Predicta, started as a 1956 Thunderbird he bought from a North Broadway salvage yard after it had been hit by a train.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^we11] He built it in 11 weeks, and it won Car of the Future at the Grand National Roadster Show in Oakland.[^gg] "When I started the Predicta, I had no idea how to build a bubble top," he said. "Nobody seemed to know anything about it."[^fc] He made show appearances for Monogram the way Barris, Winfield and Roth did for AMT, and he said more than a million plastic Predicta models were sold.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>[^fc][^we11] At a show at the Cow Palace in San Francisco, a young filmmaker named George Lucas took an interest in the car and later asked to use Starbird's name in a movie. "I thought it was some little B movie that would never amount to much," Starbird said. It was *American Graffiti*.[^we11]

The Star Kustom Shop lasted nine years. In 1963 he opened a bigger place, Starbird Custom Auto Creations.[^ska] When the muscle cars came, he said, "about every shop in the world closed except Barris and me."[^fc] The show business is what grew. At its height the show ran in 15 cities before he cut it back to Wichita and Tulsa.[^we11b] Donna was co-owner of the show company, National Show Producers.[^dso] By January 2011, when he was 77, the *Eagle* counted his Century II show as his 445th; he has given other counts since.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>[^we11] He had built more than 100 cars, and many sat in the National Rod & Custom Car Hall of Fame Museum he founded in 1995 in Afton, Oklahoma.[^we11][^gg]

## Handing it down

That September he sold the Wichita show to five local partners, among them Tom Devlin and his son Tim, of Devlin Rod & Customs, who planned to give the profits to charity. "It's very difficult to give up something you've put 55 years of effort into," he said.[^we11b] Donna died in 2021, at 87.[^dso] In 2022, at 89, he agreed to merge the museum into Speedway Motors' Museum of American Speed. "Frankly, when I walk through the empty building, I almost get tears," he said. He was still in the shop every day, building a bubble top he called the Extreme Executive Cruiser, "an office on wheels." "You know me, I like to be different."[^fc]

The family has the rest. A grandson, Dakota Wentz, reopened the Star Kustom Shop name in San Dimas, California, in 2008, at 26.[^ska] The Tulsa show passed to Darryl's son Cliff and daughter Cristy. In February 2025 it was the 61st Darryl Starbird National Rod and Custom Show, with more than 1,000 cars. "My grandfather has been promoting car shows for nearly 70 years," said Cristy's son, Brice Bledsoe, who helps run it. "It's definitely a family business."[^fox] The show he started to explain the word "custom" is still going, and still a Starbird show.

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When did he build the Predicta?** Fuel Curve (2022) says he "garnered national attention in 1958" with a *Rod & Custom* cover and that "A year later he debuted his Predicta."[^fc] The *Wichita Eagle* (2011) says it was "a bubble-top car that he built in the early 1960s."[^we11] The family shop's history lists it among the Star Kustom Shop's cars and says bubble tops had swept the custom world "By the end of 1960."[^ska] The Grand National Roadster Show's Car of the Future records would date it. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **How many shows has he put on?** The *Eagle* called the January 2011 Century II show "Starbird's 445th car show if you count the ones he's put on in other parts of the country."[^we11] In 2022 he told the *Goodguys Gazette*, "I've held 439 car shows around the country."[^fc] The 2018 *Gazette* profile says "more than 400."[^gg] The later count is lower than the earlier one, so at least one is a round guess. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **A million Predicta models?** Only the *Eagle*'s 2011 story gives the figure, as Starbird's own statement: "more than 1 million plastic model replicas of the Predicta have since been sold."[^we11] The model maker's production records would confirm it. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
