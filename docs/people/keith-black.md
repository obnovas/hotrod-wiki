# Keith Black: Rings in the Driveway and the KB Block (1926–1991)

*He took the family car apart in the driveway at about 14, built boat engines in the backyard, and the aluminum block is the KB*

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium (Black's own words in a hall-of-fame biography, his son Ken's interview and the Garlits and SEMA hall notices agree on the shop, the boats and the Chrysler work; they split on his birth year, the driveway story's details, the Greer-Black-Prudhomme win count and whether the famous aluminum block followed a 392 or a 426) · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> family account (Ken Black to Hemmings, undated, posted 2018), Motorsports Hall of Fame of America nomination biography (1995, with first-person quotations), Don Garlits Museum of Drag Racing hall of fame notice (class of 1992), SEMA Hall of Fame (1986) · **Last verified:** 2026-11-08 (Round 35) · **Open issues:** ⚠ birth year 1926 or 1927; ⚠ how the family car came apart; ⚠ Greer-Black-Prudhomme's win-loss record; ⚠ 392 or 426 as the pattern for the KB block · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

    — Keith Black, recalled in his Motorsports Hall of Fame of America biography, 1995.[^mshf]

## The family car in the driveway

Keith Black took the family car apart in the driveway when he was about 14, built boat engines in a backyard, and that work became Keith Black Racing Engines. The aluminum block from the shop is the KB. He was born in Huntington Park, California, on June 25, 1926, by the Motorsports Hall of Fame's count, and lived and worked in the state all his life. Hemmings, writing from an interview with his son Ken, gives the birth year as 1927.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> The sources disagree on 1926 or 1927. Both accounts start the mechanical education in the same place: the family driveway.[^mshf][^hemm18]

Black told it this way: "I always liked to get involved with mechanical things. When I was about 14, I talked my mother into letting me take the old family car apart. I put rings and bearings in it, and when my dad came home, he about croaked when he saw his car all apart in the driveway, and his kid working on it." Ken remembered a different setup. His father, he said, essentially taught himself, starting the day he took his parents' car apart while they were away at church; they came home to find it completely disassembled and 14-year-old Keith sitting in the middle, figuring out why the engine needed new rings and bearings.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup>[^mshf][^hemm18] The sources disagree on how the car came apart.

He apprenticed with several nearby shops. The hall of fame names Clay Smith, Art Sparks and Cliff Collins; Ken remembered Clay Smith and Cliff Collins, and said working with Smith, "who just took in a young punk kid, taught him a lot about camshafts and timing." It made him realize, Ken said, "that a block just held all the parts—the camshaft and the timing made everything happen." He also went to work with his father in the family auto-parts business.[^mshf][^hemm18]

## "I put a driver in it"

Black raced boats in the mid-1940s. The hall of fame and the SEMA notice both say he set a world record the second time out on the Salton Sea. Hemmings adds that the powerboats he built there broke the 100 mph barrier, and that the Ford flathead V-8s he built for those boats were in such demand that they became a business.[^mshf][^sema][^hemm18]

He told the hall of fame how the business started: "I raced boats for a bit of myself, then someone said, 'Come build an engine for me,' and I built an engine (a flathead) for him. And then somebody else would say, 'Well, gee, that ran good, so build me one,' and someone else would want one, and so on. I got started in my backyard in the engine business." Ken added a story from around the same time: fellow racer Bobby Sykes saw that Keith was having a difficult time around the course, offered to drive the boat and lapped the field. Afterward people asked Keith what he had done to the boat. "He replied, 'I put a driver in it.'" That moment, Ken said, along with a fairly serious accident and a feeling of responsibility for his fledgling family, helped Keith decide to build engines instead of driving them.[^mshf][^hemm18]

By 1959 he had enough work to open Keith Black Racing Engines. By 1961, the hall of fame says, his boat-racing exploits included nearly 50 national and international records. Ken said drag racers had been ribbing him about building just boat engines, "and that he couldn't build a car engine, so he did it." The boat engines had more torque to churn through the water, Ken said, "and that torque proved useful on the drag strip."[^mshf][^hemm18]

## Greer-Black-Prudhomme, then the Hawaiian

A longtime friend, Tommy Greer, asked Keith to run a car with him. "It took more than a little convincing," the hall of fame says. The result was the Greer-Black-Prudhomme Top Fueler, with [Don Prudhomme](don-prudhomme.md) driving. How many rounds it won is a pile-up of its own. The hall of fame gives "over 250 eliminator rounds and lost fewer than 25" for the 1962 and 1963 seasons; Ken told Hemmings it won 90 percent of its matches over those same two seasons; the Don Garlits Museum says Keith guided the car "to victory in some 95% of its approximately 250 races from 1962 through 1964." On the Prudhomme page the argument is even older: a Schiefer Clutch advertisement claimed 236–7, and NHRA's later count is closer to 81–8.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> The sources disagree on the count. Prudhomme's own verdict on the engine builder was shorter: "It was unbelievable how smart this Keith Black was."[^mshf][^hemm18][^garlits92][^prudhomme]

Black, the hall of fame says, would take advice when it came from an authority and made logical sense: "if what they say makes logical sense, then why not buy?" Racers remembered long phone conversations. "We provide an ongoing service center for information," he said of the shop. "There is no limit. We've always tried to take the attitude that, if the customer does well, then we do well; so anything we can do that will enhance his capabilities to do better; ultimately we gain from."[^mshf]

After the Greer car, he worked with [Roland Leong](roland-leong.md)'s Hawaiian Top Fuel dragsters in the late 1960s. The Garlits Museum says that the subsequent season, after Greer-Black-Prudhomme, Black turned his attention to customer Leong, "whose 'Hawaiian' consequently became the first car to win NHRA's Winternationals and US Nationals in the same year." In the early 1970s he also worked on the Plymouth Barracuda Funny Car campaigned by "Big John" Mazmanian, then, the hall of fame says, "abandoned hands-on drag racing" and limited himself to being an engine manufacturer.[^mshf][^garlits92]

## Chrysler, and the KB block

In 1965 Chrysler contracted with Black to develop its marine racing program. Ken said that affiliation led his father to switch entirely from building flatheads to building the new 426 Hemis, and that he would still go to a boat race one weekend and a drag race the next, which "likely led Chrysler to his doorstep." The hall of fame says he loved the research work "because of the challenge it presented, and the fact that it made good business sense." Hemmings, in a piece posted in 2018 but written, by its own count, 15 years after his death, said the shop still operated in South Gate and that Ken ran it.[^mshf][^hemm18]

Ed Donovan introduced an aftermarket aluminum engine in 1971. The hall of fame calls it the first; Hemmings, in Ken's telling, says those first aluminum Hemi blocks were not Keith's innovation, but that Keith "mastered that market when he introduced his KB block in 1974, at the urging (and with the design assistance) of former Chrysler engineer Bob Tarozzi." Ken said the cost had held his father back: "It was a big cost. And after the Chrysler contract, Dad was used to having others pay for big programs." That engine, Ken said, dominated the fuel ranks for the next decade and later allowed aluminum engines based on Chevrolet and Oldsmobile designs in the mid-1980s.[^mshf][^hemm18]

What the block was patterned on is not quite settled. The Garlits Museum credits Keith Black Racing Engines with "perfecting the aluminum 426 hemi block." SEMA's 1986 hall-of-fame notice, written while he was still living, describes "aluminum blocks fashioned after the Chrysler 392 Hemi" and says KB blocks held every national record in Top Fuel between 1975 and 1984.<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Sources disagree: see the bottom of the page">⚠</a></sup> The sources disagree on a 392 or a 426. The Garlits Museum also credits the shop with developing a safe "slipper" clutch; the hall of fame calls Black a pioneer in clutch development in the late 1960s, "when it was discovered that huge clouds of tire smoke wasn't the fastest way down the strip."[^garlits92][^sema][^mshf]

Customers named by the hall of fame include [Don Garlits](don-garlits.md), Prudhomme, Shirley Muldowney, Kenny Bernstein, Joe Amato, John Force and Tommy Ivo. This round turned up no USPTO patent in Black's name; the innovations are described in the hall notices and in Ken's interview, not in a patent we could read.[^mshf]

Friends called him "Uncle Keith." He died of cancer in 1991; the hall of fame gives the date as May 13, 1991, and says he was survived by his wife Jane and two children. Ken most highly regarded his father's habit of taking advice and cutting through gimmicks. "Dad said he was very fortunate to have crossed paths with the right kinds of people," Ken said. "He always said he couldn't do it all himself, but he knew who to call to get the right answers."[^mshf][^hemm18]

The Greer-Black-Prudhomme years, and the Snake name that came out of that car, are told in full on [Don Prudhomme](don-prudhomme.md). The Hawaiian that Black suggested Prudhomme should drive is on [Roland Leong](roland-leong.md).

The family car had been apart in the driveway when he was about 14. The backyard boat engines became the shop. The aluminum block is the KB.
