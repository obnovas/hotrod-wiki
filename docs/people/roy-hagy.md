# Roy Hagy: The Other Prewar Top-Chopper

*Almost everything known here comes from one oral source: Tom Sparks, interviewed by Pat Ganahl for* The American Custom Car*, as relayed by Kustomrama.*[^k] ◐

- Hagy and Jimmy Summers are named as two of the first LA shops to specialize in customizing production cars, as opposed to coachbuilders like Bohman & Schwartz or Coachcraft.[^k]
- His shop was on Vermont at Second Street. When Sparks worked there (c.1942–43, at about 15), Hagy was working out of a back room at Russell Johnson's paint shop.[^k]
- Sparks: "Hagy worked mostly on what we call fat-fender cars today: '36s, '39-'40s. He did mostly filling, removing handles, recessing license plates, and chopping tops. **Hagy didn't like to use much lead; he'd metal-finish everything.** ... And he always worked alone."[^k]
- Sparks was an unpaid "sand boy" and learned gas welding and painting there.[^k]
- George and Tom Bistagne learned bodywork from Hagy around 1939–40.[^k]
- Known car: Tommy Winship's 1940 Ford coupe (later Earl Bruce's), with a chopped top, filled quarter windows, rounded door corners, and dechromed trim.[^k]
- On where chopping came from, Sparks said, "It had to start sometime in the mid- to late-1930s." He added a caveat: "We weren't in Hollywood, and we didn't go very far."[^k]

**Why it matters:** metal-finishing rather than leading puts Hagy against the grain of the "lead sled" era that followed ([Techniques](../techniques/index.md)).

[^k]: Kustomrama, "Roy Hagy" (citing Pat Ganahl, *The American Custom Car*). https://kustomrama.com/wiki/Roy_Hagy
