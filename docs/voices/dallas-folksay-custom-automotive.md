# T-Bones and Cheater Slicks: A Dallas Folklorist at the Speed Shop (Dallas, 1947–68)

!!! abstract "Article record"
    **Confidence:** Medium · **Source types:** <span class="hr-badge">Grassroots</span> A state folklore society's annual volume (Texas Folklore Society, 1968), read as page OCR on the Portal to Texas History; firsthand forum posts by Dallas hot rodders on The H.A.M.B. (2009 and 2015) · **Last verified:** 2026-10-27 (Round 24; Nye page OCR and forum quotes rechecked) · **Open issues:** when and where Custom Automotive opened ⚠; the forum's details of Adams's life rest on one or two posters ◐; the 1950 Amarillo prize is one secondhand story ◐ · [How we rate this](../reference/source-standards.md)

!!! quote "In their own words"
    "Late in 1963 when Charlie Adams was digging a pit in his speed shop, the joke in Dallas hot rod circles was to refer to the pit as 'the place where they're going to bury all them ole flatheads.'"

    — Hermes Nye, "T-Bones and Cheater Slicks: The Folksay of the Drag Strip," Texas Folklore Society, 1968.[^nye21]

Folklorists go where a group has its own words. In the 1960s one of them went to a Dallas speed shop. Hermes Nye was, in the words of a 1957 Folkways album note, "a lawyer by trade" in Dallas and "a devoted folk singer and collector."[^fw] In 1956 he had brought the "hootenanny" to the Texas Folklore Society's annual meetings, and in 1968 he served as its counsel when it incorporated.[^tfs] That same year the Society's annual volume, *Tire Shrinker to Dragster*, printed his field guide to the talk of the drag strip, with thanks to the men behind the counter at Charlie Adams's **Custom Automotive**.[^nye35]

## A tribe with its own talk (1968)

Nye opened by borrowing a definition from Roger Abrahams, a folklorist at the University of Texas: you find folklore wherever there is a group with "a hard congenial core" and shared ways. "In additional to bar associations, prison cell-blocks, cow camps, and faculty clubs," Nye wrote, "another milieu where many of the above conditions obtain is the drag strip."[^nye11]

Then he walked the reader through the hobby the way a rookie would go through it. You come into the speed shop in "perhaps all innocence for a set of mags or knock-offs," buy a carburetor adapter for five to ten dollars, and take your car out to the strip, "and promptly gets his butt beat."[^nye14] Back you come for a bore job, and you find "that the raising of his compression has been in direct proportion to the lowering of his bank balance."[^nye14] By the end the engine won't stand still at less than 2,000 or 3,000 rpm, and you need a third car to get to work.[^nye15]

The lists that follow are the heart of the piece, and they read like a dictionary written in a pit lane. Tires were skins, slicks, hides or boots, and the cheater slicks of his title had "just enough groove to satisfy the rules."[^nye15] The Model T was the **T-Bone** and the Model A, "by a curious process of logic and perhaps from lack of imagination," the A-Bone.[^nye16] A "fast windshield" was one plastered with parts makers' decals, whether or not the parts were under the hood.[^nye17] The **Johnson rod** was a "mythical auto part, blamed for any unknown fault or defect," and **midnight auto supply** was where stolen parts came from.[^nye21] A "squirrel" in a speed shop was a browser who never bought, and Nye recorded the counter's answer: "Why don't you squirrels pool your resources and buy a decal for your fast windshield?"[^nye25]

Some of the words came from far away. The strip's electronic timers were "Fontana Eyes," Nye wrote, "popularized at the Fontana strip in California"; see [Pomona's Choppers and the Fontana strip](../drag-racing/pomona-choppers-fontana.md).[^nye24] Some came from home. Entry 17 in his list of strip talk was "Green Latrine; Green Valley Dragstrip near Ft. Worth," the AHRA strip on a dairy farm whose story is told in [The Track That Milk Built](../drag-racing/green-valley-raceway-smithfield.md).[^nye25] He also listed the drivers' nicknames, from "Don the Snake Prudhomme" to "Dandy Dick Landy," and the club jackets in the stands that announced a LONE WOLF, a NIGHT PROWLER or a man who "HAS GOODIES, WILL TRAVEL."[^nye27][^nye24]

## The Dallas men he thanked

The author's note is where the Dallas scene shows through. Nye thanked "Frank X. Tolbert, motor editor of the Dallas Morning News"; "Young David Hendricks of Denton, who graciously let me go through his old files of magazines"; Ralph Guldahl, Jr., "of the Lions Drag Strip," who answered his question about the quarter mile; and "Charlie Adams, Bobby Wolcott and Charles Greenhill, all of the Custom Automotive speed shop in Dallas."[^nye34][^nye35]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-1"><a href="#q-1" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>

Adams turns up in the text too. "Around 1950," Nye wrote, Adams drove his chopped and channeled deuce, "shiny with twenty-six coats of rubbed lacquer," under its own power from Dallas to Amarillo, and at the strip there won "a fifty dollar war bond which had set the thrifty track owner back a mere thirty-seven fifty."[^nye15]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-2"><a href="#q-2" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup> Nye set that beside the purses of the mid-1960s, which could total $50,000 at a big strip.[^nye15] For the Panhandle strip of the 1960s, see [Canyon's clubs and the Amarillo strip](../drag-racing/canyon-orchids-celtics-amarillo.md).

## The shop, as its old hands remember it

Nye never described Custom Automotive's building. Men who knew it did, decades later, on The H.A.M.B., the Jalopy Journal's forum. Karl Knecht, who posts as KKrod (he signed the thread's opening post with his name)[^hamb1], wrote in 2015 that Adams "returned to Dallas from southern California and opened up Custom Automotive, Inc in 1948," on Pearl Street; that he had served in the Army Air Corps from 1941 to 1947; and that he "was close friends with Ak Miller and knew most of the early speed shop pioneers." He added that "Custom Automotive was open for five decades," and posted a photo of Adams and his highboy roadster "taken at the first ever drag races at Caddo Mills airport in 1951" (see [Texas: Three Stopwatches at Caddo Mills](../places/texas.md)).[^hamb52] In a second post he added that Adams had grown up in lower Oak Lawn, met Vic Edelbrock in California after the war, and "Had his first shop on Fitzhugh in 1947. This was a Gulf station at Fitzhugh and Cole."[^hamb53]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-3"><a href="#q-3" title="Sources disagree: see Open questions">⚠</a></sup>

Bobby Wolcott, who posts as wbrw32, is almost certainly the Wolcott of Nye's thank-you list (the identification is ours). Knecht's post names him: "Bobby Wolcott (WBRW32) worked for Custom Automitive in the 50's."[^hamb52] In 2009 Wolcott gave the address as "2122 N Pearl st......Between McKinney ave and Ross Ave," and another Dallas member replied that it was now the address of the Federal Reserve building.[^shops] Of the shop pictured in his forum avatar he wrote: "another speed shop in Dallas since late 40's I was there for 11 yrs." He does not name it in that post.[^shops] In 2015 he added that Adams had flown B-17s and counted among his California friends Roy Richter, Fred Offenhauser and "Vic Sr.," and that Adams's '32 had run at Bonneville as the "Liqua-Moly Special" ("spellimg?," he wrote) before Mitch Rosansky bought it and raced it at Caddo Mills with Bob James driving.[^hamb38][^hamb55]<sup class="hr-q" id="qr-4"><a href="#q-4" title="Single source so far: see Open questions">◐</a></sup>

The shop outlived the flathead jokes. One member wrote that he worked there in 1976–77, "when they were on crown and emerald," and Wolcott added that it was later bought by Butch Lehman, "from the east Coast."[^shops]

Custom Automotive was not the only counter in town. The same 2009 thread remembered Hot Rod Exchange "on Beckley in west Dallas," whose owner Ben Benson was, in Wolcott's words, "the best wheeler dealer on used parts in all of Dallas."[^shops] Mike Britton wrote that he and his wife's "first date was a trip to Hot Rod Exchange in Feb. of '65. I was trading 331 hemi parts for Y block parts. She can't ever say she didn't know what she was getting into!"[^shops]

Nye ended his essay worrying about where it was all going, now that a magazine was describing the 1966 Charger as "Dodge's New Street Hemi": "will all this in turn engender an even more fascinating, weird, and arcane folklore?"[^nye34] He closed his thanks with the strip's own words: "These thanks are as much of a trophy as I could manage; I'm sorry that they all had to run for tin."[^nye35]

## Open questions & disputed details

*From the detective's notebook: where the sources disagree, where we have only one, and what we are still looking for. Each item links back to its place in the story.*

<a id="q-1" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **Why a quarter mile?** Nye's helper at Lions answered: "No, it was not based on the quarter-horse race distance, but was fixed by C. J. Hart and the racers at Santa Ana Airport, where there was limit[e]d shut-off space."[^nye35] Hart himself, quoted in his Motorsports Hall of Fame biography, tied it to horse racing instead: "There was a lot of talk in those days that quarter horses were faster than cars." Neither telling is dated to the 1950s; the dispute is logged on [our Santa Ana page](../eras/1950s-santa-ana-drags.md#how-the-rules-got-invented). <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-1" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-2" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **Did Adams win an Amarillo war bond around 1950?** Only Nye tells it, dated "around 1950," and he does not say who told him. We have not found the meet in an Amarillo paper.[^nye15] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-2" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-3" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Disputed.** **When and where did Custom Automotive open?** The same poster gives two starts: Custom Automotive, Inc., opened "in 1948" on Pearl Street, and a "first shop on Fitzhugh in 1947," a Gulf station, before the move to Pearl.[^hamb52][^hamb53] (Wolcott's "since late 40's" in 2009 was about "another speed shop in Dallas" that he did not name, so it does not help here.)[^shops] A Dallas city directory of 1947–49 would settle it. <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-3" title="Back to the story">↩</a>

<a id="q-4" class="hr-q-anchor"></a>**Single source so far.** **How did Adams serve in the war, and what was his Bonneville car called?** Knecht writes Army Air Corps, 1941–47; Wolcott writes that Adams was "a captian in the Air Force, flying the B-17s." The Bonneville name comes only from Wolcott, who was unsure of its spelling. We have no service record or Bonneville entry list.[^hamb52][^hamb55][^hamb38] <a class="hr-q-back" href="#qr-4" title="Back to the story">↩</a>
